\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture we finished the computation of the QR factorization.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{How to use the factors.} With Householder QR, $Q$ is never formed. To apply $Q^\ast$ to a vector we run through the stored reflectors $\vec{v}_1, \dots, \vec{v}_n$ forward, and to apply $Q$ we run through them backward.
  \item \emph{Givens rotations.} A rotation in the $(i, j)$ plane with $c = A_{ii}/\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}$ and $s = A_{ji}/\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}$ zeros out one entry at a time. This gives Givens QR.
  \item \emph{Full and reduced QR}, and the \emph{existence and uniqueness} of QR.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Solving Overdetermined Least Squares}

We now return to the problem that motivated QR in the first place: given $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ with $m > n$ and $\vec{b} \in \K^m$, find $\vec{x}$ minimizing
\begin{equation}
  \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2.
\end{equation}
There are more equations than unknowns, so $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ generally has no solution, and we cannot simply solve it as in the square case.

We have already seen an ``intuitive'' explanation of why $\vec{x} = \hat{R}^{-1}\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$ solves this problem for a reduced QR factorization $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$. Let us recall it. Extend $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_n$ to an orthonormal basis $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_m$ of $\K^m$. In this basis,
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\vec{b}$ has the coordinates $Q^\ast\vec{b}$;
  \item $A\vec{x} = \hat{Q}(\hat{R}\vec{x})$ has the coordinates $\hat{R}\vec{x}$ in the first $n$ directions and $0$ in the remaining $m - n$.
\end{itemize}
Since $\hat{R}$ is invertible, we can match the first $n$ coordinates of $\vec{b}$ exactly by solving $\hat{R}\vec{x} = \hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$, while nothing we do can affect the remaining ones. For $m = 3$ and $n = 2$, this gives the following picture.

This is convincing, but it leans on the picture. Why is matching coordinates optimal, and is it the only optimal choice? \emph{How do we make this rigorous?} One route uses that $Q^\ast$ preserves norms (\Cref{sec:unitary}). This lecture takes a different route that needs no factorization at all.

Before using any factorization, we answer a more basic question: \emph{what condition does a minimizer satisfy?} Such a condition is useful in two ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It lets us \emph{verify} that the $\vec{x}$ produced by QR really is optimal.
  \item It turns a minimization problem into a \emph{system of equations}.
\end{itemize}
The main line of this lecture is
\begin{equation}
  \vec{x} \text{ minimizes } \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 \quad\iff\quad A\vec{x} - \vec{b} \perp \range(A) \quad\iff\quad A^\ast A\vec{x} = A^\ast\vec{b}.
\end{equation}
In \Cref{ch:ls} we saw this geometrically; here we prove it. We then study the coefficient matrix $A^\ast A$ of the last system, which leads to Gram matrices and positive semidefinite matrices.

\section{Conventions}

As throughout, $\K$ is $\R$ or $\C$. Working over $\K$ lets one derivation cover both cases, at the price of writing it with the complex rules: conjugate transposes $A^\ast$, real parts $\operatorname{Re}$, and a separate argument for the imaginary part. For real problems, read $A^\ast$ as $A^\T$ and drop $\operatorname{Re}$.

We use the inner product $\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \vec{x}^\ast\vec{y}$, as in \Cref{ch:ls,ch:qr}. It is linear in the second argument, conjugate linear in the first, and satisfies $\ip{\vec{y}}{\vec{x}} = \overline{\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}}$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} + \ip{\vec{y}}{\vec{x}} = 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}}.
\end{equation}
The residual is $\vec{r} \defeq A\vec{x} - \vec{b}$. Perturbation directions are denoted by $\vec{d}$.

One identity is used again and again in this lecture.

\begin{lemma}[Moving a Matrix Across the Inner Product]
  \label{lem:adjoint}
  For any $M \in \K^{m \times n}$ (not necessarily square or unitary), $\vec{u} \in \K^n$, and $\vec{v} \in \K^m$,
  \begin{equation}
    \ip{M\vec{u}}{\vec{v}} = \ip{\vec{u}}{M^\ast\vec{v}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  This is just associativity: $\ip{M\vec{u}}{\vec{v}} = (M\vec{u})^\ast\vec{v} = \vec{u}^\ast(M^\ast\vec{v}) = \ip{\vec{u}}{M^\ast\vec{v}}$.
\end{proof}

In words, a matrix can be moved from one side of the inner product to the other, and it becomes $M^\ast$ on the way. Two remarks.
\begin{itemize}
  \item This is \emph{not} the same as multiplying both sides by $M^\ast$. For the $m \times n$ factor $\hat{Q}$ of a reduced QR factorization, we have $\hat{Q}^\ast\hat{Q} = I$ but $\hat{Q}\hat{Q}^\ast \ne I$, and in general $\ip{\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{y}}{\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{z}} \ne \ip{\vec{y}}{\vec{z}}$ (compare \Cref{sec:unitary}).
  \item In Axler's \emph{Linear Algebra Done Right} (Section 7.A), \Cref{lem:adjoint} is the definition of the adjoint.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Optimality Condition}

We want to find $\vec{x}$ that minimizes $\norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2$. The idea is simple: a minimizer cannot be improved by moving it. If $\vec{x}$ minimizes the objective, then for every $\vec{\varepsilon} \in \K^n$,
\begin{equation}
  \norm{A(\vec{x} + \vec{\varepsilon}) - \vec{b}}^2 \ge \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2.
\end{equation}
So we first compute exactly how the objective changes under a perturbation.

\begin{lemma}[Change of the Objective Under a Perturbation]
  \label{lem:ls-expand}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ and $\vec{b} \in \K^m$. For all $\vec{x}, \vec{\varepsilon} \in \K^n$, with $\vec{r} = A\vec{x} - \vec{b}$,
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ls-expand}
    \norm{A(\vec{x} + \vec{\varepsilon}) - \vec{b}}^2 - \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 = \norm{A\vec{\varepsilon}}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{\vec{r}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Since $A(\vec{x} + \vec{\varepsilon}) - \vec{b} = A\vec{\varepsilon} + \vec{r}$, we have
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
      \norm{A\vec{\varepsilon} + \vec{r}}^2 - \norm{\vec{r}}^2
      &= \ip{A\vec{\varepsilon} + \vec{r}}{A\vec{\varepsilon} + \vec{r}} - \ip{\vec{r}}{\vec{r}} \\
      &= \ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{A\vec{\varepsilon}} + \ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{\vec{r}} + \underbrace{\ip{\vec{r}}{A\vec{\varepsilon}}}_{=\,\overline{\ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{\vec{r}}}} \\
      &= \norm{A\vec{\varepsilon}}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{\vec{r}},
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  where the last step uses $z + \bar{z} = 2\operatorname{Re}z$.
\end{proof}

The lemma assumes nothing about $\vec{x}$. Let us look at its right-hand side.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The quadratic term $\norm{A\vec{\varepsilon}}^2$ is always $\ge 0$, so it can never make the objective decrease.
  \item Only the cross term $2\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{\varepsilon}}{\vec{r}}$ can be negative.
\end{itemize}
This suggests that $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer exactly when the cross term vanishes for every $\vec{\varepsilon}$. That is the content of the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Optimality Condition for Least Squares]
  \label{thm:ls-optimality}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times n}$, $\vec{b} \in \K^m$, and $\vec{x} \in \K^n$. The following two statements are equivalent:
  \begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em, labelwidth=1.6em, font=\normalfont\bfseries]
    \item[(P)] $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer, i.e., $\norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 \le \norm{A\vec{y} - \vec{b}}^2$ for all $\vec{y} \in \K^n$.
    \item[(Q)] $\ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}} = 0$ for all $\vec{d} \in \K^n$, i.e., $A\vec{x} - \vec{b} \perp \range(A)$.
  \end{description}
  Moreover, if $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer, then the set of all minimizers is $\vec{x} + \nullsp(A)$. In particular, the minimizer is unique if and only if $A$ has full column rank.
\end{theorem}

The plan of the proof: for (P) $\Rightarrow$ (Q), perturb $\vec{x}$ by a small step $\delta\vec{d}$ and let $\delta \to 0$, so that only the cross term survives; for (Q) $\Rightarrow$ (P), plug (Q) into \Cref{lem:ls-expand} to kill the cross term.

\begin{proof}
  Write $\vec{r} = A\vec{x} - \vec{b}$.

  \emph{(P) $\Rightarrow$ (Q).} First suppose that $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer. Fix any $\vec{d} \in \K^n$ and any $\delta > 0$. Applying (P) with $\vec{y} = \vec{x} + \delta\vec{d}$, and then \Cref{lem:ls-expand} with $\vec{\varepsilon} = \delta\vec{d}$, we get
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
      0 &\le \norm{A(\vec{x} + \delta\vec{d}) - \vec{b}}^2 - \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 \\
      &= \delta^2\norm{A\vec{d}}^2 + 2\delta\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} \\
      \implies 0 &\le \delta\norm{A\vec{d}}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}},
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  where we divided by $\delta > 0$; since $\delta$ is real, it comes out of the inner product without conjugation. This holds for every $\delta > 0$. Taking the limit $\delta \to 0^+$ on both sides, we get
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ls-re-ge}
    \operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} \ge 0 \qquad \text{for all } \vec{d} \in \K^n.
  \end{equation}
  We now upgrade this inequality to an equality in two steps.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{The real part.} Since \eqref{eq:ls-re-ge} holds for all $\vec{d}$, it also holds for $-\vec{d}$, which gives $-\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} \ge 0$. Thus $\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} = 0$ for all $\vec{d} \in \K^n$.
    \item \emph{The imaginary part.} If $\K = \C$, we apply this to $i\vec{d}$. Since the first argument of the inner product is conjugated,
    \begin{equation}
      0 = \operatorname{Re}\ip{iA\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} = \operatorname{Re}\left(-i\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}}\right) = \operatorname{Im}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}}.
    \end{equation}
  \end{itemize}
  So both the real and the imaginary part of $\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}}$ vanish, and $\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}} = 0$ for all $\vec{d} \in \K^n$, as desired.

  \emph{(Q) $\Rightarrow$ (P).} Now suppose that (Q) holds, and fix any $\vec{y} \in \K^n$. Applying \Cref{lem:ls-expand} with $\vec{\varepsilon} = \vec{y} - \vec{x}$, and then (Q) with $\vec{d} = \vec{y} - \vec{x}$, we get
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ls-pythagoras}
    \begin{aligned}
      \norm{A\vec{y} - \vec{b}}^2
      &= \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 + \norm{A(\vec{y} - \vec{x})}^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\underbrace{\ip{A(\vec{y} - \vec{x})}{\vec{r}}}_{=\,0 \text{ by (Q)}} \\
      &= \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 + \underbrace{\norm{A(\vec{y} - \vec{x})}^2}_{\ge\,0} \\
      &\ge \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2.
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  Since $\vec{y}$ was arbitrary, $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer.

  \emph{The set of minimizers.} Finally, let $\vec{x}$ be a minimizer. By the first part, $\vec{x}$ satisfies (Q), so the second line of \eqref{eq:ls-pythagoras} holds for every $\vec{y}$. Hence $\vec{y}$ is also a minimizer if and only if $\norm{A(\vec{y} - \vec{x})}^2 = 0$, i.e., $\vec{y} - \vec{x} \in \nullsp(A)$. This set is the single point $\vec{x}$ if and only if $\nullsp(A) = \{\zerovec\}$, i.e., $A$ has full column rank.
\end{proof}

Thus, if $\vec{x}$ minimizes the squared residual, then $\ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x}} = \ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{b}}$ for all $\vec{d}$. Let us reflect on the two directions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Necessity}, (P) $\Rightarrow$ (Q): orthogonality is \emph{necessary} for optimality. Every minimizer satisfies it, so solving (Q) cannot miss a minimizer.
  \item \emph{Sufficiency}, (Q) $\Rightarrow$ (P): orthogonality is \emph{sufficient}. To certify that a given $\vec{x}$ is a minimizer, it suffices to check (Q). This is how we will verify the QR solution in \Cref{sec:verify-qr}.
\end{itemize}

The two halves of the proof also have different characters.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Necessity only compares $\vec{x}$ with nearby points $\vec{x} + \delta\vec{d}$. For small $\delta$, the quadratic term $\delta^2\norm{A\vec{d}}^2$ is negligible, so the sign of the change is decided by the directional derivative $2\operatorname{Re}\ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{r}}$, and at a minimizer no directional derivative can be negative.
  \item Sufficiency compares $\vec{x}$ with an arbitrary $\vec{y}$ in one step, so the minimizer it certifies is global. Geometrically, \eqref{eq:ls-pythagoras} is the Pythagorean theorem, as in the following picture.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Geometric Meaning}

The vectors $A\vec{d}$ for $\vec{d} \in \K^n$ are exactly the elements of $\range(A)$. So the optimality condition says that the residual is orthogonal to every vector in the column space, $A\vec{x} - \vec{b} \perp \range(A)$. Geometrically: drop a perpendicular from $\vec{b}$ to $\range(A)$; its foot is $A\vec{x}$, the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $\range(A)$. This is the picture of \Cref{ch:ls}, now with a proof.

\section{What the Optimality Condition Says}

What do the optimality conditions mean in concrete cases? The key point is that (Q) tests the residual only against vectors of the form $A\vec{d}$. How much this tells us depends on how many vectors have that form.

\subsection{Invertible Square Matrices: Solving a Linear System}

Let $m = n$ and $A$ be invertible (full rank), and let $\vec{x}$ be a minimizer. By necessity, it satisfies the orthogonality condition. The condition holds for \emph{every} $\vec{d}$, so we may pick a special direction: $\vec{d} = A^{-1}(A\vec{x} - \vec{b})$, which has $A\vec{d} = A\vec{x} - \vec{b}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  0 = \ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}} = \ip{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}} = \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2,
\end{equation}
so $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$. In words: for invertible square matrices, least squares is just solving the linear system, and the minimal residual is zero.

\subsection{Singular or Tall Matrices: Only Some Directions Are Tested}

If $A$ is singular or $m > n$, not every $\vec{y} \in \K^m$ can be written as $A\vec{d}$. Then $\range(A)$ is a proper subspace, and the optimality condition only checks some directions (some equations). The residual generally does not lie in $\range(A)$, so we cannot conclude $\vec{r} = \zerovec$, only $\vec{r} \perp \range(A)$.

\subsection{Verifying the QR Solution}
\label{sec:verify-qr}

We can now make the intuitive QR argument rigorous. Let $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$ be a reduced QR factorization with $\hat{Q}^\ast\hat{Q} = I$ and $\hat{R}$ upper triangular and invertible ($A$ of full column rank). Take $\vec{x} = \hat{R}^{-1}\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$. We check (Q), moving $\hat{Q}$ to the right with \Cref{lem:adjoint}:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}} &= \ip{\hat{Q}\hat{R}\vec{d}}{\hat{Q}\hat{R}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b} - \vec{b}} \\
    &= \ip{\hat{R}\vec{d}}{\hat{Q}^\ast\hat{Q}\hat{R}\hat{R}^{-1}\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b} - \hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}} \\
    &= \ip{\hat{R}\vec{d}}{\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b} - \hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}} = 0.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Note that $\hat{Q}^\ast$ acts on \emph{every} term in the second argument, so $\vec{b}$ becomes $\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$ as well.

Hence $\vec{x} = \hat{R}^{-1}\hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$ satisfies (Q), and by sufficiency it is a least squares solution; since $A$ has full column rank, it is the only one. In practice we never form $\hat{R}^{-1}$; we solve the upper triangular system $\hat{R}\vec{x} = \hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$ by back substitution.

\section{The Normal Equations}
\label{sec:normal-eq}

The optimality condition is a statement ``for all $\vec{d}$''. We now turn it into a single linear system. The trick is to use the transpose: apply \Cref{lem:adjoint} to both sides, moving $A$ to the right.
\begin{equation}
  \ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x}} = \ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{b}} \ \ \forall\vec{d} \in \K^n
  \quad\iff\quad
  \ip{\vec{d}}{A^\ast A\vec{x}} = \ip{\vec{d}}{A^\ast\vec{b}} \ \ \forall\vec{d} \in \K^n
  \quad\iff\quad
  A^\ast A\vec{x} = A^\ast\vec{b}.
\end{equation}
Only the last step needs an argument. Let $\vec{w} = A^\ast A\vec{x} - A^\ast\vec{b}$. The middle condition says that $\vec{w}$ is orthogonal to every vector; taking $\vec{d} = \vec{w}$ gives $\norm{\vec{w}}^2 = 0$, so $\vec{w} = \zerovec$. The other direction is clear.

So (Q) is equivalent to the normal equations. Combining this with (P) $\iff$ (Q) from \Cref{thm:ls-optimality} gives the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[The Normal Equations]
  \label{thm:normal}
  $\vec{x}$ minimizes $\norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2$ if and only if it solves the \emph{normal equations}
  \begin{equation}
    A^\ast A\vec{x} = A^\ast\vec{b}.
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

We have turned our system into a square one! The overdetermined $m \times n$ system $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ has become an $n \times n$ system. The name ``normal'' means perpendicular: the equation $A^\ast(A\vec{x} - \vec{b}) = \zerovec$ says exactly that the residual is perpendicular to the column space, as in \eqref{eq:normal}.

The normal equations have clear advantages and one serious drawback.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Pro:} the system is square, and its matrix $A^\ast A$ is Hermitian (symmetric in the real case); in fact it is positive semidefinite (\Cref{thm:gram}). Such systems can be solved with the Cholesky factorization of \Cref{ch:cholesky} at half the cost of LU.
  \item \emph{Con:} the condition number is squared, $\kappa_2(A^\ast A) = \kappa_2(A)^2$, so forming $A^\ast A$ loses precision when $A$ is ill-conditioned (see the supplement at the end of this lecture).
\end{itemize}

\section{Gram Matrices and Positive Semidefinite Matrices}

Solving the normal equations means solving a system whose matrix has the form $B^\ast B$. (We switch to the letter $B$ so that $A$ is free for the coefficient matrix.) So it pays to study the structure of such matrices. We begin with the larger class they belong to.

\subsection{Hermitian and Anti-Hermitian Matrices}

\begin{definition}[Hermitian and Anti-Hermitian Matrices]
  A matrix $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ is \emph{Hermitian} if $A^\ast = A$ and \emph{anti-Hermitian} (or skew-Hermitian) if $A^\ast = -A$. For $\K = \R$, these are called \emph{symmetric} and \emph{antisymmetric} (or skew-symmetric).
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Eigenvalues of (Anti-)Hermitian Matrices]
  \label{prop:herm-eig}
  All eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are real, and all eigenvalues of an anti-Hermitian matrix are purely imaginary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Let $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$ with $\vec{v} \ne \zerovec$, so that $\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v} = \lambda\norm{\vec{v}}^2$. Taking the complex conjugate of the scalar $\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v}$,
  \begin{equation}
    \overline{\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v}} = (\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v})^\ast = \vec{v}^\ast A^\ast\vec{v} = \pm\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v},
  \end{equation}
  with $+$ for Hermitian and $-$ for anti-Hermitian $A$. So $\vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v}$ is real in the first case and purely imaginary in the second, and so is $\lambda = \vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v}/\norm{\vec{v}}^2$.
\end{proof}

These matrices behave a lot like complex numbers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Every square matrix splits uniquely into a Hermitian and an anti-Hermitian part, $A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^\ast) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^\ast)$, much like a complex number splits into real and imaginary parts.
  \item A second analogy, with $z = e^{i\theta}r$, $r \ge 0$, is the polar decomposition below. It uses the following notion, which we study in detail in the next subsection: a Hermitian matrix $S$ is \emph{positive semidefinite} if $\vec{v}^\ast S\vec{v} \ge 0$ for all $\vec{v}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{theorem}[Polar Decomposition]
  \label{thm:polar}
  For every $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ there exist a unitary $U$ and a Hermitian positive semidefinite $S$ such that
  \begin{equation}
    A = US.
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Take an SVD $A = W\Sigma V^\ast$ and insert $V^\ast V = I$:
  \begin{equation}
    A = W\Sigma V^\ast = \underbrace{(WV^\ast)}_{U}\underbrace{(V\Sigma V^\ast)}_{S}.
  \end{equation}
  We check the two factors.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $U = WV^\ast$ is a product of unitary matrices, hence unitary.
    \item $S = V\Sigma V^\ast$ is Hermitian since $\Sigma$ is real diagonal, and $\vec{v}^\ast S\vec{v} = \sum_i\sigma_i\abs{(V^\ast\vec{v})_i}^2 \ge 0$. \qedhere
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

Geometrically, $S$ stretches space along the orthogonal directions given by the columns of $V$, and $U$ then rotates (or reflects) the result. The factor $S$ is always determined by $A$: since $A^\ast A = SU^\ast US = S^2$, $S$ is the positive semidefinite square root of $A^\ast A$. If $A$ is invertible, so is $S$, and $U = AS^{-1}$ is determined as well.

\subsection{Gram Matrices}

A matrix of the form $B^\ast B$ is called a \emph{Gram matrix} (or inner product matrix), because its entries are the inner products of the columns $B_{:,1}, \dots, B_{:,n}$ of $B$ with each other. Using $B\vec{e}_j = B_{:,j}$,
\begin{equation}
  (B^\ast B)_{ij} = \vec{e}_i^\ast B^\ast B\vec{e}_j = \ip{B\vec{e}_i}{B\vec{e}_j} = \ip{B_{:,i}}{B_{:,j}}.
\end{equation}
Two consequences are immediate.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The diagonal entries $(B^\ast B)_{ii} = \norm{B_{:,i}}^2$ are nonnegative.
  \item $B^\ast B = I$ exactly when the columns of $B$ are orthonormal.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Positive (Semi)definite Matrices]
  A Hermitian matrix $A \in \K^{n \times n}$ is
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{positive semidefinite} if $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x} \ge 0$ for all $\vec{x} \in \K^n$;
    \item \emph{positive definite} if $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x} > 0$ for all $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The main result of this section says that these two notions describe the same matrices.

\begin{theorem}[Gram Matrices Are Exactly the Positive Semidefinite Matrices]
  \label{thm:gram}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $A \in \K^{n \times n}$ is Hermitian positive semidefinite if and only if it is a Gram matrix, that is, $A = B^\ast B$ for some matrix $B$.
    \item For $B \in \K^{m \times n}$, the Gram matrix $B^\ast B$ is positive definite if and only if $B$ has full column rank.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Item 1 does not hold for all Hermitian matrices. For example, $\diag(1, -1)$ is symmetric but not a Gram matrix: its second diagonal entry would have to be $\norm{B_{:,2}}^2 \ge 0$. Note that the rank condition in item 2 is on $B$, not on the Gram matrix $A = B^\ast B$, which is square.

\begin{proof}[Proof of item 1]
  \emph{Gram matrices are Hermitian positive semidefinite.} We have
  \begin{equation}
    (B^\ast B)^\ast = B^\ast(B^\ast)^\ast = B^\ast B, \qquad \vec{x}^\ast B^\ast B\vec{x} = \norm{B\vec{x}}^2 \ge 0.
  \end{equation}

  \emph{Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices are Gram matrices.} We take a square root of $A$ in three steps.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Diagonalize.} By the spectral theorem, a Hermitian matrix can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix: $A = U\Lambda U^\ast$, where $U$ is unitary and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues of $A$ on the diagonal.
    \item \emph{The eigenvalues are nonnegative.} They are real by \Cref{prop:herm-eig}. If $A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}$ with $\vec{v} \ne \zerovec$, then $\lambda\norm{\vec{v}}^2 = \vec{v}^\ast A\vec{v} \ge 0$, so $\lambda \ge 0$. (If $A$ is positive definite, the same argument gives $\lambda > 0$.)
    \item \emph{Take square roots.} So we can define $\sqrt{\Lambda} = \diag(\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \dots, \sqrt{\lambda_n})$. Inserting $U^\ast U = I$ in the middle,
    \begin{equation}
      A = U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast = \left(U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast\right)^\ast\left(U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast\right),
    \end{equation}
    where the last step uses that $\sqrt{\Lambda}$ is a real diagonal matrix and hence equal to its conjugate transpose.
  \end{enumerate}
  Taking $B = U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast$ gives $A = B^\ast B$.
\end{proof}

The matrix $B = U\sqrt{\Lambda}\,U^\ast$ is the \emph{matrix square root} $A^{1/2}$. It is not the only choice: for any unitary $V$, $(VB)^\ast(VB) = B^\ast B$. In particular, taking a QR factorization $B = QR$ (\Cref{thm:qr-exists}) gives $A = R^\ast Q^\ast QR = R^\ast R$ with $R$ upper triangular. This is the \emph{Cholesky factorization} of $A$, the subject of the next lecture.

\begin{proof}[Proof of item 2]
  We prove the contrapositive by a chain of equivalences:
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
      B \text{ is column rank deficient}
      &\iff \exists\,\vec{x} \in \K^n \setminus \{\zerovec\} : B\vec{x} = \zerovec \\
      &\iff \exists\,\vec{x} \in \K^n \setminus \{\zerovec\} : \norm{B\vec{x}}^2 = 0 \\
      &\iff \exists\,\vec{x} \in \K^n \setminus \{\zerovec\} : \vec{x}^\ast B^\ast B\vec{x} = 0.
    \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  By item 1, $B^\ast B$ is positive semidefinite, so $\vec{x}^\ast B^\ast B\vec{x} \ge 0$ always holds. Hence $B^\ast B$ fails to be positive definite exactly when $\vec{x}^\ast B^\ast B\vec{x} = 0$ for some $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$. So $B$ is column rank deficient if and only if $B^\ast B$ is not positive definite; negating both sides gives item 2.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Consequences for Least Squares}

Putting \Cref{thm:normal,thm:gram} together,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    A \text{ has full column rank} &\iff A^\ast A \text{ is positive definite} \\
    &\iff \text{the normal equations have the unique solution } \vec{x} = (A^\ast A)^{-1}A^\ast\vec{b},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
consistent with the uniqueness statement of \Cref{thm:ls-optimality}. A positive definite system can be solved with the Cholesky factorization $A^\ast A = R^\ast R$, which costs about $\frac{1}{3}n^3$ flops, half of LU, and needs no pivoting.

\paragraph{Relation to the projection argument.} Introductory linear algebra texts (for example Lay's) derive the normal equations differently. Let $\hat{\vec{b}}$ be the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $\range(A)$. By the properties of the projection, $\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}} \perp \range(A)$, that is, $\vec{b} - A\hat{\vec{x}} \in \nullsp(A^\ast)$, which rearranges to the normal equations. Here $\hat{\vec{b}} \in \K^m$ is the projected point, while $\hat{\vec{x}} \in \K^n$ holds the coefficients that combine the columns of $A$ into it, $\hat{\vec{b}} = A\hat{\vec{x}}$. The point $\hat{\vec{b}}$ is always unique; $\hat{\vec{x}}$ is unique if and only if $A$ has full column rank.

This derivation is shorter, but it relies on the \emph{best approximation theorem}: if $W$ is a subspace and $\hat{\vec{b}}$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{b}$ onto $W$, then $\norm{\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}}} < \norm{\vec{b} - \vec{w}}$ for all $\vec{w} \in W$ with $\vec{w} \ne \hat{\vec{b}}$. Its proof takes two steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose an orthonormal basis $\vec{u}_1, \dots, \vec{u}_k$ of $W$ and set $\hat{\vec{b}} = \sum_i\ip{\vec{u}_i}{\vec{b}}\vec{u}_i$. A direct computation gives $\ip{\vec{u}_j}{\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}}} = 0$, so $\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}} \perp W$.
  \item Split $\vec{b} - \vec{w} = (\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}}) + (\hat{\vec{b}} - \vec{w})$, where the first part is orthogonal to $W$ and the second lies in $W$. By the Pythagorean theorem,
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{b} - \vec{w}}^2 = \norm{\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}}}^2 + \norm{\hat{\vec{b}} - \vec{w}}^2 > \norm{\vec{b} - \hat{\vec{b}}}^2.
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
So the best approximation theorem is ``orthogonal $\Rightarrow$ closest'', our sufficiency, while our perturbation argument is ``closest $\Rightarrow$ orthogonal'', our necessity. Our proof is self-contained: it needs no basis, no Gram--Schmidt, and no existence result for projections.

\paragraph{Why the perturbation argument.} Its structure, ``at a minimizer, the first-order change in every direction is nonnegative'', generalizes to settings that the projection argument does not reach:
\begin{itemize}
  \item first-order conditions for general differentiable functions (the gradient vanishes);
  \item constrained problems, where $\vec{d}$ can no longer be replaced by $-\vec{d}$, so the conclusion stays an inequality; this leads to variational inequalities and the KKT conditions;
  \item infinite-dimensional problems, such as the Galerkin orthogonality of finite elements, ``$\ip{v}{\text{residual}} = 0$ for every test function $v$'', which has the same structure as ``for all $\vec{d}$'' here.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A numerical warning about the normal equations.} The normal equations are elegant, but by \Cref{thm:twonorm} the singular values of $A^\ast A$ are the squares of those of $A$, so
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_2(A^\ast A) = \kappa_2(A)^2.
\end{equation}
Forming and solving $A^\ast A\vec{x} = A^\ast\vec{b}$ therefore loses about twice as many digits as necessary when $A$ is ill-conditioned. In terms of cost, forming $A^\ast A$ takes about $mn^2$ flops and Cholesky another $\frac{1}{3}n^3$, roughly half of Householder QR when $m \gg n$. But the speed is worth it only when $A$ is well-conditioned. This is why in practice one solves $\hat{R}\vec{x} = \hat{Q}^\ast\vec{b}$ with a QR factorization instead~\cite[Lecture 11]{TB}.
