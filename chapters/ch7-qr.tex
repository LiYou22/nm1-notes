\section{Picking Up from Householder QR}

At the end of Householder QR (\Cref{alg:householder}), the upper triangle of $A$ holds $R$ and the strictly lower triangle holds the Householder vectors $\vec{v}_k$; $Q$ is never computed explicitly. This raises two questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Applications need $Q^\ast\vec{b}$ (for least squares) or $Q\vec{x}$. How do we compute them from the $\vec{v}_k$ alone?
  \item Every Householder step processes a whole column. If $A$ is already close to upper triangular and only a few entries need to be eliminated, is this wasteful?
\end{itemize}
The first leads to the implicit use of $Q$, the second to Givens rotations. Finally we return to theory: does a QR factorization always exist, and is it unique?

\section{Using $Q$}

\paragraph{Implicit storage.} Householder QR does not store $Q$ as a matrix, only the vectors $\vec{v}_k$ defining the reflectors
\begin{equation}
  Q_k = I - 2\frac{\vec{v}_k\vec{v}_k^\ast}{\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{v}_k}.
\end{equation}
Here $\vec{v}_k$ is regarded as a vector of length $m$ whose first $k - 1$ entries are zero, so $Q_k$ acts only on rows $k$ through $m$; this is the same as the block form $Q_k = \diag(I_{k-1}, F_k)$ of \Cref{ch:householder}. From $Q_n \cdots Q_1A = R$, and since each $Q_k$ is Hermitian and its own inverse ($Q_k^\ast = Q_k = Q_k^{-1}$),
\begin{equation}
  Q = (Q_n \cdots Q_1)^{-1} = Q_1Q_2 \cdots Q_n, \qquad Q^\ast = Q_n \cdots Q_1.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Two algorithms.} A product of matrices acts on a vector from right to left. In $Q^\ast\vec{b} = Q_n \cdots Q_1\vec{b}$, the factor $Q_1$ acts first, so the loop runs $k = 1, \dots, n$. In $Q\vec{x} = Q_1 \cdots Q_n\vec{x}$, the factor $Q_n$ acts first, so the loop runs $k = n, \dots, 1$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Implicit computation of $Q^\ast\vec{b}$.}
  \label{alg:qtb}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, n$}
      \State $\vec{b}[k{:}m] \gets \vec{b}[k{:}m] - 2\vec{v}_k\left(\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{b}[k{:}m]\right)/(\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{v}_k)$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Implicit computation of $Q\vec{x}$.}
  \label{alg:qx}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$k = n, n - 1, \dots, 1$}
      \State $\vec{x}[k{:}m] \gets \vec{x}[k{:}m] - 2\vec{v}_k\left(\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{x}[k{:}m]\right)/(\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{v}_k)$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

As in \Cref{ch:householder}, we follow the parentheses: first compute the scalar $\vec{v}_k^\ast\vec{b}[k{:}m]$, then update the vector. Each step costs $O(m - k)$, and the total is about $4mn - 2n^2$ flops, the same order as a matrix--vector product. We never form $\vec{v}_k\vec{v}_k^\ast$.

Each step is the same in both algorithms; only the loop runs in opposite directions. This is just like forward and back substitution.

\paragraph{Consequences of implicit storage.}
\subparagraph{In place.} We scale $\vec{v}_k$ so that its $k$-th entry is $1$, so this entry need not be stored. Scaling does not change the reflector, which only depends on the direction of $\vec{v}_k$. The other $m - k$ entries fit exactly into column $k$ below the diagonal, which was just set to zero. So the factorization simply overwrites $A$. For $m = 4$, $n = 3$, writing $v_{jk}$ for entry $j$ of $\vec{v}_k$:
\begin{equation}
  A \;\to\; \begin{bmatrix}
    r_{11} & r_{12} & r_{13} \\
    v_{21} & r_{22} & r_{23} \\
    v_{31} & v_{32} & r_{33} \\
    v_{41} & v_{42} & v_{43}
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subparagraph{A full QR factorization from only $n$ vectors.} An explicit $Q$ takes $m^2$ numbers. The $\vec{v}_k$ take only about $mn - \frac{n^2}{2}$. This is a big saving when $m \gg n$. Applying $Q$ is cheaper too: $4mn - 2n^2$ flops instead of $2m^2$ with an explicit $Q$. If we really need $\hat{Q}$ (the first $n$ columns of $Q$), we compute $Q\vec{e}_1, \dots, Q\vec{e}_n$ with \Cref{alg:qx}.

\subparagraph{$Q$ is orthogonal by definition.} This point is important. The computed $\vec{v}_k$ have rounding errors. But $I - 2\vec{v}\vec{v}^\ast/(\vec{v}^\ast\vec{v})$ is an exact reflector for \emph{every} nonzero $\vec{v}$, so $Q$ is still exactly unitary. Rounding errors only change \emph{which} unitary matrix we get. They show up as a small backward error in $A$: $Q\tilde{R} = A + \delta A$ with $\norm{\delta A} \le O(\epsm)\norm{A}$.

Gram--Schmidt is different. It computes the columns of $\hat{Q}$ explicitly, so rounding errors make them lose orthogonality: by up to $O(\epsm\kappa(A))$ for MGS and $O(\epsm\kappa(A)^2)$ for CGS. For example, with $\kappa(A) = 10^8$, MGS keeps only about $8$ digits of orthogonality, and CGS keeps none. Householder stays orthogonal to $O(\epsm)$ for any $\kappa(A)$.

When we apply the Householder $Q$ (for example to compute $Q^\ast\vec{b}$), we only apply exact reflectors in floating point. This is backward stable, which is why least squares via Householder QR is reliable.

\section{Givens Rotations}
\label{sec:givens}

\paragraph{Motivation: Householder can be overkill.} Householder works on a whole column at a time. Step $k$ always updates the whole block $A[k{:}m, k{:}n]$, even if column $k$ has only one nonzero below the diagonal. This is wasteful when $A$ is already close to upper triangular. A typical example is an \emph{upper Hessenberg} matrix, which is zero below the first subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \times & \times & \times & \times \\
    \times & \times & \times & \times \\
    0 & \times & \times & \times \\
    0 & 0 & \times & \times
  \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here each column has just one entry to eliminate. Such matrices appear in the QR algorithm for eigenvalues (\Cref{ch:hessenberg-qr}). So we want an orthogonal transformation that zeros \emph{one entry at a time} and touches only the two rows involved. Givens rotations do exactly this.

\paragraph{The $2 \times 2$ case.} Consider the leading $2 \times 2$ block
\begingroup\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\endgroup
We want to eliminate $A_{21}$. Geometrically, the first column $\vec{a}_1 = (A_{11}, A_{21})^\T$ is a vector in the plane. We \emph{rotate} the plane so that $\vec{a}_1$ lands on the horizontal axis. The second column $\vec{a}_2$ rotates along with it.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.4, every node/.style={font=\small}, >=stealth]
  \foreach \x/\r/\lbla/\lblb/\pos in {0/0/{$\vec{a}_1$}/{$\vec{a}_2$}/right, 6/-25/{$G\vec{a}_1$}/{$G\vec{a}_2$}/below} {
    \begin{scope}[xshift=\x cm]
      \draw[->, black!60] (-0.4,0) -- (2.8,0);
      \draw[->, black!60] (0,-0.4) -- (0,2.2);
      \draw[->, very thick, red!70!black] (0,0) -- ({25+\r}:2.4) node[\pos] {\lbla};
      \draw[->, very thick, blue!70!black] (0,0) -- ({75+\r}:1.9) node[above] {\lblb};
    \end{scope}
  }
  \draw[black!60] (0.9,0) arc (0:25:0.9);
  \node at (12.5:1.15) {$\theta$};
  \draw[->, thick] (3.4,1.0) -- node[above] {rotate clockwise by $\theta$} (5.4,1.0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{definition}[Givens Rotation]
  With $\norm{\vec{a}_1} = \sqrt{A_{11}^2 + A_{21}^2}$, the \emph{Givens rotation} for $\vec{a}_1$ is
  \begingroup\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
  \begin{equation}
    G = \begin{bmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c = \frac{A_{11}}{\norm{\vec{a}_1}}, \qquad s = \frac{A_{21}}{\norm{\vec{a}_1}}.
  \end{equation}
  \endgroup
\end{definition}

Applying $G$ to $\vec{a}_1$ indeed puts it on the horizontal axis:
\begingroup\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{equation}
  G\begin{bmatrix} A_{11} \\ A_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\norm{\vec{a}_1}}\begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^2 + A_{21}^2 \\ -A_{21}A_{11} + A_{11}A_{21} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \norm{\vec{a}_1} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\endgroup

\newpage
$G$ is a rotation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $c^2 + s^2 = 1$, so $G^\T G = I$ and $\det G = 1$.
  \item If $\vec{a}_1$ makes the angle $\theta$ with the horizontal axis, then $c = \cos\theta$ and $s = \sin\theta$, and $G$ rotates clockwise by $\theta$.
\end{itemize}
Compare with Householder:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A Householder reflector is a \emph{reflection} ($\det = -1$). It maps a whole vector onto the direction of $\vec{e}_1$ at once.
  \item A Givens rotation is a \emph{rotation} ($\det = 1$). It acts in one two-dimensional plane and zeros one entry.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
  \vec{r}_i^\T \gets c\,\vec{r}_i^\T + s\,\vec{r}_j^\T, \qquad \vec{r}_j^\T \gets -s\,\vec{r}_i^\T + c\,\vec{r}_j^\T,
\end{equation}
and every other row is left alone (see \Cref{sec:givens-two-rows} for why).

To eliminate $A_{ji}$ ($j > i$), take
\begin{equation}
  c = \frac{A_{ii}}{\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}}, \qquad s = \frac{A_{ji}}{\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}},
\end{equation}
which is the $2 \times 2$ case transplanted to rows $i$ and $j$. Suppose the first $i - 1$ columns have already been reduced. Then one step gives the following picture.

Existing zeros are preserved:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the first $i - 1$ columns, rows $i$ and $j$ are both zero. A linear combination of two zeros is zero.
  \item In column $i$, row $i$ becomes $\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}$, row $j$ becomes $0$, and no other row changes.
\end{itemize}
So we sweep through the subdiagonal nonzeros column by column, from left to right. Within column $i$, we pair row $i$ with $j = i + 1, \dots, m$ in turn. For a $4 \times 3$ matrix this takes six rotations, one per subdiagonal entry.

In the picture below, $\times$ is a generic nonzero, red marks the entries that the current rotation changes, and the bold $\bm{0}$ is the entry it eliminates.
\begingroup
\newcommand*{\Xc}{\textcolor{red!70!black}{\times}}
\newcommand*{\Zc}{\textcolor{red!70!black}{\bm{0}}}
\begin{align*}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  &\xrightarrow{G(1, 2)} \begin{bmatrix} \Xc & \Xc & \Xc \\ \Zc & \Xc & \Xc \\ \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{G(1, 3)} \begin{bmatrix} \Xc & \Xc & \Xc \\ 0 & \times & \times \\ \Zc & \Xc & \Xc \\ \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{G(1, 4)} \begin{bmatrix} \Xc & \Xc & \Xc \\ 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times \\ \Zc & \Xc & \Xc \end{bmatrix} \\[8pt]
  &\xrightarrow{G(2, 3)} \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \Xc & \Xc \\ 0 & \Zc & \Xc \\ 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{G(2, 4)} \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \Xc & \Xc \\ 0 & 0 & \times \\ 0 & \Zc & \Xc \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{G(3, 4)} \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \Xc \\ 0 & 0 & \Zc \end{bmatrix} = R.
\end{align*}
\endgroup
Notice that $G(1, 3)$ does not touch row $2$, so the zero created by $G(1, 2)$ survives. From $G(2, 3)$ on, the zeros in column $1$ are carried along as combinations of zeros.

Labeling the rotations in the order they are applied, we get
\begin{equation}
  G_N \cdots G_1A = R, \qquad Q = G_1^\ast \cdots G_N^\ast,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of subdiagonal nonzeros. For a dense $m \times n$ matrix,
\begin{equation*}
  N = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(m - i) = mn - \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\end{equation*}
As with Householder, $Q$ is not formed explicitly. We store the pairs $(c, s)$ and apply the rotations in sequence when needed, just as in \Cref{alg:qtb,alg:qx}. Every $G$ is orthogonal with $\kappa = 1$, so by the error-amplification framework of \Cref{ch:householder}, Givens QR is backward stable as well.

\paragraph{Efficiency.}
\subparagraph{Dense matrices: slower than Householder.} Each rotation acts on two rows, at $6$ flops per column ($4$ multiplications and $2$ additions). Eliminating column $i$ takes $m - i$ rotations, each acting on rows of length about $n - i$, for a total of about
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n}6(m - i)(n - i) \approx 3mn^2 - n^3 \text{ flops},
\end{equation}
about $1.5$ times the cost of Householder ($2mn^2 - \frac{2}{3}n^3$).

\subparagraph{Sparse or nearly triangular matrices: possibly faster.} For an $n \times n$ upper Hessenberg matrix, only $n - 1$ rotations are needed (one per subdiagonal entry), each costing $O(n)$, for a total of about $3n^2$ flops. Givens touches only the entries that must be eliminated and so exploits sparsity naturally.

\section{Full and Reduced QR}

Let $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ with $m \ge n$.

\begin{definition}[Reduced and Full QR Factorizations]
  A \emph{reduced QR factorization} of $A$ is
  \begin{equation}
    A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}, \qquad \hat{Q} \in \K^{m \times n} \text{ with orthonormal columns}, \quad \hat{R} \in \K^{n \times n} \text{ upper triangular}.
  \end{equation}
  A \emph{full QR factorization} of $A$ is
  \begin{equation}
    A = QR, \qquad Q \in \K^{m \times m} \text{ unitary}, \quad R = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{R} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \in \K^{m \times n}, \ \hat{R} \text{ upper triangular}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Gram--Schmidt produces a reduced factorization; Householder and Givens produce a full one. Drawing each factor to scale, this gives the following picture.

Splitting $Q = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{Q} & \hat{Q}_\perp \end{bmatrix}$ by columns,
\begin{equation}
  QR = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{Q} & \hat{Q}_\perp \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \hat{R} \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \hat{Q}\hat{R}.
\end{equation}
In the picture, the extra $m - n$ columns $\hat{Q}_\perp$ (red) multiply only the zero block of $R$ (also red) and do not contribute to the product. Their role is to complete $\hat{Q}$ to an orthonormal basis of $\K^m$; when $A$ has full rank, the columns of $\hat{Q}_\perp$ span $\range(A)^\perp$.

\section{Existence}

\begin{theorem}[Existence of QR]
  \label{thm:qr-exists}
  Every $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ with $m \ge n$ has a full QR factorization and a reduced QR factorization.
\end{theorem}

The proof is constructive: we run Gram--Schmidt, look at where it can fail, and fix those cases.

\begin{proof}
  \emph{Case 1: $A$ has full rank, reduced QR.} Gram--Schmidt can only fail at the normalization $\vec{q}_k = \hat{\vec{q}}_k/\norm{\hat{\vec{q}}_k}$, if $\hat{\vec{q}}_k = \zerovec$. This happens only if
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\hat{\vec{q}}_k} = 0 \iff \vec{a}_k \in \spn(\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{k-1}) = \spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_{k-1}).
  \end{equation}
  Then $\vec{a}_k$ would be a combination of the earlier columns, which contradicts full rank. So Gram--Schmidt never fails, and every $r_{kk} = \norm{\hat{\vec{q}}_k} > 0$.

  \emph{Case 2: $A$ is rank deficient, reduced QR.} We run Gram--Schmidt as usual. When $\hat{\vec{q}}_k = \zerovec$, we set $r_{kk} = 0$ and pick any unit vector $\vec{q}_k$ orthogonal to $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{k-1}$. Such a vector exists because $k - 1 < n \le m$. We still have
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{a}_k = \sum_{i<k}r_{ik}\vec{q}_i + 0 \cdot \vec{q}_k,
  \end{equation}
  so $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$ holds. The only difference is that $\hat{R}$ has some zeros on its diagonal.

  \emph{Case 3: full QR.} We first compute a reduced QR factorization. Then we add $m - n$ orthonormal vectors to $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_n$ to get an orthonormal basis of $\K^m$, and we add $m - n$ zero rows to $\hat{R}$.
\end{proof}

\section{Uniqueness}

\paragraph{When QR is not unique.}
\subparagraph{Full QR with $m > n$ is not unique.} In $QR = \hat{Q}\hat{R} + \hat{Q}_\perp \cdot 0$, the columns of $\hat{Q}_\perp$ only multiply the zero block of $R$, so they do not affect the product. Any orthonormal basis of $\range(\hat{Q})^\perp$ works. For example, we can replace $\hat{Q}_\perp$ by $\hat{Q}_\perp U$ for any unitary $U \in \K^{(m-n) \times (m-n)}$.

\subparagraph{Rank-deficient $A$.} If $\hat{\vec{q}}_k = \zerovec$, then $r_{kk} = 0$, so $\vec{q}_k$ is multiplied by zero in $\vec{a}_k = \sum_{i<k} r_{ik}\vec{q}_i + 0 \cdot \vec{q}_k$. Any unit vector orthogonal to $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{k-1}$ works.

\begin{example}[Non-Uniqueness for a Rank-Deficient Matrix]
  Let
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{q}_1 = \vec{e}_1, \qquad \hat{\vec{q}}_2 = \vec{a}_2 - \vec{e}_1 = \zerovec.
  \end{equation}
  Both $\vec{q}_2 = \vec{e}_2$ and $\vec{q}_2 = \vec{e}_3$ work, with the same $\hat{R} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.
\end{example}

\subparagraph{Phases.} Even for full-rank $A$, some freedom is left. For any $d_k$ with $\abs{d_k} = 1$, we can multiply $\vec{q}_k$ by $d_k$ and row $k$ of $\hat{R}$ by $\bar{d}_k$. The product does not change, because $\bar{d}_kd_k = 1$. In the real case, this means each column can flip its sign. So for uniqueness we need a normalization, such as $r_{kk} > 0$.

\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of Reduced QR]
  \label{thm:qr-unique}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times n}$, $m \ge n$, have full column rank. Then the reduced QR factorization $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$ with $r_{jj} > 0$ for all $j$ is unique.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Let $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R} = \tilde{Q}\tilde{R}$ be two such factorizations. We show that the columns agree, $\tilde{\vec{q}}_k = \vec{q}_k$, one $k$ at a time. Fix $k$ and write $\vec{q} = \vec{q}_k$ and $\tilde{\vec{q}} = \tilde{\vec{q}}_k$. We use the inner product $\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \vec{x}^\ast\vec{y}$.

  \emph{Step 1: what we know about $\vec{q}$ and $\tilde{\vec{q}}$.} Since $A$ has full rank, all $r_{jj} \ne 0$, so $\hat{R}$ is upper triangular and invertible. From $\hat{Q} = A\hat{R}^{-1}$, the first $k$ columns of $\hat{Q}$ span the same space as the first $k$ columns of $A$:
  \begin{equation}
    \spn(\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_k) = \spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_k) \qquad \text{for every } k.
  \end{equation}
  So $\vec{q}$ lies in $\spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_k)$. It is also orthogonal to $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{k-1}$, hence to $\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_{k-1}$. The same holds for $\tilde{\vec{q}}$.

  \emph{Step 2: $\tilde{\vec{q}}$ is a multiple of $\vec{q}$.} Since $\spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_k) = \spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_{k-1}, \vec{q})$, we can write
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{\vec{q}} = \alpha\vec{q} + \sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\beta_j\vec{a}_j.
  \end{equation}
  We take inner products with $\vec{q}$ and with $\tilde{\vec{q}}$. The $\vec{a}_j$ terms drop out, because both $\vec{q}$ and $\tilde{\vec{q}}$ are orthogonal to every $\vec{a}_j$ with $j < k$:
  \begin{equation}
    \ip{\vec{q}}{\tilde{\vec{q}}} = \alpha, \qquad 1 = \ip{\tilde{\vec{q}}}{\tilde{\vec{q}}} = \alpha\ip{\tilde{\vec{q}}}{\vec{q}} = \alpha\bar{\alpha} = \abs{\alpha}^2.
  \end{equation}
  With these two facts,
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\tilde{\vec{q}} - \alpha\vec{q}}^2 = \ip{\tilde{\vec{q}}}{\tilde{\vec{q}}} - \alpha\ip{\tilde{\vec{q}}}{\vec{q}} - \bar{\alpha}\ip{\vec{q}}{\tilde{\vec{q}}} + \abs{\alpha}^2 = 1 - 1 - 1 + 1 = 0.
  \end{equation}
  So $\tilde{\vec{q}} = \alpha\vec{q}$ with $\abs{\alpha} = 1$.

  \emph{Step 3: $r_{kk} > 0$ forces $\alpha = 1$.} From $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$ we get $r_{kk} = \ip{\vec{q}_k}{\vec{a}_k}$. In the same way,
  \begin{equation*}
    \tilde{r}_{kk} = \ip{\tilde{\vec{q}}_k}{\vec{a}_k} = \ip{\alpha\vec{q}_k}{\vec{a}_k} = \bar{\alpha}r_{kk}.
  \end{equation*}
  Both $r_{kk}$ and $\tilde{r}_{kk}$ are positive, so $\bar{\alpha} = \tilde{r}_{kk}/r_{kk} > 0$. A positive number with $\abs{\alpha} = 1$ is $1$, so $\alpha = 1$.

  So $\tilde{\vec{q}}_k = \vec{q}_k$ for every $k$, i.e., $\tilde{Q} = \hat{Q}$. Then $\tilde{R} = \hat{Q}^\ast A = \hat{R}$.
\end{proof}

In short: inside $\spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_k)$, the directions orthogonal to $\spn(\vec{a}_1, \dots, \vec{a}_{k-1})$ form a line. So $A$ fixes $\vec{q}_k$ up to a factor of absolute value $1$, and $r_{kk} > 0$ fixes that factor.

\subsection{Householder as an Existence Proof}

The Householder algorithm never breaks down. The only special case is $\vec{x} = A[k{:}m, k] = \zerovec$; then we simply take $F_k = I$. So Householder gives a full QR factorization of \emph{any} $A$, rank-deficient or not. This is a shorter existence proof than the one based on Gram--Schmidt. Trefethen and Bau prove existence with Gram--Schmidt in Lecture 7; their Lecture 10 implicitly gives the Householder proof~\cite{TB}.

\subsection{Uniqueness via Cholesky}

If $A = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$, then
\begin{equation}
  A^\ast A = \hat{R}^\ast\hat{Q}^\ast\hat{Q}\hat{R} = \hat{R}^\ast\hat{R}.
\end{equation}
So $\hat{R}^\ast$ is a Cholesky factor of $A^\ast A$. If $A$ has full column rank, $A^\ast A$ is Hermitian positive definite. Its Cholesky factorization with positive diagonal is unique. So $\hat{R}$ is unique, and then $\hat{Q} = A\hat{R}^{-1}$ is unique too.

This is a proof, not an algorithm. We should not \emph{compute} QR this way, because forming $A^\ast A$ squares the condition number (\Cref{ch:ls}).

\subsection{Why a Givens Rotation Changes Only Two Rows}
\label{sec:givens-two-rows}
Think of each column of $A$ as a point in $\K^m$. Row $k$ of $A$ collects the $k$-th coordinates of all these points. $G(i, j)$ rotates in the plane spanned by $\vec{e}_i$ and $\vec{e}_j$. This is like a rotation about the $z$-axis in 3D: every point moves, but only its $x$ and $y$ coordinates change, and its $z$ coordinate stays the same. In the same way, every column of $A$ moves, but only its entries $i$ and $j$ change. So only rows $i$ and $j$ of $A$ change.

\subsection{Computing $c$ and $s$ Without Overflow}

Computing $\sqrt{A_{ii}^2 + A_{ji}^2}$ directly can go wrong. The squares can overflow for large entries and underflow for small ones. So we first divide by the larger entry. If $\abs{A_{ji}} > \abs{A_{ii}}$, we set
\begin{equation*}
  \tau = A_{ii}/A_{ji}, \qquad s = 1/\sqrt{1 + \tau^2}, \qquad c = s\tau,
\end{equation*}
and we swap the roles of $A_{ii}$ and $A_{ji}$ otherwise. Now $\tau^2 \le 1$, so nothing overflows. This gives $(c, s)$ up to a common sign, which still zeros the entry. The function \texttt{hypot} in many languages works this way. Demmel also shows how to store a rotation in a single number, so that it fits into the zeroed entry~\cite[\S3.4.2]{Demmel}.

\subsection{Other Advantages of Givens Rotations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Parallelism.} Rotations on different pairs of rows do not interact. We can apply them at the same time.
  \item \emph{Updating.} Suppose we already have the QR factorization of $A$, and a new row is added. We can eliminate the new row with $n$ Givens rotations, instead of factoring again. This is common in recursive least squares.
  \item \emph{Hessenberg matrices.} QR of a Hessenberg matrix is the core step of the QR algorithm for eigenvalues~\cite[Lectures 28--29]{TB}.
\end{itemize}
