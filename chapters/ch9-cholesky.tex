\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture we solved the least squares problem $\min_{\vec{x}}\norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2$ in two ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{By QR.} With a full QR factorization,
  \begin{equation*}
    \norm{A\vec{x} - \vec{b}}^2 = \norm{\hat{R}\vec{x} - (Q^\ast\vec{b})_{1:n}}^2 + \norm{(Q^\ast\vec{b})_{n+1:m}}^2.
  \end{equation*}
  We make the first term zero by solving $\hat{R}\vec{x} = (Q^\ast\vec{b})_{1:n}$. The second term does not depend on $\vec{x}$. It is the squared distance from $\vec{b}$ to $\range(A)$.
  \item \emph{By the optimality condition.} At the minimizer, the residual is orthogonal to $\range(A)$: $\ip{A\vec{d}}{A\vec{x}} = \ip{A\vec{d}}{\vec{b}}$ for all $\vec{d}$ (\Cref{thm:ls-optimality}). Written as a linear system, these are the normal equations $A^\ast A\vec{x} = A^\ast\vec{b}$ (\Cref{thm:normal}).
\end{itemize}
The matrix $A^\ast A$ of the normal equations is a Gram matrix. We saw that Gram matrices are exactly the positive semidefinite matrices: $M$ is Hermitian positive semidefinite if and only if $M = B^\ast B$ for some $B$ (\Cref{thm:gram}).

The factor $B$ in $M = B^\ast B$ is not unique at all. For any unitary $U$, $(UB)^\ast(UB) = B^\ast U^\ast UB = B^\ast B$. Since we have so much freedom, we can ask:
\begin{center}
  \emph{If $A = B^\ast B$ is positive definite, can we choose $B$ to be upper triangular?}
\end{center}
If yes, then $A = R^\ast R$ with $R$ upper triangular. Then solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{c}$ takes only two triangular solves: first $R^\ast\vec{y} = \vec{c}$, then $R\vec{x} = \vec{y}$. In this lecture we show that the answer is yes. The resulting factorization is called the \emph{Cholesky factorization}. Even better, we will see that it needs no pivoting and is always backward stable.

Here is our plan.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Derive Cholesky from LU by making elimination \emph{symmetric}, and turn it into an algorithm.
  \item Prove that the algorithm never breaks down (existence) and that it does not amplify rounding errors (stability).
  \item Look at the elimination in blocks, which leads to \emph{Schur complements}.
  \item Show that Cholesky and QR compute the same triangular factor.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Conventions.} In this lecture $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ is a square matrix. It is not related to the $m \times n$ matrix of least squares. We say that $A$ is \emph{s.p.d.}\ if it is Hermitian positive definite, i.e., $A = A^\ast$ and $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x} > 0$ for all $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$. (In the real case this means symmetric positive definite, hence the name.) We write the Cholesky factorization as $A = LL^\ast$ with $L$ lower triangular; this is the same as $A = R^\ast R$ with $R = L^\ast$.

Since $A$ is Hermitian, its first row is the conjugate transpose of its first column. So we can split off the first row and column as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chol-block}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha & \vec{a}^\ast \\ \vec{a} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \alpha = A_{11} \in \R, \quad \vec{a} = A[2{:}m, 1] \in \K^{m-1}, \quad A_{22} = A[2{:}m, 2{:}m].
\end{equation}

\section{Deriving Cholesky by Symmetric Elimination}
\label{sec:chol-derive}

The idea is simple. We already know how to factor a matrix by Gaussian elimination. We run elimination on $A$. But at every step we do the \emph{same} operation on the rows and on the columns. This way the matrix stays Hermitian. Let us go through one step.

\paragraph{Step 1: one step of LU.} The first step of LU subtracts $a_i/\alpha$ times row $1$ from row $i + 1$, for every row below the first. In matrix form, this is multiplication from the left by a unit lower triangular $L_1$:
\begin{equation}
  L_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\vec{a}/\alpha & I \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  L_1A = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha & \vec{a}^\ast \\ 0 & A_{22} - \dfrac{\vec{a}\vec{a}^\ast}{\alpha} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The first column is now zero below the diagonal. The lower right block has changed to
\begin{equation}
  S \defeq A_{22} - \frac{\vec{a}\vec{a}^\ast}{\alpha}.
\end{equation}
The matrix $S$ is called a \emph{Schur complement}. We study it in \Cref{sec:schur}. Note that $\vec{a}\vec{a}^\ast$ is an outer product. So the update is a rank-one change of $A_{22}$.

Can we divide by $\alpha$? Yes: $\alpha = A_{11} = \vec{e}_1^\ast A\vec{e}_1 > 0$ by positive definiteness.

\paragraph{Step 2: add the matching column operation.} If we continued with LU on $S$, we would get $A = LU$, where $L$ and $U$ are not related. We want $A = LL^\ast$ instead. So we keep the matrix symmetric. After the row operation $L_1$ on the left, we apply the matching column operation $L_1^\ast$ on the right. The result is
\begin{equation}
  L_1AL_1^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let us check this. Right multiplication by $L_1^\ast$ subtracts $\overline{a_j}/\alpha$ times column $1$ from column $j + 1$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It does not change the lower right block, since column $1$ of $L_1A$ is zero below the diagonal.
  \item It makes the first row zero. Since $A$ is Hermitian, the $(1, j + 1)$ entry of $L_1A$ is $\overline{a_j}$. So it becomes $\overline{a_j} - (\overline{a_j}/\alpha)\alpha = 0$.
\end{itemize}
This is where symmetry helps. The first row is the conjugate of the first column. So the column operation that clears the row uses the same multipliers as the row operation that cleared the column. One matrix $L_1$ handles both sides.

\paragraph{Step 3: normalize by \texorpdfstring{$\sqrt{\alpha}$}{the Square Root of the Pivot}.} Finally, we scale the top left entry to $1$. We divide the first row and the first column by $\sqrt{\alpha}$. In matrix form, we replace $L_1$ by $\bar{L}_1 = \diag(1/\sqrt{\alpha}, 1, \dots, 1)L_1$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \bar{L}_1A\bar{L}_1^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  \bar{L}_1^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\alpha} & 0 \\ \vec{a}/\sqrt{\alpha} & I \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
(To invert $\bar{L}_1$, undo the scaling and then flip the sign of the multipliers.) We move $\bar{L}_1$ and $\bar{L}_1^\ast$ to the other side and get
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chol-step}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\alpha} & 0 \\ \vec{a}/\sqrt{\alpha} & I \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\alpha} & \vec{a}^\ast/\sqrt{\alpha} \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Now suppose that $S$ has a Cholesky factorization $S = L_SL_S^\ast$. We put it into \eqref{eq:chol-step} and multiply out:
\begin{equation}
  A = LL^\ast, \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{\alpha} & 0 \\ \vec{a}/\sqrt{\alpha} & L_S \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
In words: \emph{the first column of $L$ is the first column of $A$ divided by $\sqrt{\alpha}$, and the rest of $L$ is the Cholesky factor of the Schur complement $S$.} So Cholesky is a recursion: compute one column, update the remaining block to $S$, and repeat on $S$. Only one thing can go wrong: some later $S$ might not be s.p.d., and then its top left entry might not be positive. We rule this out in \Cref{sec:chol-exists}.

\begin{strategy}[Computing a Cholesky Factorization by Hand]
  Repeat for $k = 1, \dots, m$:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Let $d_k$ be the top left entry of the current matrix.
    \item Column $k$ of $L$ is the current first column divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$.
    \item Replace the current matrix by its Schur complement: delete the first row and column, and subtract $\vec{a}\vec{a}^\ast/d_k$ from what is left.
  \end{itemize}
\end{strategy}

\begin{example}[A $3 \times 3$ Example]
  \label{ex:chol-3x3}
  Let
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  \emph{Step 1.} $d_1 = 4$, so the first column of $L$ is $(4, 2, 2)^\T/2 = (2, 1, 1)^\T$. The Schur complement is
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  \emph{Step 2.} $d_2 = 4$, so the next column is $(4, 2)^\T/2 = (2, 1)^\T$. The Schur complement is $5 - 2 \cdot 2/4 = 4$.

  \emph{Step 3.} $d_3 = 4$, so the last entry is $\sqrt{4} = 2$. Altogether,
  \begin{equation}
    L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix},
  \end{equation}
  We can check that $LL^\T = A$.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Why the square root?} If we eliminate symmetrically without taking square roots, we get $A = \tilde{L}D\tilde{L}^\ast$. Here $\tilde{L}$ is unit lower triangular and $D = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_m)$ holds the pivots. LU puts all of $D$ into $U = D\tilde{L}^\ast$. Cholesky splits it evenly between the two factors:
\begin{equation}
  A = (\tilde{L}D^{1/2})(\tilde{L}D^{1/2})^\ast = LL^\ast.
\end{equation}
This needs every $d_k > 0$, so that $D^{1/2}$ is real.

\section{The Cholesky Algorithm}

\paragraph{The right-looking algorithm.} We now turn the recursion into an in-place algorithm. Step $k$ computes column $k$ of $L$ and then applies the Schur complement update to the lower right block. Since every Schur complement is Hermitian, we only store and update the lower triangle.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Cholesky factorization, right-looking, in place (lower triangle).}
  \label{alg:chol-right}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, m$}
      \State $A_{kk} \gets \sqrt{A_{kk}}$
      \For{$i = k + 1, \dots, m$}
        \State $A_{ik} \gets A_{ik} / A_{kk}$ \Comment{column $k$ of $L$}
      \EndFor
      \For{$j = k + 1, \dots, m$}
        \For{$i = j, \dots, m$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} - A_{ik}\overline{A_{jk}}$ \Comment{Schur complement update}
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In line 2, $A_{kk}$ is the current pivot $d_k$, the top left entry of the current Schur complement, not an entry of the original $A$. In the update, $A_{ik}$ and $A_{jk}$ have both been divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$, so we subtract $a_i\overline{a_j}/d_k$, the $(i, j)$ entry of $\vec{a}\vec{a}^\ast/\alpha$. At the end, the lower triangle of $A$ holds $L$. In short form:
\begin{center}
  \texttt{A[:,k] /= sqrt(A[k,k])}, \quad then \quad \texttt{A[i,j] -= A[i,k]*conj(A[j,k])}.
\end{center}

\paragraph{Comparison with LU.} Cholesky differs from LU in three ways.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Normalization.} Columns are divided by the square root of the pivot, as explained above.
  \item \emph{Half the work and storage.} Every Schur complement is Hermitian. So we only need to keep one triangle. The other one is its conjugate transpose. The cost drops from about $\frac{2}{3}m^3$ flops for LU to about $\frac{1}{3}m^3$.
  \item \emph{No pivoting.} We prove in \Cref{sec:chol-exists,sec:chol-errors} that Cholesky without pivoting never breaks down and is stable.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where positive definiteness enters.} The algorithm uses positive definiteness in exactly one place: the square root $\sqrt{A_{kk}}$ in line 2. Every \emph{updated} pivot must be positive. Then it has a real square root, and we can divide by it. Positive definiteness of $A$ directly tells us only that the \emph{original} diagonal entries are positive. To show that the updated pivots are also positive, we need a proof. This is the existence theorem below. The next example shows that a proof is really needed.

\begin{example}[A Symmetric Matrix That Is Not Positive Definite]
  Run \Cref{alg:chol-right} on
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  For $k = 1$: $\sqrt{1} = 1$, the first column becomes $(1, 2)^\T$, and the update gives $A_{22} = 1 - 2 \cdot 2 = -3$. For $k = 2$ we need $\sqrt{-3}$, so the algorithm fails. Both original diagonal entries are $1$. The problem is the updated pivot.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Left-looking and up-looking variants.} The right-looking algorithm is not the only possible order of computation. Other orders give especially good memory access patterns. To see the options, we compare entries on both sides of $A = LL^\ast$ for $i \ge j$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:chol-entries}
  L_{jj} = \sqrt{A_{jj} - \sum_{k<j}\abs{L_{jk}}^2}, \qquad L_{ij} = \frac{1}{L_{jj}}\left(A_{ij} - \sum_{k<j}L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}\right) \quad (i > j).
\end{equation}
Any loop order gives a valid algorithm, as long as it evaluates these formulas using only entries of $L$ that are already finished. All variants below do exactly the same floating-point operations. In exact arithmetic they produce the same $L$. They differ only in the order of the three nested loops over $i$, $j$, $k$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
  \toprule
  Variant & Outer loop & Produces per step & Reads & Writes \\
  \midrule
  right-looking & $k$ & column $k$ of $L$ & column $k$ & whole trailing Schur complement \\
  left-looking & $j$ & column $j$ of $L$ & columns $1, \dots, j - 1$ & column $j$ only \\
  up-looking & $i$ & row $i$ of $L$ & rows $1, \dots, i - 1$ & row $i$ only \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subparagraph{Left-looking.} The right-looking algorithm is \emph{eager}. As soon as a column is finished, it applies that column's update to the whole trailing block. The left-looking algorithm is \emph{lazy}. When it reaches column $j$, it applies all the updates from the finished columns to its left at once. Then it normalizes the column. So during step $j$ it writes only to column $j$, and after that step column $j$ is never touched again. In the right-looking variant, by contrast, every step writes to the whole trailing block. The innermost loop runs down a column. This suits column-major languages (Julia, Fortran, MATLAB).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Cholesky factorization, left-looking, in place (lower triangle).}
  \label{alg:chol-left}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$j = 1, \dots, m$}
      \For{$k = 1, \dots, j - 1$}
        \For{$i = j, \dots, m$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} - A_{ik}\overline{A_{jk}}$
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
      \State $s \gets \sqrt{A_{jj}}$ \Comment{save it: line 7 overwrites $A_{jj}$}
      \For{$i = j, \dots, m$}
        \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} / s$
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subparagraph{Up-looking.} Here $L$ is computed row by row. Row $i$ depends only on its own earlier entries and on the finished rows above it. Row $i$ of $L$, conjugated, is column $i$ of $R = L^\ast$. So a column-major code stores the upper triangular factor $R$ instead. Then walking along a row of $L$ becomes walking down a column of $R$. In \emph{packed storage}, we store the upper triangle column by column. Column $i$ has length $i$ and comes right after column $i - 1$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 & 7 & 11 \\ & 3 & 5 & 8 & 12 \\ & & 6 & 9 & 13 \\ & & & 10 & 14 \\ & & & & 15 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Computing column $i$ uses only earlier data. So when $A$ grows by one row and one column, we just append one new segment at the end of the factor. Nothing computed before has to change.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Cholesky factorization, up-looking, in place (lower triangle).}
  \label{alg:chol-up}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$i = 1, \dots, m$}
      \For{$j = 1, \dots, i$}
        \For{$k = 1, \dots, j - 1$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} - A_{ik}\overline{A_{jk}}$
        \EndFor
        \If{$j < i$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} / A_{jj}$ \Comment{$A_{jj}$ already holds $L_{jj}$}
        \Else
          \State $A_{ii} \gets \sqrt{A_{ii}}$
        \EndIf
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In \Cref{alg:chol-left,alg:chol-up} it is tempting to divide by $\sqrt{A_{jj}}$ in every case. This is wrong.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In the left-looking loop, $A_{jj}$ itself is overwritten when $i = j$. This is why that algorithm saves $s$ first.
  \item In the up-looking loop, for $j < i$ the entry $A_{jj}$ already holds $L_{jj}$. This is why that algorithm divides by $A_{jj}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Existence: Cholesky Never Breaks Down}
\label{sec:chol-exists}

We have seen that LU without pivoting can meet a zero pivot. For example, $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ fails at the very first step. Cholesky needs even more. The pivots must be \emph{real and positive}, because we take their square roots. In this section we show that for s.p.d.\ matrices this always holds.

Here is the idea of the proof. After $k$ steps of symmetric elimination, our current matrix is $A$ times a nonsingular matrix on the right, and times its conjugate transpose on the left. We first show that such a ``sandwich'' of an s.p.d.\ matrix is again s.p.d. Then all its diagonal entries are positive. In particular, the next pivot is positive.

\begin{lemma}[Congruence Preserves Positive Definiteness]
  \label{lem:congruence}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ be s.p.d.\ and $B \in \K^{m \times m}$ be nonsingular. Then $B^\ast AB$ is s.p.d.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  First, $B^\ast AB$ is Hermitian, since $(B^\ast AB)^\ast = B^\ast A^\ast B = B^\ast AB$. Next we show positive definiteness. Let $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$. Since $B$ is nonsingular, $B\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$. Hence
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{x}^\ast B^\ast AB\vec{x} = (B\vec{x})^\ast A(B\vec{x}) > 0,
  \end{equation}
  as desired.
\end{proof}

The map $A \mapsto B^\ast AB$ is called a \emph{congruence}. We do need $B$ to be nonsingular. If $B\vec{x} = \zerovec$ for some $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$, then $\vec{x}^\ast B^\ast AB\vec{x} = 0$. Then we only get positive semidefiniteness.

\begin{theorem}[Existence of the Cholesky Factorization]
  \label{thm:chol-exists}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ be s.p.d. Then Cholesky without pivoting meets only real, positive pivots $d_1, \dots, d_m$. Hence it produces $A = LL^\ast$, where $L$ is lower triangular with positive diagonal. Also, the pivots have the lower bound
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pivot-bound}
    d_k \ge \frac{1}{\norm{A^{-1}}_2} = \lambda_{\min}(A), \qquad k = 1, \dots, m.
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  We show by induction on $k = 0, \dots, m - 1$ that the pivot $d_{k+1}$ is real and positive.

  Suppose that the first $k$ pivots are positive. Then the elimination matrices $L_1, \dots, L_k$ of the first $k$ steps are defined. (For $k = 0$ there is nothing to assume.) After $k$ symmetric steps, we have
  \begin{equation}
    S \defeq L_k \cdots L_1AL_1^\ast \cdots L_k^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} \diag(d_1, \dots, d_k) & 0 \\ 0 & S^{(k)} \end{bmatrix},
  \end{equation}
  where $S^{(k)}$ is the current Schur complement. Its top left entry is the next pivot, $d_{k+1} = S_{k+1,k+1}$.

  Let $B = L_1^\ast \cdots L_k^\ast$, so that $S = B^\ast AB$. Each $L_i$ is unit lower triangular, so it has determinant $1$. Hence $B$ is nonsingular. By \Cref{lem:congruence}, $S$ is s.p.d. Now:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{The pivot is real.} $S$ is Hermitian, so $S_{k+1,k+1} = \overline{S_{k+1,k+1}}$.
    \item \emph{The pivot is positive.} A standard basis vector picks out a diagonal entry, $\vec{e}_j^\ast S\vec{e}_j = S_{jj}$. So by positive definiteness,
    \begin{equation}
      d_{k+1} = S_{k+1,k+1} = \vec{e}_{k+1}^\ast S\vec{e}_{k+1} > 0.
    \end{equation}
  \end{itemize}
  This completes the induction. With all pivots positive, the construction of the previous section gives $A = LL^\ast$ with $L_{kk} = \sqrt{d_k} > 0$.

  The lower bound \eqref{eq:pivot-bound} needs a fact about Schur complements. We prove it in \Cref{sec:schur}.
\end{proof}

Note that ``real'' and ``positive'' come from different assumptions. The pivot is real because $S$ is Hermitian. It is positive because $S$ is positive definite. Along the way we also proved a useful fact: \emph{every diagonal entry of a positive definite matrix is positive}. The lower bound \eqref{eq:pivot-bound} says even more than positivity. No pivot is smaller than $\lambda_{\min}(A) = 1/\norm{A^{-1}}_2$, for every $k$.

\begin{corollary}[Uniqueness of the Cholesky Factorization]
  \label{cor:chol-unique}
  An s.p.d.\ matrix has exactly one factorization $A = LL^\ast$, where $L$ is lower triangular with positive diagonal.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
  Existence is \Cref{thm:chol-exists}. Suppose $A = LL^\ast = MM^\ast$ with both factors of this form. Then $M^{-1}L = M^\ast L^{-\ast}$. The left side is lower triangular, and the right side is upper triangular. So both sides equal a diagonal matrix $\Delta$. Its entries $L_{kk}/M_{kk}$ are positive. Also,
  \begin{equation}
    \Delta\Delta^\ast = (M^{-1}L)(M^{-1}L)^\ast = M^{-1}LL^\ast M^{-\ast} = M^{-1}MM^\ast M^{-\ast} = I,
  \end{equation}
  so $\abs{\Delta_{kk}} = 1$. A positive number of absolute value $1$ is $1$, so $\Delta = I$ and $L = M$.
\end{proof}

\section{Rounding Errors}
\label{sec:chol-errors}

Existence tells us that the algorithm runs to completion in exact arithmetic. We now ask what happens in floating point.

\paragraph{The key quantity for LU.} Recall from the error analysis of LU (\Cref{thm:lu-backward}) that the computed factors satisfy $\tilde{L}\tilde{U} = A + \delta A$ with
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\delta A} \le cm\epsm\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}} \quad \text{(entrywise)}.
\end{equation}
So the key quantity is $\abs{L}\abs{U}$. If it is of the same size as $\abs{A}$, the algorithm is backward stable. If it is much larger, errors are amplified. Without pivoting, $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ can be arbitrarily large. For example, for $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} \delta & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with small $\delta$, $L$ contains the entry $1/\delta$.

\paragraph{No element growth in Cholesky.} For Cholesky, $U = L^\ast$, so the key quantity is $\abs{L}\abs{L^\ast}$. We will show that it is never larger than the largest entry of $A$.

\begin{proposition}[No Growth in the Cholesky Factor]
  \label{prop:chol-growth}
  Let $A = LL^\ast$ be s.p.d. Then for all $i, j$,
  \begin{equation}
    \abs{L_{ij}} \le \sqrt{A_{ii}}, \qquad \left(\abs{L}\abs{L^\ast}\right)_{ij} \le \sqrt{A_{ii}A_{jj}} \le \max_{k,l}\abs{A_{kl}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  We go in four steps.

  \emph{Step 1: the diagonal of $A$.} The $(i, i)$ entry of $A = LL^\ast$ is
  \begin{equation*}
    A_{ii} = \sum_k L_{ik}\overline{L_{ik}} = \sum_k\abs{L_{ik}}^2.
  \end{equation*}
  So $A_{ii}$ is the squared length of row $i$ of $L$. Each $\abs{L_{ij}}^2$ is one term of this sum, so $\abs{L_{ij}}^2 \le A_{ii}$. This is the first claim.

  \emph{Step 2: an entry of $\abs{L}\abs{L^\ast}$.} By the rule for matrix products, with $\abs{L^\ast}_{kj} = \abs{L_{jk}}$,
  \begin{equation*}
    \left(\abs{L}\abs{L^\ast}\right)_{ij} = \sum_k\abs{L_{ik}}\abs{L_{jk}}.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{Step 3: Cauchy--Schwarz.} For real numbers $a_k, b_k$, the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality says
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_k a_kb_k \le \left(\sum_k a_k^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_k b_k^2\right)^{1/2}.
  \end{equation*}
  We take $a_k = \abs{L_{ik}}$ and $b_k = \abs{L_{jk}}$. By Step 1, the two sums on the right are $A_{ii}$ and $A_{jj}$. So
  \begin{equation*}
    \sum_k\abs{L_{ik}}\abs{L_{jk}}
    \le \left(\sum_k\abs{L_{ik}}^2\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_k\abs{L_{jk}}^2\right)^{1/2}
    = \sqrt{A_{ii}}\sqrt{A_{jj}}.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{Step 4: compare with the largest entry.} The geometric mean of two numbers is at most the larger one. Both $A_{ii}$ and $A_{jj}$ are entries of $A$. So
  \begin{equation*}
    \sqrt{A_{ii}A_{jj}} \le \max(A_{ii}, A_{jj}) \le \max_{k,l}\abs{A_{kl}}.
  \end{equation*}
  Steps 2--4 together give the second claim.
\end{proof}

The same chain also shows where the large entries of an s.p.d.\ matrix are. Since $A_{ij} = \sum_k L_{ik}\overline{L_{jk}}$, we have $\abs{A_{ij}} \le \sum_k\abs{L_{ik}}\abs{L_{jk}} \le \sqrt{A_{ii}}\sqrt{A_{jj}}$. So the largest entry of $A$ is on its diagonal: $\max_{i,j}\abs{A_{ij}} = \max_i A_{ii}$.

Here $\max_{k,l}\abs{A_{kl}} = \norm{\operatorname{vec}(A)}_\infty$ is the largest entry of $A$. (The vector $\operatorname{vec}(A) \in \K^{m^2}$ stacks the columns of $A$.) It is \emph{not} the induced $\infty$-norm (maximum row sum) of \Cref{prop:one-inf-norm}. In words: the growth factor of Cholesky is $1$. We put this into the backward error bound and get the main result.

\begin{theorem}[Backward Stability of Cholesky]
  \label{thm:chol-stable}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ be s.p.d. Suppose Cholesky runs to completion in floating-point arithmetic and produces $\tilde{L}$. Then, to first order in $\epsm$,
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{L}\tilde{L}^\ast = A + \delta A, \qquad \abs{\delta A_{ij}} \le cm\epsm\max_{k,l}\abs{A_{kl}}
  \end{equation}
  for a modest constant $c$. That is, Cholesky without pivoting is backward stable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  The analysis of \Cref{thm:lu-backward} applies with $\tilde{U} = \tilde{L}^\ast$ and gives $\abs{\delta A} \le cm\epsm\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{L}^\ast}$~\cite[Theorem 10.3]{Higham}. Strictly speaking, \Cref{prop:chol-growth} is about the exact factor. The computed factor satisfies $\sum_k\abs{\tilde{L}_{ik}}^2 = A_{ii} + \delta A_{ii}$. This differs by a term of order $\epsm$, which changes the bound only at second order. Hence $\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{L}^\ast} \le \max_{k,l}\abs{A_{kl}}$ to first order, as desired.
\end{proof}

So \Cref{thm:chol-exists,thm:chol-stable} answer two different questions. The first says that the algorithm runs to completion in exact arithmetic. The second says that in floating point its answer is accurate. In LU, we need pivoting to fix both problems. For s.p.d.\ matrices, both hold automatically. (In floating point, completion itself is guaranteed as long as $\kappa_2(A)$ is not too close to $1/\epsm$~\cite[Theorem 10.7]{Higham}.)

\clearpage
\paragraph{Symmetric indefinite matrices.} What if $A$ is Hermitian but not positive definite? A Hermitian matrix is \emph{indefinite} if $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x}$ takes both positive and negative values. Equivalently, it has both positive and negative eigenvalues. For such a matrix, pivots can be zero or negative, and Cholesky fails. Instead, we compute
\begin{equation}
  PAP^\T = LDL^\T,
\end{equation}
where $P$ is a permutation and $D$ is block diagonal with $1 \times 1$ and $2 \times 2$ blocks ($LDL^\ast$ in the complex case).

Why do we use the symmetric permutation $PAP^\T$, and not $PA$ as in LU?
\begin{itemize}
  \item The product $PAP^\T$ swaps rows and columns in the same way. So it keeps the matrix symmetric, and we can still work on one half.
  \item $PA$ swaps only rows. This destroys symmetry.
\end{itemize}
But a symmetric permutation has a limit. It only moves diagonal entries to other diagonal positions. It can never bring an off-diagonal entry onto the diagonal. For example, consider
\begin{equation}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its diagonal is zero, so every $1 \times 1$ pivot is zero no matter how we permute. The solution is to take the whole $2 \times 2$ block as one pivot. This block is invertible, so it becomes one block of $D$, with $L = I$.

In general, each step moves an invertible $1 \times 1$ or $2 \times 2$ block $E$ to the top left. Then it factors
\begin{equation}
  PAP^\T = \begin{bmatrix} E & C^\ast \\ C & B \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ CE^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} E & 0 \\ 0 & B - CE^{-1}C^\ast \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} I & E^{-1}C^\ast \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
and repeats on the Schur complement $B - CE^{-1}C^\ast$, just as in the derivation of Cholesky. In practice, we choose the blocks by \emph{Bunch--Kaufman pivoting} (LAPACK \texttt{sytrf}). If a diagonal entry is too small compared with the off-diagonal entries in its column, it uses a $2 \times 2$ block instead. This avoids small pivots. Its stability is about the same as LU with partial pivoting. It costs about $\frac{1}{3}m^3$ flops.

\section{Schur Complements}
\label{sec:schur}

So far we eliminated one row and column at a time. Let us now do the same thing with blocks. This leads to the Schur complement. It appears everywhere in numerical linear algebra (and also in statistics and optimization).

\paragraph{Block Cholesky.} We partition $A = LL^\ast$ into $2 \times 2$ blocks. Here $L_{11}$ and $L_{22}$ are square and lower triangular:
\begin{equation}
  L = \begin{bmatrix} L_{11} & 0 \\ L_{21} & L_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  LL^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} L_{11}L_{11}^\ast & L_{11}L_{21}^\ast \\ L_{21}L_{11}^\ast & L_{21}L_{21}^\ast + L_{22}L_{22}^\ast \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Comparing blocks, we have
\begin{equation}
  L_{11}L_{11}^\ast = A_{11}, \qquad L_{21}L_{11}^\ast = A_{21}, \qquad L_{21}L_{21}^\ast + L_{22}L_{22}^\ast = A_{22}.
\end{equation}
The top right block gives the conjugate transpose of the second equation, so it gives nothing new.

We want to find $L_{22}L_{22}^\ast$. The second equation gives
\begin{equation*}
  L_{21} = A_{21}L_{11}^{-\ast}.
\end{equation*}
Using $A_{21}^\ast = A_{12}$, we get
\begin{equation}
  L_{21}L_{21}^\ast = A_{21}L_{11}^{-\ast}L_{11}^{-1}A_{21}^\ast = A_{21}(L_{11}L_{11}^\ast)^{-1}A_{21}^\ast = A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}.
\end{equation}
We put this into the third equation and get
\begin{equation}
  L_{22}L_{22}^\ast = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}.
\end{equation}
The matrix on the right has a name.

\begin{definition}[Schur Complement]
  Let $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with $A_{11}$ square and invertible. The \emph{Schur complement} of $A_{11}$ in $A$ is
  \begin{equation}
    A/A_{11} \defeq A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

The Schur complement $S$ from Step 1 of \Cref{sec:chol-derive} is the special case where $A_{11} = \alpha$ is $1 \times 1$. Then $A_{11}^{-1}$ is just division by a scalar.

Note that the Schur complement is \emph{defined} as $A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}$. The block equations show that it is \emph{equal} to $L_{22}L_{22}^\ast$. This tells us two things.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $L_{22}$ is the Cholesky factor of the Schur complement. So the algorithm can recurse on blocks instead of single columns.
  \item The Schur complement $A/A_{11}$ is s.p.d.: for $\vec{y} \ne \zerovec$, let $\vec{x} = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} -A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}\vec{y} \\ \vec{y} \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Then $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x} = \vec{y}^\ast (A/A_{11})\vec{y} > 0$. This gives a second proof that the recursion never breaks down. (The first proof was the congruence argument.)
\end{enumerate}

The Schur complement is also exactly what the column-by-column elimination of \Cref{sec:chol-exists} computes. Let $A_{11}$ be the leading $k \times k$ block. Then after $k$ steps of symmetric elimination,
\begin{equation}
  L_k \cdots L_1AL_1^\ast \cdots L_k^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} \diag(d_1, \dots, d_k) & 0 \\ 0 & A/A_{11} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, both come from eliminating the first $k$ rows and columns of $A$. The block equations above show that what is left in the lower right is $L_{22}L_{22}^\ast = A/A_{11}$.

In other words, \emph{$k$ steps of elimination produce the Schur complement of the leading $k \times k$ block.}

\paragraph{The inverse and the Schur complement.} The following lemma is the key tool for the pivot bound. It relates the Schur complement to a block of the inverse.

\begin{lemma}[Blocks of the Inverse]
  \label{lem:schur-inverse}
  Let $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ be invertible with $A_{11}$ invertible, let $S = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}$ be invertible, and partition $\Theta \defeq A^{-1}$ in the same way. Then
  \begin{equation}
    \Theta_{22} = S^{-1}.
  \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The idea is to compute a block LU factorization of $A$ and invert it. Block elimination gives
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ A_{21}A_{11}^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & 0 \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} I & A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  (Multiply it out to check.) To invert a unit block triangular matrix, we negate its off-diagonal block. So
  \begin{equation}
    A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} I & -A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & S^{-1} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -A_{21}A_{11}^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  To read off the lower right block of the product, we only need two pieces:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item the second block row of the left factor, which is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \end{bmatrix}$;
    \item the second block column of the right factor, which is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \end{bmatrix}^\T$.
  \end{itemize}
  So the lower right block of $A^{-1}$ is
  \begin{equation*}
    \begin{bmatrix} 0 & I \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} A_{11}^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & S^{-1} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ I \end{bmatrix} = S^{-1}.
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

The proof uses only that $A_{11}$ and $S$ are invertible. It needs neither symmetry nor definiteness.

In words: \emph{to get the Schur complement, invert, take the submatrix, and invert again}:
\begin{equation}
  A/A_{11} = \left((A^{-1})_{22}\right)^{-1}.
\end{equation}

Watch the inverse here. It is easy to remember the lemma wrongly as $\Theta_{22} = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}$. But the block of the inverse is $S^{-1}$, not $S$. The slogan ``invert, take submatrix, invert'' helps us remember the correct version.

\paragraph{Proof of the pivot bound.} We can now finish the proof of \Cref{thm:chol-exists}. The plan has two parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The $k$-th pivot is a diagonal entry of a Schur complement $S'$, so it is at least $\lambda_{\min}(S')$.
  \item By \Cref{lem:schur-inverse}, $(S')^{-1}$ is a block of $A^{-1}$. This gives $\lambda_{\min}(S') \ge \lambda_{\min}(A)$.
\end{itemize}
Both parts use the Rayleigh quotient bound: for Hermitian $H$ and $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rayleigh-bound}
  \lambda_{\min}(H) \le \frac{\vec{x}^\ast H\vec{x}}{\vec{x}^\ast\vec{x}} \le \lambda_{\max}(H).
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \eqref{eq:pivot-bound}]
  Fix $k$. Let $S'$ be the Schur complement of the leading $(k - 1) \times (k - 1)$ block of $A$ (for $k = 1$, $S' = A$). As shown above, $S'$ is the matrix left over after $k - 1$ steps of elimination, and it is s.p.d. So the $k$-th pivot is its top left entry, and by \eqref{eq:rayleigh-bound} with $\vec{x} = \vec{e}_1$,
  \begin{equation}
    d_k = \vec{e}_1^\ast S'\vec{e}_1 \ge \lambda_{\min}(S').
  \end{equation}

  It remains to show $\lambda_{\min}(S') \ge \lambda_{\min}(A)$. Let $\Theta = A^{-1}$. It is s.p.d., with eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i(A)$, so $\lambda_{\max}(\Theta) = 1/\lambda_{\min}(A)$. By \Cref{lem:schur-inverse}, $(S')^{-1} = \Theta_{22}$, the lower right block of $\Theta$.

  Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $S'$ with eigenvector $\vec{y}$. Then $\lambda > 0$ and $(S')^{-1}\vec{y} = \lambda^{-1}\vec{y}$. Pad $\vec{y}$ with zeros:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \zerovec \\ \vec{y} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \vec{x}^\ast\Theta\vec{x} = \vec{y}^\ast\Theta_{22}\vec{y}, \qquad
    \vec{x}^\ast\vec{x} = \vec{y}^\ast\vec{y}.
  \end{equation*}
  So by \eqref{eq:rayleigh-bound} applied to $\Theta$,
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{\lambda}
    = \frac{\vec{y}^\ast(S')^{-1}\vec{y}}{\vec{y}^\ast\vec{y}}
    = \frac{\vec{x}^\ast\Theta\vec{x}}{\vec{x}^\ast\vec{x}}
    \le \lambda_{\max}(\Theta)
    = \frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}.
  \end{equation*}
  Hence $\lambda \ge \lambda_{\min}(A)$ for every eigenvalue of $S'$.

  Putting the two parts together,
  \begin{equation}
    d_k \ge \lambda_{\min}(S') \ge \lambda_{\min}(A) = \frac{1}{\norm{A^{-1}}_2},
  \end{equation}
  where the last equality holds because the singular values of an s.p.d.\ matrix are its eigenvalues (\Cref{thm:spectral}).
\end{proof}

The same argument with $\lambda_{\min}(\Theta)$ gives $\lambda \le \lambda_{\max}(A)$. So every eigenvalue of a Schur complement of an s.p.d.\ matrix lies in $[\lambda_{\min}(A), \lambda_{\max}(A)]$.

\section{Cholesky and QR}
\label{sec:chol-qr}

We end with a link back to least squares. The Cholesky factorization and the QR factorization are two views of the same thing. Let $B \in \K^{m \times n}$ have full column rank. Then $B^\ast B$ is s.p.d.\ (\Cref{thm:gram}). If $B = QR$, then
\begin{equation}
  B^\ast B = R^\ast Q^\ast QR = R^\ast R.
\end{equation}
So the $R$ of QR is a Cholesky factor of $B^\ast B$. The next theorem states this exactly, and also gives the converse.

\begin{theorem}[QR of $B$ Is Cholesky of $B^\ast B$]
  \label{thm:chol-qr}
  Let $B \in \K^{m \times n}$ have full column rank.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $B = \hat{Q}\hat{R}$ is a reduced QR factorization with $r_{jj} > 0$, then $B^\ast B = \hat{R}^\ast\hat{R}$ is the Cholesky factorization of $B^\ast B$.
    \item Conversely, if $B^\ast B = R^\ast R$ is the Cholesky factorization, then $B = (BR^{-1})R$ is a reduced QR factorization: $BR^{-1}$ has orthonormal columns.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \emph{Item 1.} We have $B^\ast B = \hat{R}^\ast\hat{Q}^\ast\hat{Q}\hat{R} = \hat{R}^\ast\hat{R}$, and $\hat{R}^\ast$ is lower triangular with positive diagonal. By \Cref{cor:chol-unique}, this is \emph{the} Cholesky factorization.

  \emph{Item 2.} $R$ is invertible because its diagonal is positive. So
  \begin{equation}
    (BR^{-1})^\ast(BR^{-1}) = R^{-\ast}B^\ast BR^{-1} = R^{-\ast}R^\ast RR^{-1} = I,
  \end{equation}
  as desired.
\end{proof}

So QR of $B$ and Cholesky of $B^\ast B$ compute the same triangular factor. This has three consequences.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Uniqueness of QR.} The uniqueness of Cholesky (\Cref{cor:chol-unique}) gives the uniqueness of reduced QR with $r_{jj} > 0$ (\Cref{thm:qr-unique}). Indeed, $\hat{R}$ must be the Cholesky factor of $B^\ast B$, and then $\hat{Q} = B\hat{R}^{-1}$.
  \item \emph{Least squares.} Solving the normal equations with Cholesky computes the same $R$ as QR. But it goes through $B^\ast B$, whose condition number is $\kappa_2(B)^2$. The two methods agree in exact arithmetic, but not in floating point.
  \item \emph{CholeskyQR.} Item 2 is itself an algorithm: form $B^\ast B$, factor it, and set $\hat{Q} = BR^{-1}$. It is fast, and it is mostly matrix multiplications. But for the same reason (it goes through $B^\ast B$), the computed $\hat{Q}$ loses orthogonality like $\epsm\kappa_2(B)^2$. This is worse than modified Gram--Schmidt.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Over $\R$, ``Positive Definite'' Does Not Imply Symmetric}

If $A = B^\ast B$ with $B$ real, then $A$ is automatically symmetric. But suppose we only ask that $\vec{x}^\T A\vec{x} > 0$ for all real $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$. Then $A$ need not be symmetric. For example, $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has $\vec{x}^\T A\vec{x} = x_1^2 + x_2^2$.

The reason is this. Every real square matrix is the sum of a symmetric part and a skew-symmetric part $K$. Since $\vec{x}^\T K\vec{x} = 0$, the quadratic form only sees the symmetric part. This is why, over $\R$, symmetry is stated as a separate condition in ``s.p.d.''.

Over $\C$, symmetry is automatic. Suppose $\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x}$ is real for all complex $\vec{x}$. Then $\vec{x}^\ast(A - A^\ast)\vec{x} = 0$ for all $\vec{x}$. Substituting $\vec{x} = \vec{e}_j + \vec{e}_k$ and $\vec{x} = \vec{e}_j + i\vec{e}_k$ (polarization) gives $A = A^\ast$. The example above, at $\vec{x} = (1, i)^\T$, gives $2 + 2i$. So it is not positive definite over $\C$.

\subsection{Cholesky as a Test for Positive Definiteness}

Congruences by unit lower triangular matrices do not change the determinants of the leading principal submatrices. So $\det A[1{:}k, 1{:}k] = d_1 \cdots d_k$. Hence the pivots are ratios of consecutive leading principal minors:
\begin{equation}
  d_k = \frac{\det A[1{:}k, 1{:}k]}{\det A[1{:}k-1, 1{:}k-1]}.
\end{equation}
Combined with Sylvester's criterion (a Hermitian matrix is positive definite if and only if all its leading principal minors are positive), this gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    A \text{ is positive definite} &\iff \text{all leading principal minors are} > 0 \\
    &\iff \text{all pivots are} > 0 \iff \text{Cholesky completes}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
So Cholesky is the standard test for positive definiteness. It is much cheaper than computing eigenvalues. For example, \texttt{numpy.linalg.cholesky} raises \texttt{LinAlgError} exactly at this point when the input is not positive definite.

\subsection{Blocked Cholesky and Hardware}

Libraries (LAPACK and cuSOLVER \texttt{potrf}) use a blocked version, as for LU in \Cref{sec:blocked-lu}. We take $A_{11}$ to be a block of size, say, $256 \times 256$, and factor it. Then:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $L_{21} = A_{21}L_{11}^{-\ast}$ is a triangular solve with many right-hand sides (TRSM);
  \item the Schur complement update $A_{22} - L_{21}L_{21}^\ast$ is a matrix multiplication (SYRK/GEMM).
\end{itemize}
So almost all flops are Level 3 BLAS operations, and these run close to peak speed. This continues our discussion of memory access in the left- and up-looking variants.

\subsection{The Normal Equations, Again}

Backward stability of Cholesky means this: the computed $\tilde{R}$ is the exact Cholesky factor of $A^\ast A + \delta$ with a small $\delta$. But when we solve least squares through the normal equations, the matrix we factor is $A^\ast A$. So the error in $\vec{x}$ is amplified by $\kappa(A^\ast A) = \kappa(A)^2$. A stable Cholesky therefore does not make ``normal equations plus Cholesky'' a stable least squares solver. For ill-conditioned $A$, QR is still the best method (\Cref{ch:normal}).
