\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture (\Cref{ch:hessenberg-qr}) made each QR step cost $O(m^2)$ on a Hessenberg matrix and carried it out by bulge chasing, which chooses its rotations differently from the plain QR step. In this lecture we prove that both give the same $T_k$ (the implicit Q theorem), and then add shifts to make the iteration converge fast.

\section{Proof of the Implicit Q Theorem}
\label{sec:implicit-q-proof}

Recall the statement (\Cref{thm:implicit-q}). Let $Q$ be orthogonal, and let $Q^\T AQ = H$ be unreduced upper Hessenberg. Then the first column of $Q$ fixes columns $2, \dots, m$ of $Q$, up to signs.

\begin{proof}
  We follow~\cite[Theorem 4.9]{Demmel}. Let both $Q^\T AQ = H$ and $V^\T AV = G$ be as in the theorem. Assume they have the same first column, $\vec{q}_1 = \vec{v}_1$. We want to show that
  \begin{equation}
    W \defeq V^\T Q = \diag(\pm 1, \dots, \pm 1).
  \end{equation}
  Then $Q = VW$, i.e., $\vec{q}_i = \pm\vec{v}_i$ for every $i$.

  $W$ is a product of orthogonal matrices, so it is orthogonal. Its first column is $W\vec{e}_1 = V^\T\vec{q}_1 = V^\T\vec{v}_1 = \vec{e}_1$. We also have $GV^\T = V^\T AVV^\T = V^\T A$ and $AQ = QH$. With these,
  \begin{equation}
    GW = GV^\T Q = V^\T AQ = V^\T QH = WH.
  \end{equation}
  Write $\vec{w}_i$ for the $i$th column of $W$. We take column $i$ of both sides. Since $H$ is upper Hessenberg, column $i$ of $H$ is zero below row $i + 1$. So
  \begin{equation}
    G\vec{w}_i = (GW)_{:,i} = (WH)_{:,i} = \sum_{j=1}^{i+1}\vec{w}_jH_{ji}.
  \end{equation}
  Only the term $j = i + 1$ contains $\vec{w}_{i+1}$. We move the other terms to the right-hand side:
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:implicit-q-recursion}
    H_{i+1,i}\,\vec{w}_{i+1} = G\vec{w}_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i}H_{ji}\vec{w}_j.
  \end{equation}
  We show by induction that each $\vec{w}_i$ is nonzero in entries $1, \dots, i$ only:
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{w}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \times \\ \vdots \\ \times \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \\ \\ \leftarrow i \\ \\ \\ \\ \end{matrix}
  \end{equation}
  This holds for $\vec{w}_1 = \vec{e}_1$. Suppose it holds for $\vec{w}_1, \dots, \vec{w}_i$. We look at the right-hand side of \eqref{eq:implicit-q-recursion}.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $G\vec{w}_i$ is a combination of the first $i$ columns of $G$. Since $G$ is upper Hessenberg, these columns are zero below row $i + 1$.
    \item The sum is zero below row $i$, by the induction hypothesis.
  \end{itemize}
  So the right-hand side is zero below row $i + 1$. Since $H$ is unreduced, $H_{i+1,i} \ne 0$. We divide by it, and get that $\vec{w}_{i+1}$ is zero below row $i + 1$.

  So $W$ is upper triangular. $W$ is also orthogonal, so $W^{-1} = W^\T$. This matrix is upper triangular, as the inverse of an upper triangular matrix. It is also lower triangular, as the transpose of one. So $W$ is diagonal. A diagonal orthogonal matrix has $w_{ii}^2 = 1$, so $W = \diag(\pm 1, \dots, \pm 1)$.
\end{proof}

This proof works like the proof that the QR factorization is unique (\Cref{thm:qr-unique}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item We take two solutions, and look at the matrix $W$ that maps one to the other.
  \item We show that $W$ is triangular and orthogonal. Then $W$ must be diagonal, with entries of modulus one.
  \item These entries are exactly the sign choices that we cannot fix.
\end{itemize}

For $\K = \C$, the same proof works with $Q$ and $V$ unitary, and with $\T$ replaced by $\ast$. Then $W$ is a unitary diagonal matrix. So each column of $Q$ is fixed up to a factor of modulus one.

\section{Application to Bulge Chasing}
\label{sec:implicit-q-bulge}

Both the two-sweep algorithm and bulge chasing compute $T_k = U_k^\T T_{k-1}U_k$. In both, $U_k$ is a product of rotations $G_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}$, where $G_j$ acts on rows $j$ and $j+1$. We check the conditions of \Cref{thm:implicit-q}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Same first column.} Only $G_1$ touches the first row, so $U_k\vec{e}_1 = G_1\vec{e}_1$ in both cases. Also, $G_1$ is the same in both, because only the first column of $T_{k-1}$ decides it.
  \item \emph{Hessenberg form.} Both results are upper Hessenberg.
\end{itemize}
Assume $T_k$ is unreduced (if not, we deflate). Then \Cref{thm:implicit-q} says that the two $U_k$ agree up to the signs of their columns. So the two $T_k$ agree up to a similarity $D^\T T_kD$ with $D = \diag(\pm 1, \dots, \pm 1)$. This similarity only flips the signs of some rows and columns. As in \Cref{sec:basic-qr}, it does not change the diagonal or the moduli of the entries.

\section{The Shifted QR Iteration}
\label{sec:shifted-qr}

The QR iteration of \Cref{alg:basic-qr} can converge slowly. Its rate is $\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}$ (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}). As for inverse iteration (\Cref{alg:inverse}), the fix is a shift. Here are the two iterations side by side:
\begin{equation}
  \text{normal QR:}\quad
  \begin{aligned}
    T_{k-1} &= U_kR_k, \\
    T_k &= R_kU_k,
  \end{aligned}
  \qquad\qquad
  \text{shifted QR:}\quad
  \begin{aligned}
    T_{k-1} - \mu_kI &= U_kR_k, \\
    T_k &= R_kU_k + \mu_kI.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We subtract the shift $\mu_k$ before the factorization, and add it back afterwards. We check the two properties that made the unshifted iteration work.

\paragraph{The upper Hessenberg form is kept.} Subtracting and adding $\mu_kI$ changes only the diagonal. So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ is upper Hessenberg, and its QR factorization takes $m - 1$ Givens rotations;
  \item $R_kU_k$ is upper Hessenberg, by \Cref{sec:qr-hessenberg}.
\end{itemize}
Each step still costs $O(m^2)$.

\paragraph{The eigenvalues are kept.} The first equation gives $R_k = U_k^\ast(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)$. So
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kU_k + \mu_kI = U_k^\ast(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)U_k + \mu_kI = U_k^\ast T_{k-1}U_k,
\end{equation}
since $U_k^\ast(\mu_kI)U_k = \mu_kI$. So $T_k$ is again a unitary similarity transformation of $T_{k-1}$. If we did not add back $\mu_kI$, every step would shift the eigenvalues by $-\mu_k$.

\paragraph{Tracking the eigenvectors.} The relation $T_k = U_k^\ast T_{k-1}U_k$ has exactly the same form as in the unshifted iteration. So we can track $Q_k$ as before (\Cref{sec:basic-qr}). After each step we update $Q \gets QU_k$. Then $T_k = (U_1\cdots U_k)^\ast H(U_1\cdots U_k)$. Together with the Hessenberg reduction, this gives
\begin{equation*}
  A \approx (QU_1\cdots U_k)\,T_k\,(QU_1\cdots U_k)^\ast
\end{equation*}
once $T_k$ is nearly triangular.

\section{Choosing the Shift}
\label{sec:choosing-shift}

How does the shift change convergence? Which $\mu_k$ should we choose? We first look at the ideal case.

\paragraph{Exact shift.} Assume $\mu_k$ is an eigenvalue of $T_{k-1}$. Then $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ is singular. Assume also that all off-diagonal entries $t_{j+1,j}$ are nonzero, i.e., $T_{k-1}$ is unreduced. (If not, we split the problem; see below.)

First, the first $m - 1$ columns of $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ are linearly independent. The reason: rows $2, \dots, m$ of these columns form an upper triangular $(m-1) \times (m-1)$ matrix. Its diagonal entries are the subdiagonal entries of $T_{k-1}$, which are nonzero.

Now look at the QR factorization $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI = U_kR_k$. Column $j$ is a combination of $\vec{u}_1, \dots, \vec{u}_j$, and the coefficient on $\vec{u}_j$ is $r_{jj}$. If $r_{jj} = 0$ for some $j \le m - 1$, then column $j$ would lie in the span of the columns before it. So $r_{11}, \dots, r_{m-1,m-1} \ne 0$. But $R_k$ is singular. So \emph{the last entry must be zero}: $r_{mm} = 0$. Since $R_k$ is upper triangular, its whole last row is zero:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{T_{k-1} - \mu_kI}_{\text{unreduced Hessenberg}} = U_k\,\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{R_k}.
\end{equation}

What happens to $T_k$? The last row of $R_kU_k$ is the last row of $R_k$ times $U_k$. So it is zero. Adding $\mu_kI$ gives
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kU_k + \mu_kI = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{T} & \vec{t} \\ 0 \;\cdots\; 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $\hat{T} \in \K^{(m-1) \times (m-1)}$ upper Hessenberg. So $t_{m,m-1} = 0$ and $t_{mm} = \mu_k$. With an exact shift, \emph{one step} of the QR iteration splits off the eigenvalue $\mu_k$.

Then we continue on $\hat{T}$ alone. A unitary $Q$ that acts on $\hat{T}$ acts on the full matrix as $\diag(Q, 1)$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} Q^\ast & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} \hat{T} & \vec{t} \\ 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} Q & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} Q^\ast\hat{T}Q & Q^\ast\vec{t} \\ 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
So the eigenvalue we already found does not change, and the problem is one size smaller. We need to update the last column $Q^\ast\vec{t}$ only if we want the full Schur form, not just the eigenvalues.

\paragraph{Splitting.} In practice $\mu_k$ is only close to an eigenvalue. So the subdiagonal entries become small, but not zero. Whenever a subdiagonal entry gets close to $0$, we set it to zero. Then $T_k$ is (approximately) block upper triangular:
\begin{equation}
  T_k \approx \begin{bmatrix} (T_k)_{11} & (T_k)_{12} \\ 0 & (T_k)_{22} \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
Its eigenvalues are the eigenvalues of the two diagonal blocks. Both blocks are again upper Hessenberg. We recurse on $(T_k)_{11}$ and $(T_k)_{22}$ separately.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Practical QR iteration.}
  \label{alg:practical-qr}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$
    \State compute $H = Q^\ast AQ$ \Comment{\Cref{alg:hessenberg}, once}
    \State $T_0 \gets H$, $k \gets 0$
    \While{not converged}
      \State $k \gets k + 1$, choose $\mu_k$
      \State $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI = U_kR_k$ \Comment{Givens QR, $O(m^2)$}
      \State $T_k \gets R_kU_k + \mu_kI$
      \If{some subdiagonal entry of $T_k$ is $\approx 0$}
        \State split $T_k$ into $(T_k)_{11}$ and $(T_k)_{22}$ and recurse on both
      \EndIf
    \EndWhile
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Which eigenvalue to aim for?} We want to choose $\mu_k$ close to an eigenvalue, and split the problem wherever a subdiagonal entry is $\approx 0$. But close to which eigenvalue? We choose $\mu_k \approx \lambda_m$, for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The shift helps the eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ to separate if $\abs{\lambda_i - \mu} \ll \abs{\lambda_j - \mu}$ for all $j \ne i$. Without a shift ($\mu = 0$), this is easiest when $\lambda_i$ itself is small. That is the eigenvalue of smallest modulus.
  \item The QR iteration sorts the eigenvalues by size along the diagonal of $T_k$ (\Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur}). The smallest one, $\lambda_m$, is at the bottom right. A shift close to another eigenvalue would have to move that eigenvalue to the bottom. And \emph{reordering the eigenvalues slows down convergence}.
\end{enumerate}
So the shift should be close to the eigenvalue that is already converging in the bottom right corner. There we then check whether $t_{m,m-1} \approx 0$.

\section{Relationship with (Inverse) Power Iteration}
\label{sec:shifted-orth-iter}

We have seen that the QR iteration is the same as orthogonal iteration with $Q_0 = I$ (\Cref{sec:basic-qr}). In the same way, the shifted QR iteration is the same as a \emph{shifted} orthogonal iteration. Start with $T_0 = H$. The first two steps are
\begin{equation*}
  H - \mu_1I = U_1R_1, \qquad T_1 = R_1U_1 + \mu_1I, \qquad T_1 - \mu_2I = U_2R_2.
\end{equation*}
So
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    U_1U_2R_2R_1 &= U_1(T_1 - \mu_2I)R_1 = U_1(R_1U_1 + \mu_1I - \mu_2I)R_1 \\
    &= (H - \mu_1I)\bigl((H - \mu_1I) + \mu_1I - \mu_2I\bigr) \\
    &= (H - \mu_1I)(H - \mu_2I),
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In the second line we used $U_1R_1 = H - \mu_1I$ twice:
\begin{equation*}
  U_1R_1U_1R_1 = (H - \mu_1I)^2, \qquad U_1(\mu_1 - \mu_2)R_1 = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)(H - \mu_1I).
\end{equation*}

Let $\bar{Q}_k \defeq U_1\cdots U_k$ and $\bar{R}_k \defeq R_k\cdots R_1$. In the same way we get
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shifted-qr-poly}
  (H - \mu_kI)\cdots(H - \mu_1I) = \bar{Q}_k\bar{R}_k.
\end{equation}
We prove this by induction. By the similarity relation of \Cref{sec:shifted-qr}, $T_{k-1} = \bar{Q}_{k-1}^\ast H\bar{Q}_{k-1}$. So
\begin{equation}
  \bar{Q}_k\bar{R}_k = \bar{Q}_{k-1}(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)\bar{R}_{k-1} = (H - \mu_kI)\bar{Q}_{k-1}\bar{R}_{k-1}.
\end{equation}
$\bar{Q}_k$ is unitary. $\bar{R}_k$ is upper triangular, as a product of upper triangular matrices. So \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-poly} is a QR factorization of the matrix polynomial
\begin{equation*}
  p_k(H) = (H - \mu_kI)\cdots(H - \mu_1I).
\end{equation*}
The factors commute, so their order does not matter.

Compare this with the unshifted case. There, $\bar{Q}_k$ is the Q factor of $H^k$. Now the power $H^k$ is replaced by $p_k(H)$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H^k$ multiplies the eigenvector component for $\lambda_j$ by $\lambda_j^k$.
  \item $p_k(H)$ multiplies it by $p_k(\lambda_j) = \prod_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_j - \mu_i)$.
\end{itemize}
If the shifts are close to an eigenvalue, $p_k$ is tiny there. This is what makes convergence faster. Also, $T_k = \bar{Q}_k^\ast H\bar{Q}_k$ shows how to track the eigenvectors. $\bar{Q}_k$ collects all the transformations. Once $T_k$ has converged to upper triangular form, $Q\bar{Q}_k$ contains the Schur vectors of $A$.

\paragraph{What about inverse iteration?} We invert \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-poly} and get $p_k(H)^{-1} = \bar{R}_k^{-1}\bar{Q}_k^\ast$. Then we take the adjoint:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shifted-qr-inverse}
  \bar{Q}_k\bar{R}_k^{-\ast} = (H - \mu_kI)^{-\ast}\cdots(H - \mu_1I)^{-\ast},
\end{equation}
where $X^{-\ast} \defeq (X^{-1})^\ast$. Inversion and the adjoint both reverse the order of a product. But the factors on the right commute, so the order does not matter. The right-hand side is exactly the operator of shifted inverse iteration (\Cref{alg:inverse}). It is applied to $H^\ast$ with shifts $\bar{\mu}_i$. ($H^\ast$ has the eigenvalues $\bar{\lambda}_j$.)

The difference from \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-poly} is the triangular factor. $\bar{R}_k^{-1}$ is upper triangular, so $\bar{R}_k^{-\ast}$ is \emph{lower} triangular.
\begin{itemize}
  \item With an upper triangular factor, the first columns of the product use only the first columns of $\bar{Q}_k$.
  \item With a lower triangular factor, the last columns of the product use only the last columns of $\bar{Q}_k$.
\end{itemize}
In particular, the last column of $\bar{R}_k^{-\ast}$ is $(\bar{R}_k^{-\ast})_{mm}\vec{e}_m$, with $(\bar{R}_k^{-\ast})_{mm} = 1/\overline{(\bar{R}_k)_{mm}}$. So multiplying \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-inverse} by $\vec{e}_m$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \bar{\vec{q}}_m = \overline{(\bar{R}_k)_{mm}}\;(H - \mu_kI)^{-\ast}\cdots(H - \mu_1I)^{-\ast}\vec{e}_m,
\end{equation}
where $\bar{\vec{q}}_m$ is the last column of $\bar{Q}_k$. So the same matrix $\bar{Q}_k$ does two iterations at once:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    factorization & triangular factor & columns of $\bar{Q}_k$ \\
    \midrule
    $p_k(H) = \bar{Q}_k\bar{R}_k$ & upper & first columns: orthogonal (power) iteration \\
    $p_k(H)^{-\ast} = \bar{Q}_k\bar{R}_k^{-\ast}$ & lower & last columns: shifted inverse iteration \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last column is shifted inverse iteration started from $\vec{e}_m$. For a single step, this is the argument of~\cite[Section 4.4.4]{Demmel}. In short, \emph{the QR iteration is both power and inverse power iteration}. This explains two things.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The bottom right corner of $T_k$ converges first.
  \item The shift should be close to $\lambda_m$ (\Cref{sec:choosing-shift}). A shift close to an eigenvalue makes inverse iteration converge fast (\Cref{thm:inverse-iter}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Rayleigh Quotient Shift}

In class we did not fix a concrete choice of $\mu_k \approx \lambda_m$. The natural estimate is the bottom right entry,
\begin{equation*}
  \mu_k = t_{mm}^{(k-1)} = \vec{e}_m^\ast T_{k-1}\vec{e}_m.
\end{equation*}
This is the Rayleigh quotient of $T_{k-1}$ at $\vec{e}_m$. So we get a link to Rayleigh quotient iteration (\Cref{alg:rqi}). By the inverse iteration view above, the last column of $\bar{Q}_k$ is inverse iteration, with the shift taken from the current Rayleigh quotient. This gives local \emph{quadratic} convergence. Demmel~\cite[Section 4.4.4]{Demmel} explains why.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Let $\eta = \norm{T_{k-1}(m, 1{:}m-1)}/\norm{A} \ll 1$.
  \item Setting the off-diagonal part of the last row to zero changes an eigenvalue by $O(\eta\norm{A})$, if the eigenvalue is well separated. So $\abs{\lambda_m - t_{mm}} = O(\eta\norm{A})$.
  \item The next step shrinks the last row by the factor $\abs{\lambda_m - \mu}/\min_{j \ne m}\abs{\lambda_j - \mu} = O(\eta)$. This gives $O(\eta^2)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Wilkinson Shift}

The Rayleigh quotient shift can get stuck. Take $T_0 = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Then $\mu_1 = t_{22} = 0$. $T_0$ is itself orthogonal, so its QR factorization is $U_1 = T_0$, $R_1 = I$. So $T_1 = R_1U_1 = T_0$. Nothing changes, because the eigenvalues $\pm 1$ are equally far from the shift.

The \emph{Wilkinson shift} uses a different $\mu_k$. Look at the last $2 \times 2$ block of $T_{k-1}$. Of its two eigenvalues, we take the one closer to $t_{mm}$. In the example this is $\pm 1$, an exact eigenvalue, so one step is enough. For symmetric tridiagonal matrices, QR iteration with the Wilkinson shift has these properties~\cite[Theorem 5.8]{Demmel}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it converges globally, and at least linearly;
  \item for almost all matrices, it converges cubically in the limit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When Is a Subdiagonal Entry $\approx 0$?}

We get $T_k$ from $A$ by unitary similarity transformations in floating point. So $T_k$ already has rounding errors of size $O(\epsm)\norm{A}$. A subdiagonal entry smaller than this might as well be zero. Setting it to zero is a backward stable perturbation~\cite[Section 4.4.5]{Demmel}. The iteration stops when all diagonal blocks are $1 \times 1$. (For a real matrix with complex conjugate pairs of eigenvalues, some blocks are $2 \times 2$.)

\subsection{Why the Implicit Q Theorem Matters for Shifts}

With a shift $\mu$, one QR step factors $T_{k-1} - \mu I = U_kR_k$. Then it sets $T_k = R_kU_k + \mu I = U_k^\ast T_{k-1}U_k$. By \Cref{thm:implicit-q}, the first column of $U_k$ fixes $U_k$. This first column is parallel to the first column of $T_{k-1} - \mu I$, which is $(t_{11} - \mu, t_{21}, 0, \dots, 0)^\T$. So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item we compute $G_1$ from these two numbers;
  \item the rest of the step is the bulge chasing of \Cref{alg:bulge-chasing} on $T_{k-1}$ itself;
  \item we never form $T_{k-1} - \mu I$.
\end{itemize}
This is the \emph{implicitly shifted} QR algorithm~\cite[Section 4.4.8]{Demmel}. A real matrix can have complex conjugate eigenvalues. Then we can combine two such steps, with shifts $\mu$ and $\bar{\mu}$, into one \emph{double-shift} step that uses only real arithmetic (the Francis step)~\cite[Section 4.4.8]{Demmel}.
