\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture (\Cref{ch:hessenberg-qr}) reduced $A$ to Hessenberg form $H = Q^\ast AQ$, ran the QR iteration on $H$ at $O(m^2)$ per step, and did each step in one sweep by bulge chasing. Now we prove that bulge chasing gives the same $T_k$, and add shifts to make the iteration converge fast.

The plan of this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Prove the implicit Q theorem, and apply it to bulge chasing (\Cref{sec:implicit-q-proof,sec:implicit-q-bulge}).
  \item The shifted QR iteration (\Cref{sec:shifted-qr}).
  \item Choosing the shift, and the practical QR iteration (\Cref{sec:choosing-shift}).
  \item The QR iteration is both power and inverse power iteration (\Cref{sec:shifted-orth-iter}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proof of the Implicit Q Theorem}
\label{sec:implicit-q-proof}

Recall the statement (\Cref{thm:implicit-q}): if $Q$ is orthogonal and $Q^\T AQ = H$ is unreduced upper Hessenberg, then the first column of $Q$ determines columns $2, \dots, m$, up to signs.

\begin{proof}
  Let both $Q^\T AQ = H$ and $V^\T AV = G$ be such, with equal first column $\vec{q}_1 = \vec{v}_1$. We want to show that
  \begin{equation}
    W \defeq V^\T Q = \diag(\pm 1, \dots, \pm 1).
  \end{equation}
  Then $Q = VW$, i.e., $\vec{q}_i = \pm\vec{v}_i$.

  $W$ is orthogonal, and $W\vec{e}_1 = V^\T\vec{q}_1 = \vec{e}_1$. Using $GV^\T = V^\T A$ and $AQ = QH$, we have
  \begin{equation}
    GW = GV^\T Q = V^\T AQ = V^\T QH = WH.
  \end{equation}
  Take column $i$, with $\vec{w}_i$ the $i$th column of $W$. Since $H$ is upper Hessenberg,
  \begin{equation}
    G\vec{w}_i = (GW)_{:,i} = (WH)_{:,i} = \sum_{j=1}^{i+1}\vec{w}_jH_{ji}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    H_{i+1,i}\,\vec{w}_{i+1} = G\vec{w}_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i}H_{ji}\vec{w}_j.
    \label{eq:implicit-q-recursion}
  \end{equation}
  By induction, $\vec{w}_i$ is zero below entry $i$:
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{w}_i = \begin{bmatrix} \times \\ \vdots \\ \times \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \\ \\ \leftarrow i \\ \\ \\ \\ \end{matrix}
  \end{equation}
  This holds for $\vec{w}_1 = \vec{e}_1$. If it holds up to $i$, then on the right of \eqref{eq:implicit-q-recursion}, $G\vec{w}_i$ is zero below row $i + 1$ (since $G$ is Hessenberg), and the sum is zero below row $i$. Since $H$ is unreduced, $H_{i+1,i} \ne 0$, so $\vec{w}_{i+1}$ is zero below row $i + 1$.

  So $W$ is upper triangular and orthogonal. Then $W^{-1} = W^\T$ is both upper and lower triangular, so $W$ is diagonal, with $w_{ii}^2 = 1$.
\end{proof}

\section{Application to Bulge Chasing}
\label{sec:implicit-q-bulge}

Both the two-sweep algorithm and bulge chasing compute $T_k = U_k^\T T_{k-1}U_k$ with $U_k = G_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Same first column.} Only $G_1$ touches row $1$, so $U_k\vec{e}_1 = G_1\vec{e}_1$. And $G_1$ is the same in both, since only the first column of $T_{k-1}$ decides it.
  \item \emph{Hessenberg form.} Both results are upper Hessenberg.
\end{itemize}
If $T_k$ is unreduced (if not, we split), \Cref{thm:implicit-q} says the two $U_k$ agree up to column signs. So the two $T_k$ agree up to $D^\T T_kD$ with $D = \diag(\pm 1)$, which changes neither the diagonal nor the moduli of the entries.

\section{The Shifted QR Iteration}
\label{sec:shifted-qr}

The QR iteration can converge slowly: its rate is $\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}$ (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}). As for inverse iteration (\Cref{alg:inverse}), the fix is a shift:
\begin{equation}
  \text{normal QR:}\quad
  \begin{aligned}
    T_{k-1} &= U_kR_k, \\
    T_k &= R_kU_k,
  \end{aligned}
  \qquad\qquad
  \text{shifted QR:}\quad
  \begin{aligned}
    T_{k-1} - \mu_kI &= U_kR_k, \\
    T_k &= R_kU_k + \mu_kI.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{It preserves upper Hessenberg form.} The shift only changes the diagonal, so $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ is Hessenberg (Givens QR, $O(m^2)$), and $R_kU_k$ is Hessenberg (\Cref{sec:qr-hessenberg}).
  \item \emph{It preserves the eigenvalues.} See below.
  \item \emph{How do we track the eigenvectors?}
\end{itemize}
From $R_k = U_k^\ast(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)$,
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kU_k + \mu_kI = U_k^\ast(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)U_k + \mu_kI = U_k^\ast T_{k-1}U_k.
\end{equation}
So $T_k$ is a unitary similarity of $T_{k-1}$. (Without adding back $\mu_kI$, every step would shift the eigenvalues by $-\mu_k$.) This is the same relation as in the unshifted case, so we can track $Q_k$ as before (\Cref{sec:basic-qr}): update $Q \gets QU_k$, starting from the $Q$ of the Hessenberg reduction.

\section{Choosing the Shift}
\label{sec:choosing-shift}

How does the shift affect convergence? Which $\mu_k$ should we choose?

\paragraph{Exact shift.} Assume $\mu_k$ is an eigenvalue. Then $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ is singular. Assume also that the off-diagonal entries $t_{j+1,j}$ are nonzero.

The first $m - 1$ columns of $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI$ are independent: rows $2, \dots, m$ of these columns form an upper triangular matrix with the nonzero $t_{j+1,j}$ on its diagonal. In $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI = U_kR_k$, column $j$ is a combination of $\vec{u}_1, \dots, \vec{u}_j$ with coefficient $r_{jj}$ on $\vec{u}_j$. So $r_{11}, \dots, r_{m-1,m-1} \ne 0$. But $R_k$ is singular, so the last entry must be zero, $r_{mm} = 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{T_{k-1} - \mu_kI}_{\text{unreduced Hessenberg}} = U_k\,\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{R_k}.
\end{equation}

Now what happens to $T_k$? The last row of $R_kU_k$ is zero, so
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kU_k + \mu_kI = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{T} & \vec{t} \\ 0 \;\cdots\; 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
With an exact shift, one step splits off the eigenvalue $\mu_k$. We do not have to worry about the last entry any more, and continue on $\hat{T}$. A unitary $Q$ acting on $\hat{T}$ acts on the full matrix as $\diag(Q, 1)$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} Q^\ast & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} \hat{T} & \vec{t} \\ 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} Q & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  = \begin{bmatrix} Q^\ast\hat{T}Q & Q^\ast\vec{t} \\ 0 & \mu_k \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Practical QR Iteration}

\paragraph{Goal.} Choose $\mu_k \approx$ an eigenvalue, and split up the problem whenever a subdiagonal entry approaches $0$:
\begin{equation}
  T_k \approx \begin{bmatrix} (T_k)_{11} & (T_k)_{12} \\ 0 & (T_k)_{22} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of $T_k$ are those of the two diagonal blocks. Both are again Hessenberg, so we recurse on $(T_k)_{11}$ and $(T_k)_{22}$.

\paragraph{Which eigenvalue?} We choose $\mu_k \approx \lambda_m$, for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We want $\abs{\lambda_i - \mu} \ll \abs{\lambda_j - \mu}$ for $j \ne i$. Without a shift, this is easiest if $\lambda_i$ is small.
  \item The eigenvalues are ordered by size on the diagonal of $T_k$ (\Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur}), with $\lambda_m$ at the bottom right. Reordering the eigenvalues slows down convergence.
\end{enumerate}
So the shift should be close to the eigenvalue already converging in the bottom right corner, and there we check $t_{m,m-1} \approx 0$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Practical QR iteration.}
  \label{alg:practical-qr}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$
    \State compute $H = Q^\ast AQ$ \Comment{\Cref{alg:hessenberg}, once}
    \State $T_0 \gets H$, $k \gets 0$
    \While{not converged}
      \State $k \gets k + 1$, choose $\mu_k$
      \State $T_{k-1} - \mu_kI = U_kR_k$ \Comment{Givens QR, $O(m^2)$}
      \State $T_k \gets R_kU_k + \mu_kI$
      \If{some subdiagonal entry of $T_k$ is $\approx 0$}
        \State split $T_k$ into $(T_k)_{11}$ and $(T_k)_{22}$ and recurse on both
      \EndIf
    \EndWhile
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Relationship with (Inverse) Power Iteration}
\label{sec:shifted-orth-iter}

We have seen that the QR iteration is orthogonal iteration with $Q_0 = I$ (\Cref{sec:basic-qr}). So the shifted QR iteration is a \emph{shifted} orthogonal iteration. Start with $T_0 = H$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    U_1U_2R_2R_1 &= U_1(T_1 - \mu_2I)R_1 = U_1(R_1U_1 + \mu_1I - \mu_2I)R_1 \\
    &= (H - \mu_1I)\bigl((H - \mu_1I) + \mu_1I - \mu_2I\bigr) \\
    &= (H - \mu_1I)(H - \mu_2I),
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
using $U_1R_1 = H - \mu_1I$ twice in the second line. With $\underline{Q}_k \defeq U_1\cdots U_k$ and $\underline{R}_k \defeq R_k\cdots R_1$, we can write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shifted-qr-poly}
  (H - \mu_kI)\cdots(H - \mu_1I) = \underline{Q}_k\underline{R}_k.
\end{equation}
By induction, using $T_{k-1} = \underline{Q}_{k-1}^\ast H\underline{Q}_{k-1}$,
\begin{equation}
  \underline{Q}_k\underline{R}_k = \underline{Q}_{k-1}(T_{k-1} - \mu_kI)\underline{R}_{k-1} = (H - \mu_kI)\underline{Q}_{k-1}\underline{R}_{k-1}.
\end{equation}
So \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-poly} is a QR factorization of the polynomial $p_k(H) = (H - \mu_kI)\cdots(H - \mu_1I)$. In the unshifted case, $\underline{Q}_k$ is the Q factor of $H^k$, which scales the eigenvector component for $\lambda_j$ by $\lambda_j^k$. Now it is scaled by $p_k(\lambda_j) = \prod_i(\lambda_j - \mu_i)$, which is tiny if the shifts are close to $\lambda_j$.

\paragraph{What about inverse iteration?} Invert \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-poly} and take the adjoint:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:shifted-qr-inverse}
  \underline{Q}_k\underline{R}_k^{-\ast} = (H - \mu_kI)^{-\ast}\cdots(H - \mu_1I)^{-\ast},
\end{equation}
with $X^{-\ast} \defeq (X^{-1})^\ast$. The factors commute, so their order does not matter. The right side is shifted inverse iteration (\Cref{alg:inverse}) on $H^\ast$, with shifts $\bar{\mu}_i$.

Now $\underline{R}_k^{-\ast}$ is \emph{lower} triangular. So its last column is $(\underline{R}_k^{-\ast})_{mm}\vec{e}_m$, and multiplying \eqref{eq:shifted-qr-inverse} by $\vec{e}_m$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \underline{\vec{q}}_m = \overline{(\underline{R}_k)_{mm}}\;(H - \mu_kI)^{-\ast}\cdots(H - \mu_1I)^{-\ast}\vec{e}_m,
\end{equation}
where $\underline{\vec{q}}_m$ is the last column of $\underline{Q}_k$. So the same $\underline{Q}_k$ does two iterations at once:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    factorization & triangular factor & columns of $\underline{Q}_k$ \\
    \midrule
    $p_k(H) = \underline{Q}_k\underline{R}_k$ & upper & first columns: orthogonal (power) iteration \\
    $p_k(H)^{-\ast} = \underline{Q}_k\underline{R}_k^{-\ast}$ & lower & last columns: shifted inverse iteration \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{center}
\emph{The QR iteration is both power and inverse power iteration.} This is why the bottom right corner of $T_k$ converges first, and why the shift should be close to $\lambda_m$: a shift close to an eigenvalue makes inverse iteration converge fast (\Cref{thm:inverse-iter}). For a single step, see~\cite[Section 4.4.4]{Demmel}.

\subsection{Comparison with the Uniqueness of QR}

The proof of \Cref{thm:implicit-q} works like the proof that the QR factorization is unique (\Cref{thm:qr-unique}): take two solutions, look at the matrix $W$ that maps one to the other, show that it is triangular and orthogonal, hence diagonal with entries of modulus one. These entries are the sign choices we cannot fix.

For $\K = \C$, the same proof works with $Q, V$ unitary and $\T$ replaced by $\ast$. Then each column of $Q$ is fixed up to a factor of modulus one.

\subsection{The Rayleigh Quotient Shift}
\label{sec:rq-shift-supp}

A natural estimate of $\lambda_m$ is the bottom right entry,
\begin{equation*}
  \mu_k = t_{mm}^{(k-1)} = \vec{e}_m^\ast T_{k-1}\vec{e}_m,
\end{equation*}
the Rayleigh quotient of $T_{k-1}$ at $\vec{e}_m$. By the inverse iteration view, the last column of $\underline{Q}_k$ then does Rayleigh quotient iteration (\Cref{alg:rqi}). This gives local \emph{quadratic} convergence~\cite[Section 4.4.4]{Demmel}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Let $\eta = \norm{T_{k-1}(m, 1{:}m-1)}/\norm{A} \ll 1$.
  \item Setting the off-diagonal part of the last row to zero changes a well separated eigenvalue by $O(\eta\norm{A})$. So $\abs{\lambda_m - t_{mm}} = O(\eta\norm{A})$.
  \item The next step shrinks the last row by the factor $\abs{\lambda_m - \mu}/\min_{j \ne m}\abs{\lambda_j - \mu} = O(\eta)$, giving $O(\eta^2)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Wilkinson Shift}
\label{sec:wilkinson-supp}

The Rayleigh quotient shift can get stuck. Take $T_0 = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Then $\mu_1 = t_{22} = 0$. $T_0$ is orthogonal, so $U_1 = T_0$, $R_1 = I$, and $T_1 = T_0$. Nothing changes, because the eigenvalues $\pm 1$ are equally far from the shift.

The \emph{Wilkinson shift} takes the eigenvalue of the last $2 \times 2$ block of $T_{k-1}$ that is closer to $t_{mm}$. In the example this is $\pm 1$, an exact eigenvalue, so one step is enough. For symmetric tridiagonal matrices, QR iteration with the Wilkinson shift converges globally, at least linearly, and for almost all matrices cubically in the limit~\cite[Theorem 5.8]{Demmel}.

\subsection{When Is a Subdiagonal Entry $\approx 0$?}

$T_k$ comes from $A$ by unitary similarities in floating point, so it already carries rounding errors of size $O(\epsm)\norm{A}$. A subdiagonal entry smaller than this may be set to zero; this is a backward stable perturbation~\cite[Section 4.4.5]{Demmel}. The iteration stops when all diagonal blocks are $1 \times 1$ (or $2 \times 2$ for complex conjugate pairs of a real matrix).

\subsection{Why the Implicit Q Theorem Matters for Shifts}
\label{sec:implicit-q-shifts}

With a shift, the first column of $U_k$ is parallel to the first column of $T_{k-1} - \mu I$, which is $(t_{11} - \mu, t_{21}, 0, \dots, 0)^\T$. By \Cref{thm:implicit-q}, this column fixes $U_k$. So:
\begin{itemize}
  \item we compute $G_1$ from these two numbers;
  \item the rest of the step is the bulge chasing of \Cref{alg:bulge-chasing} on $T_{k-1}$ itself;
  \item we never form $T_{k-1} - \mu I$.
\end{itemize}
This is the \emph{implicitly shifted} QR algorithm. For a real matrix with complex conjugate eigenvalues, two such steps with shifts $\mu$ and $\bar{\mu}$ combine into one \emph{double-shift} step in real arithmetic (the Francis step)~\cite[Section 4.4.8]{Demmel}.
