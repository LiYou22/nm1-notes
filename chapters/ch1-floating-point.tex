\section{Overview}

The topics of the semester are: numerical representation of vectors and matrices; solving linear systems by Gaussian elimination; solving least squares problems by orthogonalization; computing eigendecompositions; low-rank approximation; iterative methods; and structured matrices.

This lecture covers the first topic. A computer cannot store real numbers exactly: every number is rounded when it is stored, and every arithmetic operation rounds again, so even $(x + a) - a = x$ can fail. The lecture proceeds in two parts.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Representation} (\Cref{sec:repr,sec:fp-geometry,sec:cancellation}): how real numbers are stored (floating point) and what errors this causes.
  \item \emph{Error analysis} (\Cref{sec:conditioning,sec:stability}): how to tell whether an error comes from the problem (\emph{conditioning}) or from the algorithm (\emph{stability}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Vectors and Matrices}

We write $\K$ for either $\R$ or $\C$, so that every result only needs to be stated once. A \emph{vector} $\vec{v} \in \K^m$ is an $m$-tuple of elements of $\K$, representing a position or direction in an $m$-dimensional coordinate system. A \emph{matrix} $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ is an $m \times n$ array of elements of $\K$, which may be thought of as $n$ positions in an $m$-dimensional coordinate system. We identify $\K^{m \times 1} \cong \K^m$, so a column vector is a matrix with one column:
\begin{equation}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \\ A_{31} & A_{32} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{a}_1 & \vec{a}_2 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Scalar multiplication and addition act entrywise. For $A \in \K^{m \times n}$, $\vec{v} \in \K^n$, and $B \in \K^{n \times \ell}$,
\begin{equation}
  (A\vec{v})_i = \sum_{k} A_{ik} v_k, \qquad (AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj},
\end{equation}
so $A$ is a linear map $\K^n \to \K^m$ and $\K^{n \times \ell} \to \K^{m \times \ell}$: it carries a region, together with the vectors $\vec{x}$ in it, to the image vectors $A\vec{x}$.

The entrywise formula is rarely the most useful way to think. Numerical linear algebra relies much more on the \emph{column view}:
\begin{equation}
  A\vec{v} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j \vec{a}_j,
\end{equation}
that is, $A\vec{v}$ is the linear combination of the columns of $A$ with coefficients given by the entries of $\vec{v}$. This view appears throughout the course. The column space $\range(A)$ decides whether $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is solvable, the QR factorization orthogonalizes the columns, and low-rank approximation asks how few directions suffice to approximately span them. Likewise, the $j$-th column of $AB$ is $A$ applied to the $j$-th column of $B$, so a matrix--matrix product is $\ell$ matrix--vector products done at once.

\section{Representing Real Numbers}
\label{sec:repr}

To store $\vec{v} \in \K^m$ or $A \in \K^{m \times n}$ in memory, we must represent real numbers. A computer's native objects are bits, $\B \defeq \{0, 1\}$, so every number must be built from finitely many of them. We get there in steps, each fixing a shortcoming of the previous one: unsigned integers, signed integers, fixed point, and floating point.

\subsection{Unsigned and Signed Integers}

A string $d \in \B^b$, with bits $d_i \in \{0, 1\}$, represents the integers $0$ through $2^b - 1$ via
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{UInt}(d) \defeq \sum_{i=1}^{b} d_i 2^{i-1}.
\end{equation}
To represent negative numbers, we use an offset and give the highest bit a negative weight:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Int}(d) \defeq \sum_{i=1}^{b-1} d_i 2^{i-1} - d_b 2^{b-1},
\end{equation}
which covers $-2^{b-1}$ through $2^{b-1} - 1$. This is \emph{two's complement}. Nonnegative numbers keep their unsigned encoding and the adder circuit is shared with unsigned integers. The price is an asymmetric range: the most negative number, such as \texttt{typemin(Int8)} $= -128$, has no positive counterpart, so $-x$ can overflow back to $x$. (A different kind of offset, the \emph{biased} encoding that stores $x + K$, is used for floating-point exponents; see \Cref{sec:ieee}.)

\subsection{Fixed-Point Numbers}

Fixing the position of the binary point and using $f = \lfloor b/c \rfloor$ of the bits as fractional digits gives
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Fixed}(d) \defeq \operatorname{Int}(d) \cdot 2^{-f}.
\end{equation}
That is, about $1/c$ of the bits represent the fractional part (e.g., $c = 2$: half of them).

\paragraph{The problem.} Fixed-point numbers are equally spaced, with spacing $2^{-f}$ everywhere. To represent both a large number $s$ and a small number $s^{-1}$, we need about $\log_2(s)$ bits before the binary point and about as many after it. So the number of bits grows \emph{linearly} with the number of orders of magnitude we want to cover. Physical simulations routinely span a dozen orders of magnitude, so fixed point quickly becomes impractical.

\subsection{Floating-Point Numbers}

The idea of floating point is to store the order of magnitude separately as an exponent. Given $(p, e) \in \B^{b_p} \times \B^{b_e}$, define
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Float}(p, e) \defeq (-1)^{p_1} \left(1 + \sum_{i=2}^{b_p} p_i 2^{-(i-1)}\right) 2^{\operatorname{Int}(e)}.
\end{equation}
The three parts are the sign bit $p_1$, the \emph{significand} (or \emph{mantissa}) in $[1, 2)$, and the power of two. The leading $1$ of the significand is \emph{implicit} and takes no storage. The decimal analogue is $\pm 1.123 \cdot 10^{-4}$.

This solves the problem of fixed point: to cover numbers from $s^{-1}$ to $s$, the exponent only has to reach $\pm\log_2(s)$, which takes about $\log_2(\log_2(s))$ bits. Fixed point needs about $\log_2(s)$ bits for the same range. So floating point covers huge ranges cheaply, which is why scientific computing uses it. A complex number is stored as two floating-point numbers. For details, the lecture recommends \cite{Overton}.

\paragraph{In memory.} The picture below shows how the bits are laid out, for single precision (Float32, $b_p = 24$, $b_e = 8$).

\section{The Geometry of Floating-Point Numbers}
\label{sec:fp-geometry}

Plotting all floating-point numbers on the real line shows that they are \emph{not equally spaced}: within each \emph{binade} $[2^E, 2^{E+1})$ they are equally spaced, but crossing a power of two doubles the spacing: if the spacing near $1$ is $\eps$, it is $2\eps$ near $2$ and $4\eps$ near $4$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.6cm]
  \draw[-{Latex}] (0.8,0) -- (8.6,0);
  \foreach \x in {1,1.25,...,2} \draw (\x,0.12) -- (\x,-0.12);
  \foreach \x in {2,2.5,...,4} \draw (\x,0.12) -- (\x,-0.12);
  \foreach \x in {4,5,...,8} \draw (\x,0.12) -- (\x,-0.12);
  \node[below] at (1,-0.15) {$1$};
  \node[below] at (2,-0.15) {$2$};
  \node[below] at (4,-0.15) {$4$};
  \node[below] at (8,-0.15) {$8$};
  \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt}] (1,0.2) -- (1.25,0.2) node[midway,above=4pt] {$\eps$};
  \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt}] (2,0.2) -- (2.5,0.2) node[midway,above=4pt] {$2\eps$};
  \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt}] (4,0.2) -- (5,0.2) node[midway,above=4pt] {$4\eps$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Why?} Within one binade the exponent $E$ is fixed, and only the significand $1.M$ changes.
\begin{itemize}
  \item In $[1, 2)$: $1.M$ has $b_p - 1$ fraction bits, so it takes $2^{b_p - 1}$ equally spaced values, with spacing $\eps \defeq 2^{-(b_p - 1)}$.
  \item In $[2^E, 2^{E+1})$: the same values times $2^E$, so the spacing is $2^E\eps$.
\end{itemize}
Every binade holds the same number of floating-point numbers, but each is twice as long as the previous one. Hence the spacing doubles.


So the absolute spacing grows with the numbers, but the relative spacing (spacing divided by the number) always stays between $\eps/2$ and $\eps$. In other words, floating point gives every number about the same \emph{relative} accuracy. This suits real data, whose uncertainty is usually proportional to its size.

Because every result is rounded to this grid, the usual rules of arithmetic can fail.

\begin{example}[Floating-Point Addition Is Not Associative]
  \label{ex:nonassoc}
  Suppose ties are rounded up, and evaluate $(1 + \eps) + 1 + 2$ in two ways.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Left to right: $(1 + \eps) + 1 = 2 + \eps$ lies exactly halfway between $2$ and $2 + 2\eps$ and rounds up to $2 + 2\eps$. Adding $2$ gives $4 + 2\eps$, halfway between $4$ and $4 + 4\eps$, which rounds up to $4 + 4\eps$.
    \item Last two terms first: $1 + 2 = 3$ exactly, and $(1 + \eps) + 3 = 4 + \eps$ rounds down to $4$.
  \end{itemize}
  The same expression yields $4$ or $4 + 4\eps$. Multiplication is not associative either, and the distributive law fails as well; commutativity of addition and of multiplication does hold.
\end{example}

\section{Cancellation}
\label{sec:cancellation}

Relative accuracy has one weak point: subtracting two nearly equal numbers can destroy it.

\begin{example}[Cancellation Exposes Existing Error]
  \label{ex:cancel}
  Suppose the stored value of $10^6 + 1$ is already off by a relative error $\eps$, so it is $(10^6 + 1)(1 + \eps)$. Subtract $10^6$:
  \begin{equation}
    (10^6 + 1)(1 + \eps) - 10^6 = 1 + 10^6 \eps.
  \end{equation}
  The exact answer is $1$, but we get $1 + 10^6\eps$.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Before the subtraction: the error is about $10^6\eps$, on a number of size $10^6$. Relative error: $\eps$.
    \item After the subtraction: the error is still $10^6\eps$, but now on a number of size $1$. Relative error: $10^6\eps$.
  \end{itemize}
  The error itself did not grow. The result got smaller, so the same error is now a million times larger relative to it.
\end{example}

\section{Conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

How do errors \emph{propagate}? Consider computing $y = f(x)$ for $f : \R \to \R$. Since the input is rounded before anything else happens, the best we can hope for is $\hat{y} = f(\hat{x})$ with $\abs{\hat{x} - x}/\abs{x} < \eps$. Comparing relative errors,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\abs{\hat{y} - y}/\abs{y}}{\abs{\hat{x} - x}/\abs{x}} = \frac{\abs{f(\hat{x}) - f(x)}}{\abs{\hat{x} - x}} \cdot \frac{\abs{x}}{\abs{f(x)}} \xrightarrow[\eps \to 0]{} \frac{\abs{x}\abs{f'(x)}}{\abs{f(x)}}.
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Condition Number]
  \label{def:cond}
  The (relative) \emph{condition number} of $f$ at $x$ is
  \begin{equation}
    \kappa_f(x) \defeq \frac{\abs{x}\abs{f'(x)}}{\abs{f(x)}}.
  \end{equation}
  The problem is \emph{well-conditioned} at $x$ if $\kappa_f(x) \approx 1$ and \emph{ill-conditioned} if $\kappa_f(x) \gg 1$.
\end{definition}

The condition number depends only on the problem and the input, not on the algorithm. This is the most important point of the lecture: no algorithm can rescue an ill-conditioned problem, because the difficulty is intrinsic.

\begin{example}[Conditioning of Subtraction]
  Let $f(x) = x - 10^6$ at $x = 10^6 + 1$. Then
  \begin{equation}
    \kappa_f(10^6 + 1) = \frac{\abs{10^6 + 1} \cdot 1}{1} \approx 10^6 \gg 1.
  \end{equation}
  This is \Cref{ex:cancel} seen as a problem: cancellation is a conditioning phenomenon, not a defect of floating point. Even an algorithm with infinite precision turns a relative uncertainty of $10^{-16}$ in $x$ into a relative uncertainty of $10^{-10}$ in the output.
\end{example}

\section{Stability}
\label{sec:stability}

Conditioning describes the problem; stability describes the algorithm. We write $\hat{f}$ for the algorithm as it actually runs on the computer: it starts from the stored input $\hat{x} = \fl(x)$ and rounds after every operation.

\begin{definition}[Stability]
  An algorithm $\hat{f}$ for $f$ is \emph{stable} if
  \begin{equation}
    \frac{\abs{\hat{f}(\hat{x}) - y}/\abs{y}}{\abs{\hat{x} - x}/\abs{x}} \lesssim \kappa_f(x).
  \end{equation}
  The left-hand side is the relative error of the output divided by the relative error of the input. In words: the algorithm amplifies input errors no more than the problem itself does. Otherwise $\hat{f}$ is \emph{unstable}.
\end{definition}

\paragraph{The form used in practice.} The input error comes from rounding, so it is at most the unit roundoff $u = \eps/2$ (\Cref{def:eps}), and it is $0$ if $x$ is already a floating-point number, which would make the ratio undefined. Replacing the denominator by $u$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\abs{\hat{f}(\hat{x}) - y}}{\abs{y}} \lesssim \kappa_f(x)\, u.
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is the error that \emph{any} algorithm must make: rounding the input costs $u$, and the problem amplifies it by $\kappa_f(x)$. A stable algorithm adds nothing beyond that. (Here $\lesssim$ means ``at most a modest constant times''.)

\begin{example}[An Unstable Algorithm for the Identity]
  \label{ex:unstable-identity}
  Let $f(x) = x$, the identity, with $\kappa_f = 1$, and compute it as $\hat{f}(x) = (x + 10^6) - 10^6$. Mathematically $\hat{f} = f$. Take $x$ to be a floating-point number, so $\hat{x} = x$ and $y = x$.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Step 1: $x + 10^6$ is rounded, $\fl(x + 10^6) = (x + 10^6)(1 + \delta)$ with $\abs{\delta} \le u$. The low-order bits of $x$ are lost here.
    \item Step 2: subtracting $10^6$ is exact (\Cref{lem:sterbenz}).
  \end{itemize}
  Hence
  \begin{equation}
    \hat{f}(x) = x + (x + 10^6)\delta,
    \qquad
    \frac{\abs{\hat{f}(x) - x}}{\abs{x}} = \frac{\abs{x + 10^6}}{\abs{x}}\abs{\delta} \approx \frac{10^6}{\abs{x}}\abs{\delta}.
  \end{equation}
  For $x \approx 1$ the error is about $10^6 u$, but a stable algorithm must reach $\kappa_f u = u$. So $\hat{f}$ is unstable, by a factor of about $10^6$; for instance, $\hat{f}(0.1) = 0.09999999997671694$. ($x = 1$ is an exception: $10^6 + 1$ is a floating-point number, so $\delta = 0$.)

  \textbf{Why it fails.} Look at the two steps separately: $\hat{f} = g_2 \circ g_1$ with $g_1(x) = x + 10^6$ and $g_2(z) = z - 10^6$. For $x \approx 1$ and $z = x + 10^6$, their condition numbers (\Cref{def:cond}) are
  \begin{equation}
    \kappa_{g_1}(x) = \frac{\abs{x}}{\abs{x + 10^6}} \approx 10^{-6}, \qquad
    \kappa_{g_2}(z) = \frac{\abs{z}}{\abs{z - 10^6}} \approx 10^6.
  \end{equation}
  Step 1 shrinks relative errors by a factor $10^6$; step 2 is a cancellation and magnifies them by $10^6$. What happens to an error depends on where it enters:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An error already present in $x$ passes through both steps: it is shrunk, then magnified, so overall it is unchanged ($\kappa_f = 1$).
    \item The rounding error $\delta$ of step 1 enters \emph{after} the shrinking step. It passes only through step 2, so it is magnified by $10^6$.
  \end{itemize}
  \textbf{Lesson.} A well-conditioned problem can still be solved by an unstable algorithm if one of its intermediate steps is ill-conditioned. We will see this again with Gram--Schmidt and the normal equations.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Unstable vs.\ ill-conditioned.} These are different diagnoses with different fixes.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Unstable}: the algorithm is at fault. Use a different algorithm.
  \item \emph{Ill-conditioned}: the problem itself is sensitive, and no algorithm can help. Change the problem (reformulate or regularize it), or use higher precision.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Rewriting Formulas to Avoid Cancellation}

The recipe: find the step that subtracts two nearly equal numbers, and use an algebraic identity to rewrite the formula without it. Mathematically nothing changes; numerically the error drops to $O(u)$.

\paragraph{Roots of a quadratic.} The roots of $ax^2 + bx + c$ are $x_{1,2} = \bigl(-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}\bigr)/(2a)$. Suppose $b > 0$ and $b^2 \gg 4ac$. Then $\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac} \approx b$, so the ``$+$'' root computes $-b + (\text{almost } b)$: a cancellation. The ``$-$'' root adds two numbers of the same sign and is accurate. So compute that one first and get the other from Vieta's relation $x_1x_2 = c/a$:
\begin{equation}
  x_2 = \frac{-b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}, \qquad x_1 = \frac{c}{a\,x_2} = \frac{2c}{-b - \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}.
\end{equation}
(If $b < 0$, swap the roles of the two roots.)

\paragraph{Variance.} The formula $\operatorname{Var} = \overline{x^2} - \bar{x}^2$ (mean of squares minus square of mean) subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers when the data have a large mean and a small spread. For the data $10^8 + 1$, $10^8 + 2$, $10^8 + 3$ it returns $0$ in Float64, while the true variance is $2/3$; it can even return a negative number. The fixes avoid squaring the large mean:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Two passes}: first compute $\bar{x}$, then $\operatorname{Var} = \frac1n\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2$. The differences $x_i - \bar{x}$ are small, and all terms are nonnegative.
  \item \emph{Welford's recurrence}: one pass over the data, updating the mean and the sum of squared deviations as each $x_k$ arrives,
  \begin{equation}
    \bar{x}_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}, \qquad M_k = M_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k),
  \end{equation}
  and finally $\operatorname{Var} = M_n/n$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Three standard cases.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $1 - \cos x$ for small $x$: for $x = 10^{-8}$, $\cos x$ rounds to exactly $1$, giving $0$ instead of $5 \cdot 10^{-17}$. Use $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$.
  \item $\log(1 + x)$ for small $x$: already $1 + x$ loses the low bits of $x$ (as in \Cref{ex:unstable-identity}); for $x = 10^{-17}$ the result is $0$. \texttt{log1p(x)} computes $\log(1 + x)$ directly from $x$ (likewise \texttt{expm1(x)} for $e^x - 1$).
  \item $\sqrt{x + 1} - \sqrt{x}$ for large $x$: for $x = 10^{16}$ the result is $0$ instead of $5 \cdot 10^{-9}$. Rationalize: $\sqrt{x + 1} - \sqrt{x} = 1/(\sqrt{x + 1} + \sqrt{x})$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sterbenz's Lemma: Close Numbers Subtract Exactly}

\begin{lemma}[Sterbenz]
  \label{lem:sterbenz}
  If $x, y$ are floating-point numbers with $y/2 \le x \le 2y$, then $x - y$ is a floating-point number, so $\fl(x - y) = x - y$ exactly.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{Intuition.} Think of decimal numbers with three significant digits. $1.23 - 1.21 = 0.02$: both inputs lie on the grid of spacing $0.01$, so their difference does too, and it is \emph{smaller} than the inputs, so it needs fewer digits, not more. Nothing has to be rounded.

\begin{proof}
  Say $y \le x \le 2y$, and let $2^e$ be the spacing of floating-point numbers near $y$, so $y$ is an integer multiple of $2^e$ with at most $b_p$ bits. The spacing near $x \ge y$ is $2^e$ or a larger power of $2$, so $x$ is an integer multiple of $2^e$ as well. Hence $x - y = k\,2^e$ with an integer $k \ge 0$. From $x \le 2y$ we get $x - y \le y < 2^{b_p}2^e$, so $k < 2^{b_p}$ and $x - y$ fits in $b_p$ bits.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Consequence: the subtraction is not to blame.} In \Cref{ex:cancel} the subtraction itself is exact by the lemma: all of the error was already in the input, and the subtraction only made it visible. Rule: if the inputs are inexact, do not compute a small result by subtracting two large numbers.

\subsection{Useful Techniques}

\paragraph{Kahan summation.} Adding $n$ numbers one by one can make an error of order $nu\sum_i\abs{x_i}$: adding a small term to a large running sum rounds away the term's low bits. Kahan's compensated summation keeps those lost bits in a variable $c$ and feeds them back:
\begin{lstlisting}
s = 0.0; c = 0.0
for x in xs
    y = x - c        # add back what was lost last time
    t = s + y        # rounded sum: low bits of y are lost
    c = (t - s) - y  # = (what was really added) - y = the lost part
    s = t
end
\end{lstlisting}
This reduces the error to about $2u\sum_i\abs{x_i}$, essentially independent of $n$~\cite{Higham}, at about four times the cost. Example: adding $0.1$ ten million times gives an error of $1.6 \cdot 10^{-4}$ naively, $0$ (to the printed digits) with Kahan, and $1.4 \cdot 10^{-9}$ with Julia's \texttt{sum}, which adds \emph{pairwise} in a binary tree so that its error grows only like $u\log_2 n$.

\paragraph{Fused multiply-add (FMA).} $\operatorname{fma}(a, b, c)$ computes $ab + c$ with a single rounding instead of two: faster and more accurate. Since a compiler may or may not use it, the same code can give slightly different results.

\paragraph{\texttt{-ffast-math}.} This compiler flag (Julia: \texttt{@fastmath}) lets the compiler treat floating point as associative, so it may reorder sums (e.g., to vectorize them). This changes results and can break Kahan summation, whose $c = (t - s) - y$ is algebraically $0$ and may be deleted. Keep it off.

\subsection{Low-Precision Formats}

Two 16-bit formats split their bits differently. Float16 has $5$ exponent bits and $b_p = 11$: $\eps \approx 10^{-3}$, but the largest number is only $65504$. BFloat16 has $8$ exponent bits, hence the same range as Float32 (up to $3.4 \cdot 10^{38}$), but only $b_p = 8$: $\eps \approx 8 \cdot 10^{-3}$, about two decimal digits. Deep learning prefers BFloat16: gradients span many orders of magnitude, so what fails first is \emph{range} (overflow, underflow), not precision; Float16 training needs \emph{loss scaling} (multiplying the loss by a constant), BFloat16 does not. Sums are still accumulated in Float32: by \Cref{prop:dot}, a dot product of length $n = 4096$ has error up to $nu\,\abs{\vec{x}}^\T\abs{\vec{y}}$, and $nu = 16$ for BFloat16 ($u = 2^{-8}$) but $nu \approx 2.4 \cdot 10^{-4}$ for Float32 ($u = 2^{-24}$). Hence tensor cores take BFloat16 (or FP8) inputs but accumulate in Float32.
