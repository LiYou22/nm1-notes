\section{Recap: From Triangular Systems to Elimination}

We begin with a recap of \Cref{ch:performance}: matrices are stored column by column, memory access costs far more than arithmetic, and the loop order and tiling of an algorithm should match the storage. We then returned to linear systems. Triangular systems are solved by forward or back substitution in $O(m^2)$ flops, and Gaussian elimination reduces a general system to a triangular one: by \Cref{thm:elim}, applying elementary lower triangular matrices $L_k = I - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$ for $k = 1, \dots, m - 1$ gives
\begin{equation}
  U \defeq L_{m-1} \cdots L_1 A \ \text{ upper triangular}, \qquad L \defeq (L_{m-1} \cdots L_1)^{-1}, \qquad A = LU.
\end{equation}
This lecture answers the two questions left open: \emph{is $L$ lower triangular, and can we compute it cheaply?} Answering them requires a little algebra of lower triangular matrices.

\section{The Algebra of Lower Triangular Matrices}

\begin{proposition}[Closure Properties]
  \label{prop:lower-closure}
  The lower triangular matrices in $\K^{m \times m}$ are closed under
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item addition,
    \item scalar multiplication,
    \item matrix multiplication, and
    \item inversion (of invertible ones).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Items 1 and 2 are clear. For item 3, let $L$ and $\tilde{L}$ be lower triangular, so $(L\tilde{L})_{ij} = \sum_k L_{ik}\tilde{L}_{kj}$. A term $L_{ik}\tilde{L}_{kj}$ can be nonzero only if $i \ge k$ and $k \ge j$, hence $i \ge j$. So every term vanishes when $i < j$, and $L\tilde{L}$ is lower triangular. Item 4 is proved below for the matrices arising in LU, using \Cref{thm:elem-inverse}; the general case is in \Cref{sec:lu-supp}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Inverses and Products of Elementary Lower Triangular Matrices]
  \label{thm:elem-inverse}
  Let $L_k = I - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$ be as in \Cref{thm:elim}. Then:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $L_k^{-1} = I + \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$, i.e., inversion just \emph{flips the signs} of the multipliers;
    \item the product of the inverses, \emph{in this order}, places every multiplier in its own position:
    \begin{equation}
      \label{eq:L-multipliers}
      L_1^{-1} L_2^{-1} \cdots L_{m-1}^{-1} =
      \begin{bmatrix}
        1 & & & \\
        \ell_{21} & 1 & & \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \\
        \ell_{m1} & \cdots & \ell_{m,m-1} & 1
      \end{bmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Trefethen and Bau call these two facts the ``two strokes of luck'' of Gaussian elimination~\cite[Lecture 20]{TB}. The order in item 2 matters. In the reverse order the multipliers mix: for example,
\begin{equation}
  L_2^{-1}L_1^{-1} = (I + \vec{\ell}_2\vec{e}_2^{\,\ast})(I + \vec{\ell}_1\vec{e}_1^{\,\ast}) = I + \vec{\ell}_1\vec{e}_1^{\,\ast} + \vec{\ell}_2\vec{e}_2^{\,\ast} + \ell_{21}\,\vec{\ell}_2\vec{e}_1^{\,\ast},
\end{equation}
and the last term adds $\ell_{21}\ell_{i2}$ to the entries of column $1$ below row $2$.

Both items rest on one simple fact: $\vec{\ell}_k$ is zero in its first $k$ entries. The number $\vec{e}_p^{\,\ast}\vec{\ell}_k$ is entry $p$ of $\vec{\ell}_k$, so it is $0$ for every $p \le k$. This is what makes the extra terms vanish in the proof below.

\begin{proof}
  For item 1, since $\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}\vec{\ell}_k = 0$,
  \begin{equation}
    (I + \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast})(I - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}) = I + \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast} - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast} - \vec{\ell}_k(\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}\vec{\ell}_k)\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast} = I.
  \end{equation}
  For item 2, we compute the product column by column. Column $j$ of $M \defeq L_1^{-1} \cdots L_{m-1}^{-1}$ is
  \begin{equation}
    M\vec{e}_j = (I + \vec{\ell}_1\vec{e}_1^{\,\ast}) \cdots (I + \vec{\ell}_{m-1}\vec{e}_{m-1}^{\,\ast})\,\vec{e}_j.
  \end{equation}
  Here $j$ is fixed: it is the column we compute. The factors are $L_p^{-1} = I + \vec{\ell}_p\vec{e}_p^{\,\ast}$ for $p = 1, \dots, m - 1$. We apply them to $\vec{e}_j$ from right to left, using $(I + \vec{\ell}_p\vec{e}_p^{\,\ast})\vec{x} = \vec{x} + \vec{\ell}_p\,(\vec{e}_p^{\,\ast}\vec{x})$, where $\vec{e}_p^{\,\ast}\vec{x}$ is entry $p$ of $\vec{x}$.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Factors with $p > j$}: entry $p$ of $\vec{e}_j$ is $0$, so these factors leave $\vec{e}_j$ unchanged.
    \item \emph{The factor $p = j$}: entry $j$ of $\vec{e}_j$ is $1$, so it gives $\vec{e}_j + \vec{\ell}_j$.
    \item \emph{Factors with $p < j$}: entry $p$ of $\vec{e}_j + \vec{\ell}_j$ is $0$, since $\vec{e}_j$ has its $1$ in position $j$ and $\vec{\ell}_j$ is zero in its first $j$ entries. So these factors leave $\vec{e}_j + \vec{\ell}_j$ unchanged.
  \end{itemize}
  Hence column $j$ of $M$ is
  \begin{equation}
    M\vec{e}_j = \vec{e}_j + \vec{\ell}_j = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & \ell_{j+1,j} & \cdots & \ell_{mj} \end{bmatrix}^\T,
  \end{equation}
  with the $1$ in position $j$. These are exactly the columns of the matrix in item 2.
\end{proof}

\section{Answering the Two Questions}

Now we can answer the two questions from the end of \Cref{ch:performance}. Since the inverse of a product reverses the order of the factors, $L = (L_{m-1} \cdots L_1)^{-1} = L_1^{-1} L_2^{-1} \cdots L_{m-1}^{-1}$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Is $L$ lower triangular? Yes.} Each $L_k^{-1}$ is lower triangular (item 1 of \Cref{thm:elem-inverse}), and so is their product (item 3 of \Cref{prop:lower-closure}). Its diagonal is all ones: $L$ is \emph{unit} lower triangular.
  \item \emph{Can we compute $L$ cheaply? Yes, for free.} By item 2 of \Cref{thm:elem-inverse}, $L$ is the matrix \eqref{eq:L-multipliers}, whose entries below the diagonal are the multipliers $\ell_{ik}$ that elimination computes anyway.
\end{itemize}

\section{The In-Place Algorithm}

\textbf{Idea:} transform $A$ into $U$ with Gaussian elimination while recording the multipliers. Both $L$ and $U$ are stored in place, in the memory of $A$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $U$ goes on and above the diagonal;
  \item the multipliers $\ell_{ik}$ go below the diagonal, where elimination has just created zeros;
  \item the diagonal of $L$ is all ones, so it need not be stored.
\end{itemize}
Hence we can overwrite $A$ with $L$ and $U$, without any extra memory.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{LU factorization without pivoting, in place.}
  \label{alg:lu}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, m$}
      \State $A_{k+1:m,\,k} \gets A_{k+1:m,\,k} / A_{kk}$ \Comment{multipliers $\ell_{ik}$, stored in place}
      \For{$j = k + 1, \dots, m$}
        \For{$i = k + 1, \dots, m$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} - A_{ik}A_{kj}$ \Comment{$= A_{ij} - \ell_{ik}u_{kj}$}
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

When the algorithm finishes, $A$ holds both factors: $U$ on and above the diagonal, and the multipliers of $L$ below it. The inner loop runs over $i$, i.e., down a column of $A$, which is the fast direction in column-major storage (\Cref{ch:performance}).

\paragraph{Vector form.} Step $k$ computes the multipliers $\vec{\ell}_k$ and subtracts an outer product from the trailing block:
\begin{equation}
  \vec{\ell}_k = A_{k+1:m,\,k} / A_{kk}, \qquad
  A_{k+1:m,\,k+1:m} \gets A_{k+1:m,\,k+1:m} - \vec{\ell}_k\,A_{k,\,k+1:m}.
\end{equation}
A column vector times a row vector has rank one, so each step is a \emph{rank-one update} of the trailing block.

\paragraph{Block form.} Write $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} a_{11} & \vec{w}^\ast \\ \vec{v} & B \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with a scalar $a_{11}$. The first step of elimination is the factorization
\begin{equation}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \vec{v}/a_{11} & I \end{bmatrix}
      \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & \vec{w}^\ast \\ 0 & S \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad S = B - \frac{\vec{v}\vec{w}^\ast}{a_{11}}.
\end{equation}
$S$ is the \emph{Schur complement} (\Cref{ch:cholesky}). The algorithm then factors $S$ in the same way, so LU is this one step applied recursively.

\section{Zero Pivots}

The algorithm divides by $A_{kk}$, the $k$-th \emph{pivot}. If a pivot is zero, it cannot proceed.

\begin{example}[An Invertible Matrix with No LU Factorization]
  The matrix
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation}
  is invertible, but $A_{11} = 0$ is a zero pivot, so the very first step divides by zero and $A = LU$ does not exist. \emph{Invertibility does not imply the existence of an LU factorization.}
\end{example}

\begin{theorem}[Zero Pivots and Leading Principal Submatrices]
  \label{thm:lu-exists}
  If the $k$-th pivot of the LU factorization of $A$ is zero, then the leading principal submatrix $A[1{:}k, 1{:}k]$ is singular. All pivots of the LU factorization are nonzero if and only if every leading principal submatrix of $A$ is nonsingular.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  \emph{Step 1: the top-left block.} Take the top-left $k \times k$ block of $A = LU$. Entry $(i, j)$ with $i, j \le k$ is $\sum_p L_{ip}U_{pj}$, and $L_{ip} = 0$ for $p > i$, so only $p \le k$ contributes. Hence
  \begin{equation}
    A[1{:}k, 1{:}k] = L[1{:}k, 1{:}k]\,U[1{:}k, 1{:}k].
  \end{equation}
  \emph{Step 2: determinants.} $L[1{:}k, 1{:}k]$ is unit lower triangular, so its determinant is $1$. $U[1{:}k, 1{:}k]$ is upper triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal, i.e., of the first $k$ pivots:
  \begin{equation}
    \det A[1{:}k, 1{:}k] = u_{11}u_{22} \cdots u_{kk}.
  \end{equation}
  So $A[1{:}k, 1{:}k]$ is nonsingular if and only if the first $k$ pivots are nonzero.

  \emph{Step 3: a zero pivot stops the algorithm early.} If $u_{kk} = 0$, the algorithm cannot continue past step $k$. But the first $k$ rows and columns of $L$ and $U$ have already been computed, and Step 1 uses only those. So the argument still applies.
\end{proof}

\section{Small Pivots Are Also Dangerous}

Exactly zero pivots are unlikely in practice. Is it then enough that no pivot is exactly zero? Consider a small number $\eps > 0$, much smaller than the machine epsilon (e.g., $\eps = 10^{-20}$; here $\eps$ is \emph{not} the machine epsilon):
\begin{equation}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \eps^{-1} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  U = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 0 & 1 - \eps^{-1} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This is mathematically correct. In floating-point arithmetic, however, $\eps^{-1}$ is huge and $1 - \eps^{-1}$ rounds to $-\eps^{-1}$. The computed factors then give
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{L}\tilde{U} = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 1 & \eps^{-1} - \eps^{-1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which is nothing like $A$. The small pivot produces a huge multiplier, and rounding wipes out all information in $A_{22}$.

\section{Error Analysis of the LU Factorization}

\begin{theorem}[Backward Error of LU]
  \label{thm:lu-backward-preview}
  Let $\epsm$ be the machine epsilon. If the LU factorization runs to completion in floating-point arithmetic without encountering a zero pivot, the computed factors $L$, $U$ satisfy, to first order in $\epsm$,
  \begin{equation}
    A = LU + E, \qquad \abs{E} \le \epsm\, m\abs{L}\abs{U},
  \end{equation}
  where $\abs{\cdot}$ takes entrywise absolute values and the inequality holds entrywise.
\end{theorem}

The proof is given in \Cref{ch:pivoting}. The theorem says that the computed factorization is the exact factorization of a nearby matrix, with a perturbation controlled by $\abs{L}\abs{U}$. If $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ is comparable to $\abs{A}$, the error is at the level of machine precision. If $L$ or $U$ contains huge entries, the bound is useless. In the example above, $L$ contains $\eps^{-1}$ and $U$ contains $1 - \eps^{-1}$, so $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ has entries of size $\eps^{-1}$ and the error can be as large as $A$ itself.

\section{(SUPPLEMENT) Further Topics}
\label{sec:lu-supp}

\paragraph{Inverses of general lower triangular matrices.} Above, we proved item 4 of \Cref{prop:lower-closure} only for matrices of the form $(L_{m-1} \cdots L_1)^{-1}$. It holds for every invertible lower triangular $T$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\det T$ is the product of the diagonal entries, so they are all nonzero. Let $D = \diag(T)$.
  \item $N \defeq D^{-1}T$ divides row $i$ of $T$ by $T_{ii}$, so $N$ is \emph{unit} lower triangular.
  \item By item 2 of \Cref{thm:elem-inverse}, $N = L_1^{-1} \cdots L_{m-1}^{-1}$ if we take the entries of $N$ below the diagonal as the multipliers. So $N^{-1} = L_{m-1} \cdots L_1$ is a product of lower triangular matrices, hence lower triangular.
  \item $T = DN$, so $T^{-1} = N^{-1}D^{-1}$ is lower triangular as well.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A shorter proof of item 2 of \Cref{thm:elem-inverse}~\cite[Lecture 20]{TB}.} For $k < j$ we have $\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}\vec{\ell}_j = 0$, so multiplying two of the factors just adds their multipliers:
\begin{equation}
  (I + \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast})(I + \vec{\ell}_j\vec{e}_j^{\,\ast}) = I + \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast} + \vec{\ell}_j\vec{e}_j^{\,\ast}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying in the factors one at a time from the right, the same cancellation happens at every step, and we get $L_1^{-1} \cdots L_{m-1}^{-1} = I + \sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$.

\paragraph{Cost of LU.} Step $k$ of \Cref{alg:lu} updates the trailing $(m - k) \times (m - k)$ block, with one multiplication and one subtraction per entry. Adding up over all steps,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{k=1}^{m-1} 2(m - k)^2 = 2\sum_{j=1}^{m-1} j^2 \approx \frac{2}{3}m^3 \text{ flops.}
\end{equation}
The divisions for the multipliers add only $O(m^2)$. The update is a rank-one update, a Level 2 operation with low computational intensity; \Cref{sec:blocked-lu} shows how blocking turns most of it into matrix multiplication.

\paragraph{Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ with LU.} We split the solve into three steps:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  Step & Operation & Cost \\
  \midrule
  1. factor & $A = LU$ & $\tfrac{2}{3}m^3$ \\
  2. forward substitution & $L\vec{y} = \vec{b}$ & $m^2$ \\
  3. back substitution & $U\vec{x} = \vec{y}$ & $m^2$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The factorization is done once. Every further right-hand side costs only $2m^2$, the same as a multiplication by $A^{-1}$. Forming $A^{-1}$ would cost about $2m^3$ up front, three times as much as LU, and is less accurate. So we never compute $A^{-1}$ to solve a linear system.

\paragraph{The pivots as ratios of minors~\cite[\S2.3]{Demmel}.} Step 2 of the proof of \Cref{thm:lu-exists} gives $\det A[1{:}k, 1{:}k] = u_{11} \cdots u_{kk}$ for each $k$. Dividing two consecutive ones,
\begin{equation}
  u_{kk} = \frac{\det A[1{:}k, 1{:}k]}{\det A[1{:}k{-}1, 1{:}k{-}1]}.
\end{equation}
So each pivot is determined by $A$ alone, before any elimination is done. For example, for the matrix of the small-pivot example, $u_{11} = \eps$ and $u_{22} = \det A/\eps = 1 - \eps^{-1}$, which matches the $U$ computed there. Moreover, $A = LU$ with $L$ unit lower triangular exists and is unique if and only if $A[1{:}k, 1{:}k]$ is nonsingular for every $k = 1, \dots, m - 1$.

\paragraph{Why the factorization is unique.} Let $A$ be nonsingular and suppose $A = L_1U_1 = L_2U_2$, with $L_1, L_2$ unit lower triangular and $U_1, U_2$ upper triangular. All four are invertible, and
\begin{equation}
  L_2^{-1}L_1 = U_2U_1^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The left side is unit lower triangular and the right side is upper triangular (\Cref{prop:lower-closure}). A matrix that is both must be $I$, so $L_1 = L_2$ and $U_1 = U_2$.

\paragraph{Pivoting, preview.} In the small-pivot example, swap the two rows first:
\begin{equation}
  PA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ \eps & 1 \end{bmatrix} =
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \eps & 1 \end{bmatrix}
  \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 - \eps \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Now the only multiplier is $\eps$, and every entry of $L$ and $U$ is at most $1$ in size. So $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ is about as large as $\abs{A}$, and by \Cref{thm:lu-backward-preview} the error is at the level of machine precision. This is the idea of \emph{partial pivoting}: at each step, swap rows so that the entry of largest absolute value in the current column becomes the pivot. Then every multiplier satisfies $\abs{\ell_{ik}} \le 1$, and we get $PA = LU$ for a permutation matrix $P$. This is the subject of \Cref{ch:pivoting}.

\paragraph{In Julia.} \texttt{lu(A)} computes $PA = LU$ with partial pivoting; \texttt{lu(A, NoPivot())} runs \Cref{alg:lu} as written. For $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 10^{-20} & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ the unpivoted factors satisfy $LU - A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, exactly the failure of the small-pivot example, while the pivoted ones are exact.
\begin{lstlisting}
F = lu(A)                 # F.L, F.U, F.p (row permutation)
x = F \ b                 # reuses the factorization: 2m^2 flops per b
G = lu(A, NoPivot())      # G.L * G.U - A == [0 0; 0 -1] for this A
\end{lstlisting}
