\section{Recap: From Orthogonal Iteration to the QR Algorithm}

We start with a recap of \Cref{ch:qr-alg}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Schur vectors and eigenvectors.} If $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ and $X = QR$, then $A = Q(R\Lambda R^{-1})Q^\ast$, and $R\Lambda R^{-1}$ is upper triangular. So the Schur vectors span the same nested subspaces as the eigenvectors (\Cref{sec:schur-eig}).
  \item \emph{Orthogonal iteration.} It finds these subspaces without knowing $X$. Since $\spn(Q_k) = \spn(A^kQ_0)$, the span of the first $p$ columns converges to $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_p)$. The rate is $\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}$ (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}).
  \item \emph{Convergence to Schur form.} We split $Q_k = [\bar{Q}_k\ \tilde{Q}_k]$ after each column $p$. This shows that $\tilde{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k \to 0$. So $T_k \defeq Q_k^\ast AQ_k$ converges to the triangular factor of a Schur factorization (\Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur}).
  \item \emph{The QR iteration.} Let $U_{k+1} \defeq Q_k^\ast Q_{k+1}$. Then we can write the iteration using only $T_k$:
  \begin{equation}
    T_k = U_{k+1}R_{k+1} \quad\longrightarrow\quad T_{k+1} = R_{k+1}U_{k+1},
  \end{equation}
  starting from $T_0 = A$. This is the basic QR iteration (\Cref{alg:basic-qr}).
\end{itemize}

In this lecture we ask: \emph{how expensive is the QR iteration, and how can we make it cheaper?}

\section{The Cost of the QR Iteration}
\label{sec:qr-improvements}

Each step of \Cref{alg:basic-qr} computes the QR factorization of a dense $m \times m$ matrix. This costs $O(m^3)$, and we may need many steps. Suppose every eigenvector converged in $O(1)$ iterations. There are still $m$ of them, so we would need $O(m)$ factorizations, for a total cost of $O(m^4)$. So faster convergence alone is not enough. We must also make each step cheaper.

The idea is to \emph{preprocess $A$ once}, so that every later QR factorization is simple. The preprocessing has to satisfy some conditions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It must not change the eigenvalues. So it must be a similarity transformation, $A \mapsto Q^\ast AQ$, and not a one-sided product as in the QR factorization.
  \item We take $Q$ unitary. Then the conditioning does not get worse (\Cref{thm:unitary}).
  \item We cannot reduce $A$ all the way to triangular form. That would compute a Schur factorization in finitely many steps, which is impossible (\Cref{sec:negative-result}).
\end{itemize}
The best we can get is triangular form plus one extra diagonal.

\begin{definition}[Hessenberg Matrix]
  \label{def:hessenberg}
  A matrix $H \in \K^{m \times m}$ is \emph{upper Hessenberg} if $h_{ij} = 0$ for $i > j + 1$, that is, it is upper triangular plus one extra subdiagonal:
  \begin{equation}
    H = \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\section{Reduction to Hessenberg Form}
\label{sec:hessenberg}

We compute $Q$ with Householder reflections (\Cref{ch:householder}). Recall that a Householder reflector is both Hermitian and unitary. So it is its own inverse and its own adjoint.

Let $P_1 \in \K^{(m-1) \times (m-1)}$ be the Householder reflector that maps
\begin{equation}
  \vec{v} \defeq A_{2:m,1} \quad\text{to}\quad \norm{\vec{v}}\vec{e}_1, \qquad\text{and set}\qquad Q_1 \defeq \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & P_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $Q_1$ on the left does not change the first row of $A$. It zeros out the first column below the subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{A}
  \xrightarrow{\ Q_1 \cdot\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1A}.
\end{equation}
To complete the similarity, we multiply by $Q_1^\ast = Q_1$ on the right. This acts only on columns $2, \dots, m$. So the first column of $Q_1A$ does not change, and its new zeros stay:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1A}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, Q_1^\ast\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1AQ_1^\ast}.
\end{equation}
This is why the reflector starts at row $2$ and leaves $a_{21}$ in place. Suppose instead we used a reflector on all rows, as in the first step of Householder QR. It would zero out $a_{21}$ too. But then multiplying by its adjoint on the right would mix all columns, and fill the first column back in.

Now we repeat the same step on the trailing $(m-1) \times (m-1)$ block. In step $k$, the reflector $P_k$ maps the part of column $k$ below the diagonal, $\vec{v} = (\cdot)_{k+1:m,k}$, to $\norm{\vec{v}}\vec{e}_1$. We embed it as $Q_k = \diag(I_k, P_k)$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Left multiplication by $Q_k$ changes only rows $k+1, \dots, m$.
  \item Right multiplication by $Q_k$ changes only columns $k+1, \dots, m$.
\end{itemize}
So the zeros we created in columns $1, \dots, k-1$ stay. We need $m - 2$ steps, since the last two columns have nothing below the subdiagonal. Then we collect the reflectors into
\begin{equation}
  Q \defeq Q_1Q_2\cdots Q_{m-2}, \qquad Q^\ast AQ = Q_{m-2}\cdots Q_1\,A\,Q_1\cdots Q_{m-2} = H.
\end{equation}
The order of the factors in $Q$ matters. Each $Q_k$ is Hermitian, but $Q^\ast = Q_{m-2}\cdots Q_1$ has them in reverse order. (On the board the reflectors are collected as ``$Q_m\cdots Q_1 = Q$''. Since every $Q_k$ is Hermitian, that product is $Q^\ast$ in our notation. Also, we need only $m - 2$ reflectors, because the last two columns have nothing below the subdiagonal.)

As in Householder QR, we never form the $Q_k$ or $Q$. We store the reflection vectors and apply them from both sides.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Householder reduction to Hessenberg form~\cite[Algorithm 26.1]{TB}.}
  \label{alg:hessenberg}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$, overwritten by $H = Q^\ast AQ$
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, m - 2$}
      \State $\vec{x} \gets A_{k+1:m,k}$
      \State $\vec{v}_k \gets \operatorname{sign}(x_1)\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1 + \vec{x}$, \quad $\vec{v}_k \gets \vec{v}_k/\norm{\vec{v}_k}$
      \State $A_{k+1:m,k:m} \gets A_{k+1:m,k:m} - 2\vec{v}_k(\vec{v}_k^\ast A_{k+1:m,k:m})$ \Comment{from the left: rows $k+1, \dots, m$}
      \State $A_{1:m,k+1:m} \gets A_{1:m,k+1:m} - 2(A_{1:m,k+1:m}\vec{v}_k)\vec{v}_k^\ast$ \Comment{from the right: columns $k+1, \dots, m$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Here the reflector maps $\vec{x}$ to $-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1$, not to $\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1$. This avoids cancellation, exactly as in \Cref{ch:householder}. Either choice gives a Hessenberg matrix. The cost has two parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The left updates cost about $\tfrac{4}{3}m^3$ flops.
  \item The right updates act on full columns. They cost about $2m^3$ flops.
\end{itemize}
The total is
\begin{equation}
  \text{work for Hessenberg reduction} \sim \tfrac{10}{3}m^3 \text{ flops}~\cite[(26.1)]{TB}.
\end{equation}
We pay this once, before the QR iteration starts.

\section{The QR Iteration on Hessenberg Matrices}
\label{sec:qr-hessenberg}

We now run \Cref{alg:basic-qr} on $T_0 = H$ instead of $A$. The first half of each step is a QR factorization $U_kR_k = T_{k-1}$. Suppose $T_{k-1}$ is upper Hessenberg. Then $O(m)$ Givens rotations are enough, as we saw in \Cref{sec:givens}. We only need to eliminate the $m - 1$ subdiagonal entries, and we can remove them one at a time from the top. Multiplying by $G_1^\ast$ on the left acts on rows $1$ and $2$, and zeros out the $(2,1)$ entry. Then $G_2^\ast$ acts on rows $2$ and $3$, and zeros out the $(3,2)$ entry. And so on:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_1^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_2^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_3^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Each rotation changes only two rows, at a cost of $O(m)$. So the whole factorization
\begin{equation}
  R_k = G_{m-1}^\ast\cdots G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}, \qquad U_k = G_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1},
\end{equation}
costs $O(m^2)$ instead of $O(m^3)$. We never form $U_k$. We only store the $m - 1$ rotations.

The second half of each step computes $T_k = R_kU_k$. We keep $U_k$ in factored form:
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kG_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1},
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $G_j$ on the right changes only columns $j$ and $j + 1$, again at a cost of $O(m)$. So the second half also costs $O(m^2)$.

For this to repeat, $T_k$ must again be upper Hessenberg. It is. Right multiplication by $G_j$ replaces columns $j$ and $j + 1$ by linear combinations of these two columns. Column $j + 1$ of $R_kG_1\cdots G_{j-1}$ has nonzeros down to row $j + 1$. So mixing it into column $j$ creates one new nonzero, at position $(j+1, j)$. This position is on the subdiagonal, and nothing appears below it:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{R_k}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_1\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{\times} & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_2\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \bm{\times} & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_3\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \bm{\times} & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{T_k}.
\end{equation}
So $T_k = R_kG_1\cdots G_{m-1}$ is upper Hessenberg, and every step of the QR iteration costs $O(m^2)$. Then $m$ iterations cost $O(m^3)$ in total. This is the same order as the one-time reduction to Hessenberg form. On the dense matrix $A$, the QR iteration would cost $O(m^4)$.

\section{Bulge Chasing}
\label{sec:bulge-chasing}

So far, one step of the QR iteration on a Hessenberg matrix has two sweeps.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The first sweep applies $G_1^\ast, \dots, G_{m-1}^\ast$ from the left. It turns $T_{k-1}$ into the upper triangular $R_k$.
  \item The second sweep applies $G_1, \dots, G_{m-1}$ from the right. It turns $R_k$ back into the Hessenberg matrix $T_k$.
\end{itemize}
In one formula,
\begin{equation}
  T_k = \bigl(G_{m-1}^\ast\cdots G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}\bigr)\,G_1\cdots G_{m-1}.
\end{equation}
Matrix multiplication is associative, so we do not have to use this order. \emph{Can we apply the left and right rotations at the same time}? That is, can we compute
\begin{equation}
  T_k = G_{m-1}^\ast\Bigl(\cdots G_2^\ast\bigl(G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}\,G_1\bigr)G_2\cdots\Bigr)G_{m-1},
\end{equation}
and finish each similarity $G_j^\ast(\cdot)G_j$ before we move on to the next rotation?

The difficulty is that we do not know the rotations in advance. We define $G_2$ by one requirement: $G_2^\ast$ must zero out the $(3,2)$ entry of $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}$. So to compute $G_2$, we need the matrix $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}$. If we apply $G_1$ on the right right away, we only have $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}G_1$. In this matrix, the second column has been mixed with the first. \emph{How can we compute $G_2$ from $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}G_1$?}

To answer this, we look at what happens when we compute $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}G_1$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Left multiplication by $G_1^\ast$ only combines rows $1$ and $2$. It keeps the Hessenberg form (it zeros out the $(2,1)$ entry).
  \item Right multiplication by $G_1$ then combines columns $1$ and $2$. Column $2$ has a nonzero in row $3$.
\end{itemize}
So we get a nonzero at position $(3,1)$, one below the subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{T_{k-1}}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_1^\ast\,\cdot\,G_1\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
This entry, marked $+$, is called the \emph{bulge}. The idea is to \emph{choose $G_2$ so that it removes the bulge}. So $G_2^\ast$ acts on rows $2$ and $3$, and zeros out the $(3,1)$ entry. Then right multiplication by $G_2$ combines columns $2$ and $3$. This creates a new bulge at $(4,2)$. So the bulge moves one step further down, along the subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_2^\ast\,\cdot\,G_2\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_3^\ast\,\cdot\,G_3\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \bm{+} & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_4^\ast\,\cdot\,G_4\ }
  \text{Hessenberg}.
\end{equation}
The last rotation chases the bulge off the bottom of the matrix. What is left is an upper Hessenberg matrix. We apply each rotation from both sides as soon as we know it, so the step is a single sweep through the matrix. This is \emph{bulge chasing}. We still need to show that this gives the same $T_k$ as the two-sweep algorithm (up to signs). This follows from the implicit Q theorem. We state it in \Cref{sec:implicit-q} and prove it at the start of the next lecture (\Cref{sec:implicit-q-proof}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{One step of the QR iteration on an upper Hessenberg matrix by bulge chasing.}
  \label{alg:bulge-chasing}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require upper Hessenberg $T \in \K^{m \times m}$, overwritten by the next iterate
    \State choose $G_1$ acting on rows $1, 2$ such that $(G_1^\ast T)_{21} = 0$
    \State $T \gets G_1^\ast TG_1$ \Comment{creates the bulge at $(3,1)$}
    \For{$j = 2, \dots, m - 1$}
      \State choose $G_j$ acting on rows $j, j+1$ such that $(G_j^\ast T)_{j+1,j-1} = 0$ \Comment{remove the bulge}
      \State $T \gets G_j^\ast TG_j$ \Comment{bulge moves to $(j+2, j)$, or leaves the matrix if $j = m - 1$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Each step changes only two rows and two columns. So one QR step still costs $O(m^2)$, but now in a single sweep through the matrix.

\section{The Implicit Q Theorem}
\label{sec:implicit-q}

We end with the statement of the theorem that justifies bulge chasing. We give its proof in \Cref{sec:implicit-q-proof}.

\begin{definition}[Unreduced Hessenberg Matrix]
  An upper Hessenberg matrix $H$ is \emph{unreduced} if all its subdiagonal entries are nonzero: $h_{j+1,j} \ne 0$ for $j = 1, \dots, m - 1$. Equivalently, $H$ is not block upper triangular.
\end{definition}

Suppose some $h_{j+1,j} = 0$. Then $H$ is block upper triangular, and its eigenvalue problem splits into the problems for the two diagonal blocks. So we can always assume that $H$ is unreduced.

\begin{theorem}[Implicit Q Theorem]
  \label{thm:implicit-q}
  Assume that $Q$ is orthogonal and $Q^\T AQ = H$ is unreduced upper Hessenberg. Then the first column of $Q$ determines columns $2, \dots, m$ of $Q$ uniquely, up to signs.
\end{theorem}

The name comes from how we use the theorem. To know the whole transformation $Q$, it is enough to know two things: its first column, and that the result is upper Hessenberg. We never have to compute $Q$ explicitly.

For bulge chasing, this works as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Both the two-sweep algorithm and the one-sweep algorithm give an upper Hessenberg $T_k = U_k^\ast T_{k-1}U_k$.
  \item In both, $U_k\vec{e}_1 = G_1\vec{e}_1$, with the same $G_1$.
\end{itemize}
So by the theorem, the two $U_k$ agree up to signs, and so do the two $T_k$ (\Cref{sec:implicit-q-bulge}).

\section{(SUPPLEMENT) Further Topics}
\label{sec:hessenberg-supp}
\subsection{The Hermitian Case: Tridiagonal Form}

If $A$ is Hermitian, then so is $H = Q^\ast AQ$. A Hermitian Hessenberg matrix is tridiagonal. We can use the zeros and the symmetry to reduce the cost from $\tfrac{10}{3}m^3$ to $\sim\tfrac{4}{3}m^3$ flops. After that, each QR step on a tridiagonal matrix costs only $O(m)$~\cite[Lecture 26]{TB}, \cite[Section 4.4.7]{Demmel}.

\subsection{Stability}

Like Householder QR, \Cref{alg:hessenberg} is backward stable. Let $\tilde{H}$ be the computed result, and let $\tilde{Q}$ be the exactly unitary matrix defined by the computed reflection vectors. Then $\tilde{Q}\tilde{H}\tilde{Q}^\ast = A + \delta A$ with $\norm{\delta A}/\norm{A} = O(\eps)$~\cite[Theorem 26.1]{TB}. So the eigenvalues of $\tilde{H}$ are the exact eigenvalues of a nearby matrix.

\subsection{The ``Bad Idea'' Revisited}

Suppose we apply a full Householder reflector from both sides, as in the first step of Householder QR. This destroys the zeros it creates. But it usually does make the entries below the diagonal smaller. This is exactly what one step of the QR iteration does. In this sense, the QR algorithm ``triangularizes in the limit''~\cite[Lecture 26]{TB}.

\subsection{In Practice}

LAPACK computes the Hessenberg reduction with blocked Householder updates (\texttt{xGEHRD}). It runs the QR iteration with multishift bulge chasing and aggressive early deflation (\texttt{xHSEQR}). In Julia, \texttt{hessenberg(A)} returns the reduction, and \texttt{eigvals(A)} runs all the steps.
