\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture (\Cref{ch:qr-alg}) linked the Schur form to the eigendecomposition ($X = QR$), proved that orthogonal iteration converges to the Schur factorization, and turned it into the basic QR iteration $T_k = U_{k+1}R_{k+1} \to T_{k+1} = R_{k+1}U_{k+1}$. But each step costs $O(m^3)$. Now we make each step cost $O(m^2)$.

The plan of this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Reduce $A$ once to Hessenberg form $H = Q^\ast AQ$ (\Cref{sec:qr-improvements,sec:hessenberg}).
  \item On $H$, one QR step takes $m - 1$ Givens rotations and costs $O(m^2)$ (\Cref{sec:qr-hessenberg}).
  \item Bulge chasing does the same step in one sweep (\Cref{sec:bulge-chasing}).
  \item The implicit Q theorem will show that bulge chasing gives the same $T_k$ (\Cref{sec:implicit-q}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Improvements to QR}
\label{sec:qr-improvements}

The bottleneck: each step of \Cref{alg:basic-qr} is a dense QR factorization, at cost $O(m^3)$, and we possibly need $m$ of them. So the cost is $O(m^4)$, even if every eigenvector converges in $O(1)$ iterations.

The idea: preprocess $A$ once, to simplify the QR factorizations. The goal is to find $Q$ such that
\begin{equation}
  H = Q^\ast AQ
\end{equation}
is as close to triangular as possible. This must be a similarity, so that the eigenvalues do not change, and we take $Q$ unitary, so that the conditioning does not get worse (\Cref{thm:unitary}).

\begin{definition}[Hessenberg Matrix]
  \label{def:hessenberg}
  $H \in \K^{m \times m}$ is \emph{upper Hessenberg} if $h_{ij} = 0$ for $i > j + 1$: it has only one nonzero lower diagonal.
  \begin{equation}
    H = \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\section{Reduction to Hessenberg Form}
\label{sec:hessenberg}

We compute $Q$ using Householder reflections (\Cref{ch:householder}). Let $P_1 \in \K^{(m-1) \times (m-1)}$ be the reflector that maps
\begin{equation}
  \vec{v} \defeq A_{2:m,1} \quad\text{to}\quad \norm{\vec{v}}\vec{e}_1, \qquad\text{and set}\qquad Q_1 \defeq \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & P_1 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Left multiplication by $Q_1$ keeps the first row and zeros out column $1$ below the subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{A}
  \xrightarrow{\ Q_1 \cdot\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1A}.
\end{equation}
Right multiplication by $Q_1^\ast$ acts only on columns $2, \dots, m$. So the new zeros in column $1$ stay:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1A}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, Q_1^\ast\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{Q_1AQ_1^\ast}.
\end{equation}
This is why the reflector starts at row $2$ and leaves $a_{21}$ in place. A reflector on all rows would also zero out $a_{21}$, but its adjoint on the right would mix all columns and fill column $1$ back in.

We repeat on the $(2{:}m, 2{:}m)$ submatrix, and so on, with $Q_k = \diag(I_k, P_k)$. Left and right multiplication by $Q_k$ only touch rows and columns $k + 1, \dots, m$, so earlier zeros stay. After $m - 2$ steps we gather the reflectors:
\begin{equation}
  Q_{m-2}\cdots Q_1\,A\,Q_1\cdots Q_{m-2} = Q^\ast AQ = H, \qquad Q \defeq Q_1\cdots Q_{m-2}.
\end{equation}
Each $Q_k$ is Hermitian, so $Q^\ast = Q_{m-2}\cdots Q_1$. In practice we never form $Q$; we store the reflection vectors (\Cref{alg:hessenberg}).

\paragraph{Why not upper triangular?} Because it is impossible. A unitary similarity to triangular form in finitely many steps would compute all eigenvalues exactly, i.e., the roots of any polynomial. This contradicts Abel's theorem (\Cref{sec:negative-result}).

\section{QR Iteration on $H$}
\label{sec:qr-hessenberg}

We now run \Cref{alg:basic-qr} on $T_0 = H$.

\paragraph{First step: compute $U_k, R_k$ with $U_kR_k = T_{k-1}$.} We have seen that $O(m)$ Givens rotations suffice (\Cref{sec:givens}). $G_j^\ast$ acts on rows $j, j+1$ and zeros out the $(j+1, j)$ entry:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_1^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_2^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_3^\ast\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Each rotation costs $O(m)$, so the QR factorization costs $O(m^2)$:
\begin{equation}
  R_k = G_{m-1}^\ast\cdots G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}, \qquad U_k = G_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Second step: compute $T_k = R_kU_k$.} We keep $U_k$ in factored form:
\begin{equation}
  T_k = R_kG_1G_2\cdots G_{m-1}.
\end{equation}
Again each rotation changes two columns, and the cost is $O(m^2)$.

So the total cost is $O(m^3)$ for $m$ iterations, the same order as the reduction to Hessenberg form.

\paragraph{Why is $T_k$ upper Hessenberg?} Right multiplication by $G_j$ forms linear combinations of columns $j$ and $j + 1$. Column $j + 1$ has nonzeros down to row $j + 1$, so this creates one new nonzero at $(j+1, j)$, on the subdiagonal:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{R_k}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_1\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{\times} & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_2\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \bm{\times} & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ \cdot\, G_3\ }
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \bm{\times} & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{T_k}.
\end{equation}
So the Hessenberg form is kept, and every step costs $O(m^2)$.

\section{Bulge Chasing}
\label{sec:bulge-chasing}

So far, one step applies all rotations from the left, then all from the right:
\begin{equation}
  T_k = \bigl(G_{m-1}^\ast\cdots G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}\bigr)\,G_1\cdots G_{m-1}.
\end{equation}
Can we apply left and right simultaneously?
\begin{equation}
  T_k = G_{m-1}^\ast\Bigl(\cdots G_2^\ast\bigl(G_1^\ast\,T_{k-1}\,G_1\bigr)G_2\cdots\Bigr)G_{m-1}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Problem.} We need $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}$ to compute $G_2$: $G_2^\ast$ must zero out its $(3,2)$ entry. How can we compute $G_2$ from $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}G_1$ instead?

What happens when we compute $G_1^\ast T_{k-1}G_1$? $G_1^\ast$ combines rows $1, 2$ and keeps the Hessenberg form. Then $G_1$ combines columns $1, 2$, and column $2$ has a nonzero in row $3$. So we get a nonzero at $(3,1)$, the \emph{bulge}:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}}_{T_{k-1}}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_1^\ast\,\cdot\,G_1\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Idea.} Choose $G_2$ such that it removes the bulge. $G_2^\ast$ acts on rows $2, 3$ and zeros out $(3,1)$; then $G_2$ combines columns $2, 3$ and creates a new bulge at $(4,2)$. So the bulge moves further down the off-diagonal:
\begingroup\setlength{\arraycolsep}{3.5pt}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_2^\ast\,\cdot\,G_2\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \bm{+} & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_3^\ast\,\cdot\,G_3\ }
  \begin{bmatrix} \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & \times & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \times & \times & \times \\ 0 & 0 & \bm{+} & \times & \times \end{bmatrix}
  \xrightarrow{\ G_4^\ast\,\cdot\,G_4\ }
  \text{Hessenberg}.
\end{equation}
\endgroup
The last rotation chases the bulge off the bottom. Each step changes two rows and two columns, so one QR step still costs $O(m^2)$, now in a single sweep (\Cref{alg:bulge-chasing}).

We need to show that this leads to the same $T_k$. This follows from the implicit Q theorem.

\section{The Implicit Q Theorem}
\label{sec:implicit-q}

An upper Hessenberg $H$ is \emph{unreduced} if all subdiagonal entries are nonzero, $h_{j+1,j} \ne 0$, i.e., $H$ is not block upper triangular. (If some $h_{j+1,j} = 0$, the eigenvalue problem splits into the two diagonal blocks.)

\begin{theorem}[Implicit Q Theorem]
  \label{thm:implicit-q}
  Assume that $Q$ is orthogonal and $Q^\T AQ = H$ is unreduced upper Hessenberg. Then columns $2, \dots, m$ of $Q$ are uniquely determined by column $1$, up to signs.
\end{theorem}

So to know $Q$, it is enough to know its first column and that the result is unreduced Hessenberg. In bulge chasing, $U_k\vec{e}_1 = G_1\vec{e}_1$ as in the two-sweep algorithm, and both results are Hessenberg. We prove the theorem and apply it in \Cref{sec:implicit-q-proof,sec:implicit-q-bulge}.

\subsection{The Householder Reduction Algorithm and Its Cost}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Householder reduction to Hessenberg form~\cite[Algorithm 26.1]{TB}.}
  \label{alg:hessenberg}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$, overwritten by $H = Q^\ast AQ$
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, m - 2$}
      \State $\vec{x} \gets A_{k+1:m,k}$
      \State $\vec{v}_k \gets \operatorname{sign}(x_1)\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1 + \vec{x}$, \quad $\vec{v}_k \gets \vec{v}_k/\norm{\vec{v}_k}$
      \State $A_{k+1:m,k:m} \gets A_{k+1:m,k:m} - 2\vec{v}_k(\vec{v}_k^\ast A_{k+1:m,k:m})$ \Comment{left: rows $k+1, \dots, m$}
      \State $A_{1:m,k+1:m} \gets A_{1:m,k+1:m} - 2(A_{1:m,k+1:m}\vec{v}_k)\vec{v}_k^\ast$ \Comment{right: columns $k+1, \dots, m$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Here the reflector maps $\vec{x}$ to $-\operatorname{sign}(x_1)\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1$ rather than $\norm{\vec{x}}\vec{e}_1$, to avoid cancellation (\Cref{ch:householder}). Either choice gives a Hessenberg matrix. The left updates cost about $\tfrac{4}{3}m^3$ flops; the right updates act on full columns and cost about $2m^3$. In total,
\begin{equation}
  \text{work for Hessenberg reduction} \sim \tfrac{10}{3}m^3 \text{ flops}~\cite[(26.1)]{TB},
\end{equation}
paid once, before the QR iteration starts.

\subsection{Bulge Chasing as an Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{One step of the QR iteration on an upper Hessenberg matrix by bulge chasing.}
  \label{alg:bulge-chasing}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require upper Hessenberg $T \in \K^{m \times m}$, overwritten by the next iterate
    \State choose $G_1$ acting on rows $1, 2$ such that $(G_1^\ast T)_{21} = 0$
    \State $T \gets G_1^\ast TG_1$ \Comment{creates the bulge at $(3,1)$}
    \For{$j = 2, \dots, m - 1$}
      \State choose $G_j$ acting on rows $j, j+1$ such that $(G_j^\ast T)_{j+1,j-1} = 0$ \Comment{remove the bulge}
      \State $T \gets G_j^\ast TG_j$ \Comment{bulge moves to $(j+2, j)$, or leaves if $j = m - 1$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{The Hermitian Case: Tridiagonal Form}

If $A$ is Hermitian, then so is $H = Q^\ast AQ$. A Hermitian Hessenberg matrix is tridiagonal. Using the zeros and the symmetry, the reduction costs $\sim\tfrac{4}{3}m^3$ flops instead of $\tfrac{10}{3}m^3$. After that, each QR step on a tridiagonal matrix costs only $O(m)$~\cite[Lecture 26]{TB}, \cite[Section 4.4.7]{Demmel}.

\subsection{Stability}

Like Householder QR, \Cref{alg:hessenberg} is backward stable. Let $\tilde{H}$ be the computed result, and $\tilde{Q}$ the exactly unitary matrix defined by the computed reflection vectors. Then $\tilde{Q}\tilde{H}\tilde{Q}^\ast = A + \delta A$ with $\norm{\delta A}/\norm{A} = O(\epsm)$~\cite[Theorem 26.1]{TB}. So the eigenvalues of $\tilde{H}$ are the exact eigenvalues of a nearby matrix.
