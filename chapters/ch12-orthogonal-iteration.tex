\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture (\Cref{ch:schur}) covered deflation, the need for subspace iteration (matrix-vector products become matrix-matrix products), eigenvalue problems as matrix factorizations $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$, defective matrices and Jordan blocks, and the Schur factorization $A = QTQ^\ast$. Now we ask when $T$ is diagonal, and we start computing several eigenpairs at once.

The plan of this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The Schur factorization is a diagonalization exactly for \emph{normal} matrices (\Cref{sec:normal}).
  \item \emph{Orthogonal iteration} with two vectors: power iteration plus one projection (\Cref{sec:orth-iter}).
  \item The same with $r$ vectors, using a QR factorization (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-general}).
  \item The columns converge to the Q factor of the eigenvector matrix (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-eigvec}).
  \item With $r = m$, this gives the Schur factorization (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-schur}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{From Schur Factorization to Diagonalization}
\label{sec:normal}

The Schur factorization $A = QTQ^\ast$ always exists, but $T$ is only upper triangular. When is it a diagonalization, that is, when is $T$ diagonal?

\begin{definition}[Normal Matrix]
  \label{def:normal}
  $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ is \emph{normal} if $AA^\ast = A^\ast A$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Normal Matrices Are Unitarily Diagonalizable]
  \label{thm:normal-diag}
  $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ is normal if and only if it has an orthogonal eigenvalue decomposition $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$ with $Q$ unitary and $\Lambda$ diagonal. In this case the triangular factor $T$ of every Schur factorization $A = QTQ^\ast$ is diagonal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  If $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$, then $AA^\ast = Q\Lambda\overline{\Lambda}Q^\ast = Q\overline{\Lambda}\Lambda Q^\ast = A^\ast A$, since diagonal matrices commute.

  Now let $A$ be normal, and take a Schur factorization $A = QTQ^\ast$ (\Cref{thm:schur}). Then
  \begin{equation}
    QTT^\ast Q^\ast = AA^\ast = A^\ast A = QT^\ast TQ^\ast \quad\Longrightarrow\quad TT^\ast = T^\ast T.
  \end{equation}
  We compare the $(1,1)$ entries. The first column of $T$ is $t_{11}\vec{e}_1$, so
  \begin{equation}
    \abs{t_{11}}^2 = (T^\ast T)_{11} = (TT^\ast)_{11} = \sum_{i=1}^m\abs{t_{1i}}^2.
  \end{equation}
  Hence $t_{1i} = 0$ for $i \ge 2$, and $T = \diag(t_{11}, T_2)$. The lower right block of $TT^\ast = T^\ast T$ says that $T_2$ is normal again. By induction on $m$, $T$ is diagonal.
\end{proof}

\section{Orthogonal Iteration}
\label{sec:orth-iter}

In this section, $A \in \R^{m \times m}$ is diagonalizable, with
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} > \dots > \abs{\lambda_m}.
\end{equation}
Non-real eigenvalues of a real matrix come in conjugate pairs of equal modulus. So all eigenvalues and eigenvectors here are real.

Assume we want to compute $r = 2$ eigenpairs at once. We apply power iteration to two vectors at once.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Problem.} Both vectors converge to the same eigenvector $\vec{x}_1$.
  \item \emph{Idea.} Project $\vec{q}_2$ onto the orthogonal complement of $\vec{q}_1$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Orthogonal iteration with two vectors.}
  \label{alg:orth-iter-2}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q}_1, \vec{q}_2 \gets$ random vectors
    \While{not converged}
      \State $\vec{q}_1 \gets A\vec{q}_1$, \quad $\vec{q}_2 \gets A\vec{q}_2$
      \State $\vec{q}_2 \gets \left(I - \dfrac{\vec{q}_1\vec{q}_1^\T}{\vec{q}_1^\T\vec{q}_1}\right)\vec{q}_2$ \Comment{project out $\vec{q}_1$}
      \State $\vec{q}_1 \gets \vec{q}_1/\norm{\vec{q}_1}$, \quad $\vec{q}_2 \gets \vec{q}_2/\norm{\vec{q}_2}$
    \EndWhile
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The last two lines are Gram--Schmidt on $[A\vec{q}_1\ A\vec{q}_2]$, i.e., a reduced QR factorization (\Cref{ch:ls}).

\subsection{What Do the Two Vectors Converge To?}

The iteration of $\vec{q}_1$ is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}_1^{(k)} = \frac{A^k\vec{q}_1^{(0)}}{\norm{A^k\vec{q}_1^{(0)}}}.
\end{equation}
So $\vec{q}_1$ performs power iteration (\Cref{alg:power}) and converges to $\vec{x}_1$ (normalized, up to sign).

Now assume that $\vec{q}_1 \approx \vec{x}_1$ already, with $\norm{\vec{x}_1} = 1$. Then, up to normalization,
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}_2^{(k)} \approx (I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)A\vec{q}_2^{(k-1)} = (I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)A(I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)\vec{q}_2^{(k-1)}.
\end{equation}
The equality holds because $\vec{q}_2^{(k-1)}$ is already orthogonal to $\vec{x}_1$. So $\vec{q}_2$ does power iteration with the matrix $(I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)A(I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)$.

\paragraph{The normal case.} For normal $A$, this matrix is the deflated matrix of \Cref{prop:hotelling}:
\begin{equation}
  (I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)A(I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T) = A - 2\lambda_1\vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T + \lambda_1\vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T = A - \lambda_1\vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T.
  \label{eq:orth-iter-deflation}
\end{equation}
To get the middle expression, we expand the product and use $A\vec{x}_1 = \lambda_1\vec{x}_1$ and $\vec{x}_1^\T A = \lambda_1\vec{x}_1^\T$. The second identity needs normality (\Cref{sec:normal-left-eigvec}).

So orthogonal iteration does deflation automatically. Unlike \Cref{sec:deflation}, it projects out the current $\vec{q}_1$, not the converged $\vec{x}_1$, so both vectors iterate at the same time. The eigenvalues of $A - \lambda_1\vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T$ are $0, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_m$, so $\vec{q}_2 \to \vec{x}_2$ at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_2}$.

\paragraph{The general case.} Write $P \defeq I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T$. Since $P\vec{x}_1 = \zerovec$,
\begin{equation}
  PAP\vec{x}_i = P\bigl(\lambda_i\vec{x}_i - \lambda_1\vec{x}_1(\vec{x}_1^\T\vec{x}_i)\bigr) = \lambda_iP\vec{x}_i.
\end{equation}
Read the left side as $PA$ applied to $P\vec{x}_i$. Together with $PA\vec{x}_1 = \lambda_1P\vec{x}_1 = \zerovec$, this gives the eigenpairs of $(I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)A$:
\begin{equation}
  (0, \vec{x}_1) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \bigl(\lambda_i, (I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)\vec{x}_i\bigr), \quad i = 2, \dots, m.
\end{equation}
The dominant one is $(\lambda_2, P\vec{x}_2)$. So, normalized and up to sign,
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}_2^{(k)} \longrightarrow (I - \vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\T)\vec{x}_2 \ne \vec{x}_2 \qquad (\text{if } \vec{x}_1 \not\perp \vec{x}_2).
\end{equation}
This makes sense: $\vec{q}_1 \perp \vec{q}_2$ by construction, but $\vec{x}_1$ and $\vec{x}_2$ need not be orthogonal.

But the subspace converges:
\begin{equation}
  \spn\bigl(\vec{q}_1^{(k)}, \vec{q}_2^{(k)}\bigr) \longrightarrow \spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2),
\end{equation}
since $P\vec{x}_2 = \vec{x}_2 - (\vec{x}_1^\T\vec{x}_2)\vec{x}_1$. This is an invariant subspace of $A$, and $\vec{q}_1, \vec{q}_2$ are the first two columns of a Schur factorization (\Cref{sec:schur-fact}).

\subsection{Extracting the Eigenvalues}

How do we find the eigenvalues? We collect the iterates and project $A$ onto their span:
\begin{equation}
  Q_k \defeq \begin{bmatrix} \vec{q}_1^{(k)} & \vec{q}_2^{(k)} \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{m \times 2}, \qquad \text{compute } Q_k^\T AQ_k \in \R^{2 \times 2}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Projected Matrix]
  \label{prop:orth-iter-2x2}
  As $k \to \infty$,
  \begin{equation}
    Q_k^\T AQ_k = \begin{bmatrix} \vec{q}_1^\T A\vec{q}_1 & \vec{q}_1^\T A\vec{q}_2 \\ \vec{q}_2^\T A\vec{q}_1 & \vec{q}_2^\T A\vec{q}_2 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & \ast \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\end{tikzpicture}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Write $Q = Q_k$. For the $(2,1)$ entry, we use $\vec{q}_1 \approx \vec{x}_1$ twice:
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{q}_2^\T A\vec{q}_1 \approx \vec{q}_2^\T A\vec{x}_1 = \lambda_1\vec{q}_2^\T\vec{x}_1 \approx \lambda_1\vec{q}_2^\T\vec{q}_1 = 0.
  \end{equation}
  For the eigenvalues, let $\vec{v}_i \in \R^2$ with $Q\vec{v}_i \approx \vec{x}_i$, $i = 1, 2$. It exists because $\vec{x}_i$ lies (approximately) in $\range(Q)$. With $Q^\T Q = I_2$,
  \begin{equation}
    Q^\T AQ\vec{v}_i \approx Q^\T A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_iQ^\T\vec{x}_i \approx \lambda_iQ^\T Q\vec{v}_i = \lambda_i\vec{v}_i.
  \end{equation}
  So $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are the eigenvalues of the $2 \times 2$ matrix. The $(1,1)$ entry is the Rayleigh quotient of $\vec{q}_1 \approx \vec{x}_1$, so it tends to $\lambda_1$, and the $(2,2)$ entry tends to $\lambda_2$.
\end{proof}

The proof also gives the eigenvectors: solve the small problem $Q_k^\T AQ_k\vec{v}_i = \lambda_i\vec{v}_i$, then lift, $\vec{x}_i \approx Q_k\vec{v}_i$.

\section{Orthogonal Iteration in General}
\label{sec:orth-iter-general}

To compute $r > 2$ eigenpairs at once, we start with $r$ random vectors, apply $A$, and reorthogonalize. In matrix form: we repeatedly multiply with $A$ and orthogonalize with a QR factorization.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Orthogonal iteration $(A, Q)$.}
  \label{alg:orth-iter}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$, starting $Q \in \K^{m \times r}$ (e.g., random)
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, \mathrm{iters}$}
      \State $Q \gets AQ$ \Comment{Level 3: $A$ is read once for all $r$ columns}
      \State $Q, R \gets \mathrm{qr}(Q)$ \Comment{reduced QR, $Q \in \K^{m \times r}$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

In the QR factorization, column $j$ is $A\vec{q}_j$ with its components along $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{j-1}$ removed. So the first two columns do exactly what \Cref{alg:orth-iter-2} does.

\section{Convergence of Eigenvector Estimates}
\label{sec:orth-iter-eigvec}

Let $Q_k = [\vec{q}_1^{(k)} \cdots \vec{q}_r^{(k)}]$ be the iterate after $k$ steps. The first $i$ columns of a QR factorization depend only on the first $i$ columns of the matrix. So the first $i$ columns of $Q_k$ are orthogonal iteration with $i$ vectors, and as in \Cref{sec:orth-iter},
\begin{equation}
  \spn\bigl(\vec{q}_1^{(k)}, \dots, \vec{q}_i^{(k)}\bigr) \longrightarrow \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, r.
  \label{eq:orth-iter-nested}
\end{equation}
This holds under a genericity assumption on $Q_0$, and we prove it in \Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}.

\paragraph{Eigenvalues.} By the same argument as before, with $Q_k\vec{v}_i \approx \vec{x}_i$,
\begin{equation}
  Q_k^\T AQ_k\vec{v}_i \approx Q_k^\T A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_iQ_k^\T\vec{x}_i \approx \lambda_iQ_k^\T Q_k\vec{v}_i = \lambda_i\vec{v}_i.
\end{equation}
What about the eigenvectors?

\paragraph{Symmetric case.} If $A$ is symmetric, then as before $\vec{q}_1^{(k)} \to \vec{x}_1$ and $\vec{q}_2^{(k)} \to \vec{x}_2$. By the same argument,
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}_i^{(k)} \longrightarrow \vec{x}_i, \qquad 2 < i \le r,
\end{equation}
up to sign. Once $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_{i-1}$ have converged, column $i$ does power iteration with $\sum_{j \ge i}\lambda_j\vec{x}_j\vec{x}_j^\T$, whose dominant eigenvector is $\vec{x}_i$.

\paragraph{Non-symmetric case.} Now let $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ be diagonalizable, but not symmetric (or normal). We take a reduced QR factorization of the first $r$ eigenvectors:
\begin{equation}
  [\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_r] \eqqcolon Q^XR^X, \qquad Q^X = [\vec{q}_1^X \cdots \vec{q}_r^X].
  \label{eq:orth-iter-QX}
\end{equation}
$R^X$ is upper triangular and invertible. So, as in \cref{eq:schur-nested}, $\spn(\vec{q}_1^X, \dots, \vec{q}_i^X) = \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_i)$ for every $i$.

\begin{proposition}[Limit of the Orthogonal Iterates]
  \label{prop:orth-iter-QX}
  Assume \cref{eq:orth-iter-nested}. Then $Q_k \to Q^X$, up to the signs of the columns.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  We check column by column, and write $\vec{q}_i$ for the limit of $\vec{q}_i^{(k)}$. First, $\vec{q}_1^{(k)} \to \vec{q}_1 = \vec{x}_1$ (normalized), and $\vec{q}_1^X = \vec{x}_1$ (normalized).

  Now
  \begin{equation}
    \spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2) = \spn(\vec{q}_1, \vec{q}_2) = \spn(\vec{q}_1^X, \vec{q}_2^X).
  \end{equation}
  So $\vec{q}_2$ is a unit vector in the orthogonal complement of $\vec{q}_1$ in this plane, and so is $\vec{q}_2^X$. This complement is a line, so $\vec{q}_2 = \pm\vec{q}_2^X$.

  We repeat the argument for $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_i) = \spn(\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_i) = \spn(\vec{q}_1^X, \dots, \vec{q}_i^X)$.
\end{proof}

For symmetric $A$ the eigenvectors are orthonormal, so $Q^X = [\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_r]$, and we get the symmetric case back.

\section{Convergence to Schur Factorization}
\label{sec:orth-iter-schur}

Now take $r = m$, so $Q_k$ and $Q^X$ are square and orthogonal. Consider
\begin{equation}
  Q_k^\T AQ_k \longrightarrow Q^{X\T}AQ^X,
\end{equation}
using
\begin{equation}
  Q^XR^X = X \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q^X = X(R^X)^{-1} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q^{X\T} = R^XX^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  Q^{X\T}AQ^X = R^XX^{-1}AX(R^X)^{-1} = R^XX^{-1}X\Lambda(R^X)^{-1} = \underbrace{R^X\Lambda(R^X)^{-1}}_{\text{upper triangular!}}.
  \label{eq:orth-iter-schur-limit}
\end{equation}
Its diagonal is $r_{ii}\lambda_ir_{ii}^{-1} = \lambda_i$. Its eigenpairs are
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\lambda_i, R^X\vec{e}_i\bigr), \qquad\text{since}\quad R^X\Lambda(R^X)^{-1}R^X\vec{e}_i = \lambda_iR^X\vec{e}_i.
\end{equation}
(The eigenvector is $R^X\vec{e}_i$, not $(R^X)^{-1}\vec{e}_i$. Lifting it gives $Q^XR^X\vec{e}_i = \vec{x}_i$.) The column signs do not matter: $Q_k \approx Q^XD$ with $D = \diag(\pm 1)$ only flips the signs of some entries above the diagonal.

In summary,
\begin{equation}
  Q_k, \quad Q_k^\T AQ_k, \quad Q_k^\T
\end{equation}
converge to the Schur factorization of $A$. We can read off the eigenvalues from the diagonal of $Q_k^\T AQ_k$. \Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur} gives the exact conditions, and \Cref{sec:basic-qr} computes $Q_k^\T AQ_k$ without forming $Q_k$.

\section{(SUPPLEMENT) Further Topics}
\label{sec:orth-iter-supp}

\subsection{Examples of Normal Matrices}
\label{sec:normal-examples}

Hermitian ($A^\ast = A$), skew-Hermitian ($A^\ast = -A$) and unitary ($A^\ast = A^{-1}$) matrices are normal: in each case $A^\ast$ commutes with $A$. The nilpotent $N = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is not:
\begin{equation}
  NN^\ast = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \ne \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = N^\ast N.
\end{equation}
Over $\C$, an ``orthogonal'' eigenvalue decomposition means that the eigenvector matrix is unitary.

\subsection{Eigenvectors of Normal Matrices Are Also Left Eigenvectors}
\label{sec:normal-left-eigvec}

In \cref{eq:orth-iter-deflation} we used $\vec{x}_1^\ast A = \lambda_1\vec{x}_1^\ast$. Here is why. $B \defeq A - \lambda_1I$ is normal, since $A$ is. For any normal $B$ and any $\vec{v}$,
\begin{equation}
  \norm{B\vec{v}}^2 = \vec{v}^\ast B^\ast B\vec{v} = \vec{v}^\ast BB^\ast\vec{v} = \norm{B^\ast\vec{v}}^2.
\end{equation}
So $B\vec{x}_1 = \zerovec$ implies $B^\ast\vec{x}_1 = \zerovec$, i.e., $A^\ast\vec{x}_1 = \overline{\lambda_1}\vec{x}_1$. Taking the conjugate transpose gives $\vec{x}_1^\ast A = \lambda_1\vec{x}_1^\ast$. Then three of the four terms in the expansion
\begin{equation}
  A - \vec{x}_1(\vec{x}_1^\ast A) - (A\vec{x}_1)\vec{x}_1^\ast + \vec{x}_1(\vec{x}_1^\ast A\vec{x}_1)\vec{x}_1^\ast
\end{equation}
equal $\pm\lambda_1\vec{x}_1\vec{x}_1^\ast$.

In the general case, $P\vec{x}_i = \vec{x}_i - (\vec{x}_1^\ast\vec{x}_i)\vec{x}_1 \ne \zerovec$ for $i \ge 2$, since the $\vec{x}_i$ are independent. For normal $A$, $\vec{x}_i \perp \vec{x}_1$, so $P\vec{x}_i = \vec{x}_i$, which agrees with \cref{eq:orth-iter-deflation}. Overall, the error of $\vec{q}_2$ decays like $\max(\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}, \abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_2})^k$, and the error of $\vec{q}_i$ is limited by the slowest ratio $\abs{\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_j}$ with $j \le i$.

\subsection{The Complex Case}

Over $\C$, the argument of \Cref{prop:orth-iter-QX} fixes each column only up to a factor $e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}$ instead of a sign: a complex line contains a whole circle of unit vectors.

\subsection{The Rayleigh--Ritz Procedure}

Take any $Q \in \K^{m \times r}$ with orthonormal columns, and form the projected matrix $H \defeq Q^\ast AQ$, as in \Cref{prop:orth-iter-2x2}. This matrix generalizes the Rayleigh quotient from one vector to a subspace. Its eigenvalues $\theta_i$ are called \emph{Ritz values}, and the lifted eigenvectors $Q\vec{v}_i$ are called \emph{Ritz vectors}.

Among all $R \in \K^{r \times r}$, the choice $R = H$ gives the smallest residual $\norm{AQ - QR}_2$. To see this, let $E \defeq AQ - QH$. Then $Q^\ast E = 0$, so for every $\vec{y}$,
\begin{equation}
  \norm{(AQ - QR)\vec{y}}^2 = \norm{E\vec{y} + Q(H - R)\vec{y}}^2 = \norm{E\vec{y}}^2 + \norm{(H - R)\vec{y}}^2 \ge \norm{E\vec{y}}^2.
\end{equation}
For Hermitian $A$, there are $r$ eigenvalues of $A$, each within $\norm{E}_2$ of a different Ritz value. These are not necessarily the largest eigenvalues~\cite[\S7.2]{Demmel}.

So in subspace iteration for Hermitian matrices, we often replace $Q_k$ by its Ritz vectors $Q_kV$, where $Q_k^\ast AQ_k = V\Theta V^\ast$. The Ritz vectors depend only on $\spn(Q_k) = \spn(A^kQ_0)$. There, the components along $\vec{x}_j$, $j > r$, are smaller than the component along $\vec{x}_i$ by roughly $\abs{\lambda_{r+1}/\lambda_i}^k$. So the $i$th Ritz vector converges like $\abs{\lambda_{r+1}/\lambda_i}^k$, not like $\abs{\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i}^k$: a few extra columns speed up convergence. Without the Rayleigh--Ritz step, extra columns do not help, since the leading columns of \Cref{alg:orth-iter} never see the later ones.

\subsection{Why Orthonormalize at Every Step}

Mathematically, $\spn(Q_k) = \spn(A^kQ_0)$. So we could compute $A^kQ_0$ and orthonormalize only once at the end. In floating point this fails. Every column of $A^kQ_0$ converges to a multiple of $\vec{x}_1$, so the columns become nearly parallel. The information about $\vec{x}_2, \dots, \vec{x}_r$ sits in components smaller by factors $\abs{\lambda_i/\lambda_1}^k$, and rounding destroys it~\cite[Lecture~28]{TB}. Orthonormalizing after every multiplication keeps a well-conditioned basis of the same subspace.

With $Q_0 = I$ and $r = m$, the product of all the triangular factors gives the QR factorization of $A^k$ itself. This is the starting point of the QR algorithm in \Cref{ch:qr-alg}.
