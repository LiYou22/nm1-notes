\section{Backward Error of the LU Factorization}

Recall from \Cref{ch:lu} that Gaussian elimination computes $A = LU$ in place, with the multipliers stored below the diagonal and $U$ on and above it, and that it fails on a zero pivot even when $A$ is nonsingular (for example $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$). \Cref{ch:lu} ended with an example in which a tiny pivot destroys the LU factorization of the well-conditioned matrix
\begin{equation}
  A = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \kappa(A) \approx 2.6 \text{ as } \eps \to 0.
\end{equation}
Here $\eps$ is just a small number, much smaller than $\epsm$ (e.g., $10^{-20}$); it is not the machine epsilon. The problem is well-conditioned, so the failure lies with the algorithm. The following theorem explains exactly where it comes from.

\begin{theorem}[Backward Error of LU]
  \label{thm:lu-backward}
  Suppose the LU factorization of $A \in \K^{m \times m}$ runs to completion in floating-point arithmetic without encountering a zero pivot, producing $\tilde{L}$ and $\tilde{U}$. Then, to first order in $\epsm$,
  \begin{equation}
    A = \tilde{L}\tilde{U} + E, \qquad \abs{E} \le m\epsm\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

So $\tilde{L}$ and $\tilde{U}$ are the exact LU factors of a nearby matrix, $A - E$. If $\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}}$ is about as large as $\abs{A}$, then $E$ is tiny compared with $A$ and the algorithm is backward stable. If $\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}}$ is much larger than $\abs{A}$, then $E$ can be as large as $A$. (``To first order'' means we drop terms of size $\epsm^2$.)

\subsection{Proof of \Cref{thm:lu-backward}}

\emph{Formulas for $L$ and $U$.} From $A = LU$ and $L_{ii} = 1$, solving for the entries of $L$ and $U$ gives (these are the numbers elimination computes, in a different order)
\begin{align}
  U_{ik} &= A_{ik} - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} L_{ij}U_{jk}, & i &\le k, \label{eq:lu-u} \\
  L_{ik} &= \frac{1}{U_{kk}}\left(A_{ik} - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} L_{ij}U_{jk}\right), & i &> k. \label{eq:lu-l}
\end{align}
\emph{Rounding in inner products} (Homework 2, \Cref{prop:dot}): in floating point, every term of a sum gets its own small relative error,
\begin{equation}
  \fl\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m} x_iy_i\Bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} x_iy_i(1 + \delta_i), \qquad \abs{\delta_i} \lesssim m\epsm.
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}
  \emph{The $U$ part ($i \le k$).} In floating point, \eqref{eq:lu-u} becomes
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{U}_{ik} = \Bigl(A_{ik} - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\tilde{L}_{ij}\tilde{U}_{jk}(1 + \delta_j)\Bigr)(1 + \delta'),
  \end{equation}
  where $\delta'$ is the error of the last subtraction. Solve for $A_{ik}$, use $\tilde{L}_{ii} = 1$, and write $1/(1 + \delta') = 1 + \delta_i$:
  \begin{equation}
    A_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^{i}\tilde{L}_{ij}\tilde{U}_{jk}(1 + \delta_j) = \sum_{j=1}^{i}\tilde{L}_{ij}\tilde{U}_{jk} + \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{i}\tilde{L}_{ij}\tilde{U}_{jk}\delta_j}_{\eqqcolon\, E_{ik}}.
  \end{equation}
  Every $\abs{\delta_j} \lesssim m\epsm$, so $\abs{E_{ik}} \le m\epsm\sum_j\abs{\tilde{L}_{ij}}\abs{\tilde{U}_{jk}} = m\epsm(\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}})_{ik}$. (The terms with $j > i$ are zero because $\tilde{L}$ is lower triangular.)

  \emph{The $L$ part ($i > k$).} The same argument applied to \eqref{eq:lu-l}. The division by $\tilde{U}_{kk}$ adds one more rounding error, which is merged into $\delta_k$ in the same way, and the bound is the same.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What the Bound Says}

If $\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}}$ is of the same order as $\abs{A}$, then $E$ is a relative perturbation of size $O(m\epsm)$ and the algorithm is backward stable. But $\abs{\tilde{L}}\abs{\tilde{U}}$ can be far larger than $\abs{A}$. In the example above,
\begin{equation}
  \abs{L}\abs{U} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \eps^{-1} & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 0 & \eps^{-1} - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \eps & 1 \\ 1 & 2\eps^{-1} - 1 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The bottom-right entry is about $2\eps^{-1}$; multiplied by $\epsm$, the error can be $O(1)$ or larger, exactly as observed. The question becomes: \emph{how can we keep $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ small?}

\section{Pivots and the Growth of Products}

Look at the storage after $k - 1$ steps of elimination: the first $k - 1$ rows already hold $U$, the first $k - 1$ columns below the diagonal hold $L$, and the trailing block $\tilde{A}$ is still being processed.
\begin{equation}
  \begin{bmatrix}
    U_{11} & \cdots & & & \\
    & \ddots & & & \\
    & & U_{k-1,k-1} & U_{k-1,k} \ \cdots & U_{k-1,m} \\
    & L & L_{k,k-1} & \tilde{A}_{kk} \ \cdots & \tilde{A}_{km} \\
    & & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
    & & L_{m,k-1} & \tilde{A}_{mk} \ \cdots & \tilde{A}_{mm}
  \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
Step $k$ turns row $k$ into a row of $U$ and column $k$ below the diagonal into a column of $L$, and updates the trailing $(m - k) \times (m - k)$ block:
\begin{equation}
  L_{ik} = \frac{\tilde{A}_{ik}}{\tilde{A}_{kk}}, \qquad U_{kj} = \tilde{A}_{kj}, \qquad \tilde{A}_{ij} \gets \tilde{A}_{ij} - L_{ik}U_{kj}.
\end{equation}
The entry $\tilde{A}_{kk}$ is the $k$-th \emph{pivot}. The largest product created in step $k$, which enters both the update and $\abs{L}\abs{U}$, is
\begin{equation}
  \max_{i,j}\abs{L_{ik}U_{kj}} = \max_{i,j}\frac{\abs{\tilde{A}_{ik}}\abs{\tilde{A}_{kj}}}{\abs{\tilde{A}_{kk}}}.
\end{equation}
The numerator is determined by column $k$ and row $k$. To keep this quantity small, we want the denominator, the pivot, to be as large as possible.

\section{Partial Pivoting}

We must pivot when $\tilde{A}_{kk} = 0$, since otherwise the algorithm cannot continue. Even when $\tilde{A}_{kk} \ne 0$, we want to avoid \emph{small} pivots, which make the products $L_{ik}U_{kj}$ explode.

\begin{definition}[Partial (Row) Pivoting]
  At step $k$, \emph{partial pivoting} chooses the pivot to be the entry of largest absolute value in column $k$, on or below the diagonal:
  \begin{equation}
    r = \operatorname*{argmax}_{i \ge k}\abs{\tilde{A}_{ik}}.
  \end{equation}
  It swaps rows $r$ and $k$ with a permutation matrix $P_k$ and then eliminates with $L_k$. The sequence $A \xrightarrow{L_k} \cdots$ becomes $A \xrightarrow{P_k} \cdot \xrightarrow{L_k} \cdots$.
\end{definition}

Because the pivot is the largest entry in its column, every multiplier satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \abs{L_{ik}} = \frac{\abs{\tilde{A}_{ik}}}{\abs{\tilde{A}_{kk}}} \le 1,
\end{equation}
so $\abs{L_{ik}U_{kj}} \le \abs{U_{kj}}$: no update exceeds the size of the current pivot row.

\subsection{Moving the Permutations Together}

With row swaps, elimination becomes
\begin{equation}
  L_{m-1}P_{m-1} \cdots L_2P_2L_1P_1A = U,
\end{equation}
where $L$'s and $P$'s alternate, so we cannot directly write $PA = LU$. We need to move all permutations to the right.

\begin{proposition}[Commuting a Permutation Past an Elimination]
  \label{prop:perm-swap}
  Let $\ell > k$ and let $P_\ell$ swap rows $\ell$ and $i$ for some $i \ge \ell$. Swapping twice undoes the swap, so $P_\ell^{-1} = P_\ell$. Then
  \begin{equation}
    P_\ell L_k = (P_\ell L_kP_\ell)P_\ell, \qquad P_\ell L_kP_\ell = I - (P_\ell\vec{\ell}_k)\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast},
  \end{equation}
  so $P_\ell L_kP_\ell$ is again an elementary lower triangular matrix, obtained from $L_k$ by swapping two of its multipliers.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  The first identity holds because $P_\ell P_\ell = I$. For the second, substitute $L_k = I - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$ and expand:
  \begin{equation}
    P_\ell(I - \vec{\ell}_k\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast})P_\ell
    = P_\ell P_\ell - (P_\ell\vec{\ell}_k)(\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}P_\ell)
    = I - (P_\ell\vec{\ell}_k)(\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}P_\ell).
  \end{equation}
  Multiplying the row vector $\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$ on the right by $P_\ell$ swaps its entries $\ell$ and $i$. Both entries are $0$, since $\ell, i > k$, so nothing changes: $\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}P_\ell = \vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$. This gives $P_\ell L_kP_\ell = I - (P_\ell\vec{\ell}_k)\vec{e}_k^{\,\ast}$.

  Finally, $P_\ell\vec{\ell}_k$ is $\vec{\ell}_k$ with entries $\ell$ and $i$ swapped. Its first $k$ entries are still $0$, so $P_\ell L_kP_\ell$ is again elementary lower triangular.
\end{proof}

Using \Cref{prop:perm-swap} repeatedly to push every $P$ to the right,
\begin{equation}
  L_{m-1}P_{m-1} \cdots L_1P_1 = L'_{m-1} \cdots L'_1\,P_{m-1} \cdots P_1, \qquad L'_k \defeq P_{m-1} \cdots P_{k+1}L_kP_{k+1} \cdots P_{m-1},
\end{equation}
and each $L'_k$ is still elementary lower triangular. With $P \defeq P_{m-1} \cdots P_1$ and $L \defeq (L'_{m-1} \cdots L'_1)^{-1}$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  PA = LU.
\end{equation}

\section{The In-Place Algorithm with Partial Pivoting}

How do we compute this in place, up to the permutation? Suppose $k$ steps are done:
\begin{equation}
  L_k \cdots L_1PA = U_k,
\end{equation}
where the $L_j$ are already the ``permutation-adjusted'' versions and $U_k$ is the current working matrix. In storage, $U_k$ occupies the upper triangle and the trailing block, and the multipliers of $L_1, \dots, L_k$ sit in the strictly lower part of the first $k$ columns. Step $k + 1$ swaps rows and then eliminates:
\begin{equation}
  L_{k+1}P_{k+1}L_k \cdots L_1PA = L_{k+1}P_{k+1}U_k.
\end{equation}
Moving $P_{k+1}$ to the right,
\begin{equation}
  L_{k+1}\underbrace{L'_k \cdots L'_1}_{\text{multipliers permuted by } P_{k+1}}\underbrace{P_{k+1}P}_{P'}A = L_{k+1}\underbrace{P_{k+1}U_k}_{U'_k} = U_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
Reading term by term:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $L'_j = P_{k+1}L_jP_{k+1}$ only permutes the stored multipliers;
  \item $P' = P_{k+1}P$ swaps the same two rows of the accumulated permutation;
  \item $U'_k = P_{k+1}U_k$ swaps the same two rows of the working matrix.
\end{itemize}
Since the multipliers and $U_k$ live in the same array, \emph{swapping entire rows (columns $1$ through $m$)} performs all three updates at once, after which we eliminate as usual.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{LU factorization with partial pivoting, in place.}
  \label{alg:gepp}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $P \gets I$
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, m$}
      \State $r \gets \operatorname{argmax}_{i \ge k}\abs{A_{ik}}$
      \State swap rows $r$ and $k$ of $A$ (all columns $1, \dots, m$) and of $P$
      \For{$i = k + 1, \dots, m$}
        \State $A_{ik} \gets A_{ik}/A_{kk}$
      \EndFor
      \For{$j = k + 1, \dots, m$}
        \For{$i = k + 1, \dots, m$}
          \State $A_{ij} \gets A_{ij} - A_{ik}A_{kj}$
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Some remarks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In Julia, \texttt{argmax(abs.(A[k:end, k]))} returns an index into the subvector, so one adds $k - 1$ to recover the global row index.
  \item The row swap runs over all columns $1, \dots, m$, not just $k, \dots, m$: the first $k - 1$ columns hold multipliers, and swapping them is exactly $L'_j = P_{k+1}L_jP_{k+1}$.
  \item On exit the strictly lower part of $A$ holds $L$, the upper part holds $U$, and $PA = LU$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Storing $P$ and the Cost of Pivoting}

In practice $P$ is not stored as a matrix but as a permutation vector of length $m$ (LAPACK's \texttt{ipiv}), so each swap exchanges two integers. The search for pivots costs only $O(m^2)$ comparisons on top of the $\tfrac{2}{3}m^3$ flops. In column-major storage, however, a row swap is a strided access, and its cost is not negligible.

\subsection{Growth Factor}

\begin{definition}[Growth Factor]
  The \emph{growth factor} of Gaussian elimination on $A$ is
  \begin{equation}
    \rho \defeq \frac{\max_{i,j}\abs{U_{ij}}}{\max_{i,j}\abs{A_{ij}}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Why it matters.} With partial pivoting $\abs{L_{ik}} \le 1$, so each entry of $\abs{L}\abs{U}$ is a sum of at most $m$ terms, each at most $\max\abs{U_{ij}} = \rho\max\abs{A_{ij}}$:
\begin{equation}
  (\abs{L}\abs{U})_{ij} \le m\rho\max_{i,j}\abs{A_{ij}}
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \abs{E_{ij}} \le m^2\rho\,\epsm\max_{i,j}\abs{A_{ij}}
\end{equation}
by \Cref{thm:lu-backward}. So partial pivoting is backward stable exactly when $\rho$ stays small.

\paragraph{How large can $\rho$ be?} Each step updates $\tilde{A}_{ij} \gets \tilde{A}_{ij} - L_{ik}U_{kj}$ with $\abs{L_{ik}} \le 1$, so the largest entry can at most double. After $m - 1$ steps,
\begin{equation}
  \rho \le 2^{m-1}.
\end{equation}
This bound is attained by the matrix with $1$ on the diagonal, $-1$ below it, and $1$ in the last column (\Cref{ex:growth}, Homework~2): no rows are swapped, and the last column of $U$ is $1, 2, 4, \dots, 2^{m-1}$. For $m = 8$, Julia's \texttt{lu} gives $\rho = 128 = 2^7$.

\paragraph{In practice.} Such matrices are extremely rare. For random matrices $\rho$ grows slowly; with \texttt{randn} (median of $5$ runs) we measured $\rho \approx 2, 4, 8, 19$ for $m = 16, 64, 256, 1024$. This is why partial pivoting is used as a stable algorithm in practice, although in theory it is not backward stable.

\subsection{Complete Pivoting}

\paragraph{The idea.} At step $k$, \emph{complete pivoting} chooses the pivot to be the entry of largest absolute value in the whole trailing block, $\max_{i,j \ge k}\abs{\tilde{A}_{ij}}$, and moves it to position $(k, k)$ by swapping two rows \emph{and} two columns. This gives
\begin{equation}
  PAQ = LU,
\end{equation}
with a row permutation $P$ and a column permutation $Q$. Swapping columns reorders the unknowns, so $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ is solved as $LU(Q^\T\vec{x}) = P\vec{b}$.

\paragraph{Benefit.} Every entry of the trailing block is now at most the pivot, so the growth is much better controlled. Wilkinson showed a bound on $\rho$ that grows much more slowly than $2^{m-1}$, roughly like $m^{(\ln m)/4}$~\cite[Chapter 9]{Higham}.

\paragraph{Cost.} Step $k$ searches all $(m - k + 1)^2$ entries of the trailing block, about $m^3/3$ comparisons in total. That is the same order as the $\tfrac{2}{3}m^3$ flops, and the column swaps add strided memory access. Since partial pivoting is almost always good enough, complete pivoting is rarely used.

\paragraph{Example.} We ran complete pivoting (a short Julia implementation) on the worst-case matrix above and on random matrices, and compared with partial pivoting:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
  \toprule
  Matrix & $\rho$, partial pivoting & $\rho$, complete pivoting \\
  \midrule
  worst case, $m = 64$ & $2^{63} \approx 9 \cdot 10^{18}$ & $2$ \\
  \texttt{randn}, $m = 64$ & $4.2$ & $1.9$ \\
  \texttt{randn}, $m = 256$ & $7.8$ & $3.6$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
On the worst-case matrix, complete pivoting removes the growth entirely. On random matrices both are small, which is why the extra cost of complete pivoting is rarely worth it.

\paragraph{A compromise: rook pivoting.} Search alternately along a column and a row until the entry is the largest in both. This is cheap and controls growth much better~\cite[Chapter 9]{Higham}.
