\section{Recap and Motivation}

Last lecture (\Cref{ch:orth-iter}) showed that the Schur factorization is a diagonalization exactly for normal matrices, and introduced orthogonal iteration: power iteration on several vectors, kept orthonormal by a QR factorization. Now we prove that it converges to the Schur factorization, and rewrite it as an iteration on a single matrix.

The plan of this chapter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The link between the eigendecomposition and the Schur form: $X = QR$ (\Cref{sec:schur-eig}).
  \item $\spn(Q_k)$ converges to the span of the dominant eigenvectors (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}).
  \item With $Q_0 = I$, $Q_k^\ast AQ_k$ converges to the Schur form (\Cref{sec:conv-schur}).
  \item The same iterates come from ``factor as $QR$, multiply as $RQ$'': the basic QR iteration (\Cref{sec:basic-qr}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Schur and Eigendecomposition}
\label{sec:schur-eig}

In this lecture we consider
\begin{equation}
  A = X\Lambda X^{-1}, \qquad \abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} > \dots > \abs{\lambda_m}.
  \label{eq:qr-alg-assumption}
\end{equation}

This assumption is \emph{generic}: an arbitrarily small perturbation makes it true. Every $A$ has a Schur factorization $A = QTQ^\ast$ with the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $T$ (\Cref{thm:schur}). We add a small perturbation to $T$, and hence to $A$:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{A} \defeq Q\bigl(T + \diag(\eps_1, \dots, \eps_m)\bigr)Q^\ast = A + Q\diag(\eps_1, \dots, \eps_m)Q^\ast.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item This is a Schur factorization of $\tilde{A}$. So its eigenvalues are $t_{ii} + \eps_i$, and we can choose the $\eps_i$ so that they have distinct magnitude.
  \item The perturbation is small: $\norm{\tilde{A} - A}_2 = \max_i\abs{\eps_i}$, since $Q$ is unitary.
  \item Distinct eigenvalues imply diagonalizability. So $\tilde{A}$ satisfies \cref{eq:qr-alg-assumption}.
\end{itemize}
(For real matrices the situation is different; see \Cref{sec:qr-alg-real-generic}.)

Now, given $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$, how do we obtain the Schur form? We take a QR factorization of the eigenvector matrix:
\begin{equation}
  X = QR \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = Q\underbrace{R\Lambda R^{-1}}_{\text{upper triangular}}Q^\ast.
  \label{eq:schur-from-eig}
\end{equation}
$R$ and $R^{-1}$ are upper triangular, so $R\Lambda R^{-1}$ is too, with diagonal $r_{ii}\lambda_ir_{ii}^{-1} = \lambda_i$. So \cref{eq:schur-from-eig} is a Schur factorization, with the eigenvalues in the order of $\Lambda$.

Since $R$ is upper triangular and invertible, $\vec{x}_j$ is a combination of $\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_j$, and vice versa:
\begin{equation}
  \spn(\vec{q}_1, \dots, \vec{q}_j) = \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_j), \qquad j = 1, \dots, m.
  \label{eq:schur-nested}
\end{equation}
This needs $X$, so it is not an algorithm. But it shows the target: orthogonal iteration should converge to these nested subspaces.

\section{Convergence of Orthogonal Iteration}
\label{sec:orth-iter-conv}

How should we think about convergence of orthogonal iteration (\Cref{alg:orth-iter})? Let $Q_k \in \K^{m \times p}$ be the iterate after $k$ steps. Consider $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ diagonalizable, with the eigenvalues ordered by modulus and a gap after the $p$th one:
\begin{equation}
  \abs{\lambda_p} > \abs{\lambda_{p+1}}.
  \label{eq:orth-iter-gap}
\end{equation}
The QR step does not change the column space. So
\begin{equation}
  \spn(Q_{k+1}) = \spn(AQ_k) = \dots = \spn(A^{k+1}Q_0) = \spn(X\Lambda^{k+1}X^{-1}Q_0).
\end{equation}
We pull out the factor $\lambda_p^{k+1}$, which does not change the span:
\begin{equation}
  \spn(Q_{k+1}) = \spn\left(\lambda_p^{k+1}X\begin{bmatrix}
    (\lambda_1/\lambda_p)^{k+1} & & & & \\
    & \ddots & & & \\
    & & 1 & & \\
    & & & \ddots & \\
    & & & & (\lambda_m/\lambda_p)^{k+1}
  \end{bmatrix}X^{-1}Q_0\right).
\end{equation}
For $j \le p$, $\abs{\lambda_j/\lambda_p} \ge 1$; for $j > p$, $\abs{\lambda_j/\lambda_p} < 1$. So the last $m - p$ rows decay:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\Lambda/\lambda_p\right)^{k+1}X^{-1}Q_0 = \begin{bmatrix} V_k \\ W_k \end{bmatrix}, \qquad V_k \in \K^{p \times p}, \qquad W_k \longrightarrow 0.
\end{equation}
We split $X = [X_p\ \tilde{X}_p]$ the same way, with $X_p = [\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_p]$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \spn(Q_{k+1}) = \spn(X\Lambda^{k+1}X^{-1}Q_0) = \spn(X_pV_k + \tilde{X}_pW_k) \longrightarrow \spn(X_p).
  \label{eq:orth-iter-split}
\end{equation}
So $\spn(Q_k)$ converges to the invariant subspace of the $p$ dominant eigenvectors, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}$ (\Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv-precise}).

\section{Convergence to Schur Factorization}
\label{sec:conv-schur}

\begin{theorem}[Orthogonal Iteration Converges to Schur Form]
  \label{thm:orth-iter-schur}
  Consider orthogonal iteration (\Cref{alg:orth-iter}) on $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ with $p = m$ and $Q_0 = I$. If all eigenvalues of $A$ have distinct absolute values and the leading blocks $(X^{-1})_{1:j,1:j}$ have full rank for $j = 1, \dots, m$, then
  \begin{equation}
    T_k \defeq Q_k^\ast AQ_k
  \end{equation}
  converges to the Schur form of $A$.
\end{theorem}

The rank condition is on $X^{-1}$, not on $X$; a condition on $X_{1:j,1:j}$ is not enough in general (\Cref{sec:xinv-condition}).

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
  Fix $p < m$ and split $Q_k = [\bar{Q}_k\ \tilde{Q}_k]$ with $\bar{Q}_k \in \K^{m \times p}$. Then
  \begin{equation}
    T_k = Q_k^\ast AQ_k = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k & \bar{Q}_k^\ast A\tilde{Q}_k \\ \tilde{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k & \tilde{Q}_k^\ast A\tilde{Q}_k \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  The first $p$ columns of a QR factorization depend only on the first $p$ columns of the matrix. So $\bar{Q}_k$ is orthogonal iteration with $p$ columns, and by \Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv},
  \begin{equation}
    \spn(\bar{Q}_k) \longrightarrow \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_p)
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \spn(A\bar{Q}_k) \longrightarrow \spn(A[\vec{x}_1 \cdots \vec{x}_p]) = \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_p).
  \end{equation}
  So the columns of $A\bar{Q}_k$ lie (asymptotically) in $\spn(\bar{Q}_k)$, and $\tilde{Q}_k$ is orthogonal to it. Hence
  \begin{equation}
    \tilde{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k \longrightarrow 0.
  \end{equation}
  This is true for all subdiagonal blocks: every entry $(i, j)$ with $i > j$ lies in the lower left block for $p = j$. So
  \begin{equation}
    Q_k^\ast AQ_k \longrightarrow \text{upper triangular}. \qedhere
  \end{equation}
\end{proof}

The diagonal then holds the eigenvalues, ordered by decreasing modulus (\Cref{sec:conv-schur-diag}).

\section{Basic QR Iteration}
\label{sec:basic-qr}

Orthogonal iteration allows computing
\begin{equation}
  T_k \defeq Q_k^\ast AQ_k,
\end{equation}
which converges to the Schur factorization (\Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur}). But computing $T_{k+1}$ requires $Q_k$ and a matrix-matrix product with $A$. Can we derive an iteration $T_k \to T_{k+1}$?

Observe that
\begin{equation}
  T_k = Q_k^\ast AQ_k, \qquad T_{k+1} = Q_{k+1}^\ast AQ_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
By orthogonal iteration (with $Q_k$ square, so $Q_kQ_k^\ast = I$),
\begin{equation}
  Q_{k+1}R_{k+1} = AQ_k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R_{k+1}Q_k^\ast = Q_{k+1}^\ast A.
\end{equation}
We put the first form into $T_k$ and the second into $T_{k+1}$:
\begin{equation}
  T_k = Q_k^\ast(Q_{k+1}R_{k+1}), \qquad T_{k+1} = (R_{k+1}Q_k^\ast)Q_{k+1}.
  \label{eq:qr-iter-derivation}
\end{equation}
With $U_{k+1} \defeq Q_k^\ast Q_{k+1}$:
\begin{equation}
  T_k = U_{k+1}R_{k+1} \quad\xrightarrow{\ QR\ \to\ RQ\ }\quad T_{k+1} = R_{k+1}U_{k+1}.
  \label{eq:qr-iter-UR}
\end{equation}
$U_{k+1}$ is unitary and $R_{k+1}$ is upper triangular, so $T_k = U_{k+1}R_{k+1}$ is a QR factorization of $T_k$. Also, $T_{k+1} = U_{k+1}^\ast T_kU_{k+1}$, so every $T_k$ has the eigenvalues of $A$.

This gives a simple algorithm: compute a QR factorization and swap the order of the factors. With $Q_0 = I$ we start from $T_0 = A$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Basic QR iteration (QR algorithm without shifts).}
  \label{alg:basic-qr}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $A \in \K^{m \times m}$
    \State $T \gets A$
    \For{$k = 1, \dots, \mathrm{maxiter}$}
      \State $U, R \gets \mathrm{qr}(T)$
      \State $T \gets RU$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $\diag(T)$ \Comment{approximate eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$}
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{How to obtain $Q$?} By definition, $U_{k+1} = Q_k^\ast Q_{k+1}$. Implicitly $Q_0 = I$, so the product telescopes:
\begin{equation}
  U_1U_2\cdots U_{k+1} = Q_0^\ast Q_1\,Q_1^\ast Q_2\cdots Q_k^\ast Q_{k+1} = Q_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
So we start from $Q \gets I$ and update $Q \gets QU$ after each step. At the end, $A \approx QTQ^\ast$.

Close to convergence, $Q_{k+1} \approx Q_k$, so $U_{k+1} = Q_k^\ast Q_{k+1}$ is a small rotation (up to signs, \Cref{sec:qr-alg-phases}). The $U_k$ are a series of rotational corrections transforming $I$ to $Q$.

\subsection{Real Matrices: Distinct Moduli Are Not Generic}
\label{sec:qr-alg-real-generic}

The perturbation in \Cref{sec:schur-eig} is complex in general. For real $A$, non-real eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs $\lambda, \overline{\lambda}$ with $\abs{\lambda} = \abs{\overline{\lambda}}$, and a small real perturbation keeps them a pair. So among real matrices, \cref{eq:qr-alg-assumption} fails on an open set, for example near every rotation of $\R^2$ by an angle $\theta \notin \{0, \pi\}$. Also, eigenvalues depend continuously on the matrix, so in the complex case the strict inequalities survive small perturbations of $\tilde{A}$.

\subsection{The Convergence Rate of the Subspace}
\label{sec:orth-iter-conv-precise}

Write $B \defeq X^{-1}Q_0 = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with $B_1 \in \K^{p \times p}$, and $\Lambda = \diag(\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2)$ with $\Lambda_1 = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p)$. Note $\lambda_p \ne 0$ by \cref{eq:orth-iter-gap}. Then
\begin{equation}
  V_k = (\Lambda_1/\lambda_p)^{k+1}B_1, \qquad W_k = (\Lambda_2/\lambda_p)^{k+1}B_2.
\end{equation}
Assume $B_1$ is invertible: $Q_0$ has a component in every direction $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_p$. This is the analogue of $\vec{x}_1^\ast\vec{q}^{(0)} \ne 0$ for power iteration, and it holds with probability one for random $Q_0$. Then
\begin{equation}
  \spn(X_pV_k + \tilde{X}_pW_k) = \spn\bigl(X_p + \tilde{X}_pW_kV_k^{-1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and since $V_k^{-1} = B_1^{-1}(\lambda_p\Lambda_1^{-1})^{k+1}$ has $\norm{V_k^{-1}} \le \norm{B_1^{-1}}$,
\begin{equation}
  \norm{\tilde{X}_pW_kV_k^{-1}} \le \norm{\tilde{X}_p}\norm{B_2}\norm{B_1^{-1}}\abs{\frac{\lambda_{p+1}}{\lambda_p}}^{k+1} \longrightarrow 0.
\end{equation}
So the rate is the gap between the last eigenvalue we keep and the first one we drop.

\subsection{Why the Condition Is on $X^{-1}$}
\label{sec:xinv-condition}

The argument of \Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv-precise} needs $B_1 = (X^{-1}Q_0)_{1:j,1:j} = (X^{-1})_{1:j,1:j}$ invertible. Asking that $X_{1:j,1:j}$ be invertible is not enough. Take
\begin{equation}
  X = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad X^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here $X_{11} = 1$ and $X$ are nonsingular, but $(X^{-1})_{11} = 0$. The starting vector $\vec{e}_1 = \vec{x}_2$ is an eigenvector with no component along $\vec{x}_1$, and in exact arithmetic $\vec{q}_1^{(k)} = \vec{x}_2$ forever.

For normal $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$ the two conditions agree: $X^{-1} = Q^\ast$ and $(Q^\ast)_{1:j,1:j} = (Q_{1:j,1:j})^\ast$. This is why Trefethen and Bau, who treat real symmetric $A$, can state the condition with the leading blocks of $Q$~\cite[Thm.~28.4]{TB}. Some books state it with $X_{1:j,1:j}$ for general $A$ (e.g., \cite[Thm.~4.8]{Demmel}).

\subsection{The Diagonal of $T_k$}
\label{sec:conv-schur-diag}

In the proof of \Cref{thm:orth-iter-schur}, $\bar{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k$ is the matrix of $A$ restricted to $\spn(\bar{Q}_k) \to \spn(\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_p)$. So its eigenvalues tend to $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p$, as in \Cref{prop:orth-iter-2x2}. This block becomes upper triangular, so its diagonal holds these eigenvalues. Comparing $p$ with $p - 1$ shows that $(T_k)_{pp} \to \lambda_p$.

A precise version of ``the columns of $A\bar{Q}_k$ lie in $\spn(\bar{Q}_k)$'': since $\tilde{Q}_k^\ast\bar{Q}_k = 0$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{Q}_k^\ast A\bar{Q}_k = \tilde{Q}_k^\ast(I - \bar{Q}_k\bar{Q}_k^\ast)A\bar{Q}_k,
\end{equation}
and $(I - \bar{Q}_k\bar{Q}_k^\ast)A\bar{Q}_k$, the part of $A\bar{Q}_k$ outside $\spn(\bar{Q}_k)$, tends to zero. This uses $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p \ne 0$.

\subsection{Sign Choices and the Phases of $U_k$}
\label{sec:qr-alg-phases}

A library QR routine may not return the same factors as orthogonal iteration. For invertible $T$, QR factors are unique only up to signs (phases) of the columns of $U$ and the rows of $R$ (\Cref{ch:qr}). Every choice gives $T_{k+1} = U^\ast T_kU$, and different choices give iterates that differ by a diagonal unitary similarity $D^\ast T_kD$. This changes neither the diagonal nor the moduli of the entries.

Near convergence, $T_k$ is nearly upper triangular, so $U_{k+1} = T_kR_{k+1}^{-1}$ is nearly upper triangular. Being unitary, it is nearly diagonal: $U_{k+1} \approx D$. With $r_{ii} > 0$, $D \approx \diag(\lambda_1/\abs{\lambda_1}, \dots, \lambda_m/\abs{\lambda_m})$. So a negative eigenvalue flips the sign of its column of $Q_k$ in every step, as in power iteration.

\subsection{The QR Algorithm Factors the Powers of $A$}

Let $\underline{R}_k \defeq R_kR_{k-1}\cdots R_1$. Then
\begin{equation}
  A^k = Q_k\underline{R}_k, \qquad T_k = Q_k^\ast AQ_k, \qquad Q_k = U_1U_2\cdots U_k.
  \label{eq:qr-alg-powers}
\end{equation}
The first identity follows by induction: $A^k = A\,Q_{k-1}\underline{R}_{k-1} = Q_kR_k\underline{R}_{k-1} = Q_k\underline{R}_k$. So the QR algorithm computes a QR factorization of $A^k$ without forming $A^k$, whose columns would all be nearly parallel to $\vec{x}_1$. The two identities in \cref{eq:qr-alg-powers} explain the algorithm~\cite[Lecture~28]{TB}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The leading $j$ columns of $Q_k$ are an orthonormal basis of the span of the leading $j$ columns of $A^k$: simultaneous power iteration on $\vec{e}_1, \dots, \vec{e}_m$.
  \item The diagonal entries $(T_k)_{jj} = \vec{q}_j^{(k)\ast}A\vec{q}_j^{(k)}$ are Rayleigh quotients. So for Hermitian $A$ they converge twice as fast as the columns of $Q_k$ (\Cref{ch:eigen}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Convergence Rates of Individual Entries}

By \Cref{sec:orth-iter-conv}, the block $(T_k)_{p+1:m,1:p}$ decays like $\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}^k$. In particular:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the subdiagonal entry $(T_k)_{j+1,j}$ decays like $\abs{\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_j}^k$;
  \item the diagonal entry $(T_k)_{pp}$ converges to $\lambda_p$ like $\max(\abs{\lambda_{p+1}/\lambda_p}^k, \abs{\lambda_p/\lambda_{p-1}}^k)$~\cite[\S4.4.3]{Demmel}.
\end{itemize}
The overall rate is the worst ratio $\max_j\abs{\lambda_{j+1}/\lambda_j}$~\cite[Thm.~28.4]{TB}. If two eigenvalues have nearly equal modulus, the unshifted algorithm becomes arbitrarily slow.

For example, take $\Lambda = \diag(30, 6, 2, 1)$ and a random $X$ with $\kappa(X) \approx 20$. After $19$ steps, the subdiagonal entries are about $7 \cdot 10^{-13}$, $1 \cdot 10^{-9}$, and $5 \cdot 10^{-6}$~\cite[Example~4.5]{Demmel}. This agrees with $(1/5)^{19} \approx 5 \cdot 10^{-14}$, $(1/3)^{19} \approx 9 \cdot 10^{-10}$, and $(1/2)^{19} \approx 2 \cdot 10^{-6}$, up to constants.

The most important block is the last row, $(T_k)_{m,1:m-1}$, which decays like $\abs{\lambda_m/\lambda_{m-1}}^k$. Once it is below a tolerance such as $\epsm\norm{A}$, we accept $(T_k)_{mm}$ as an eigenvalue and continue on the leading $(m-1) \times (m-1)$ block. This is deflation.

\subsection{Eigenvalues of Equal Modulus}

Without a gap $\abs{\lambda_p} > \abs{\lambda_{p+1}}$, the block at the split after column $p$ need not decay. The extreme case is a unitary $A$: all its eigenvalues have modulus $1$. Its QR factorization is $A = A \cdot I$, so $T_1 = I \cdot A = A$, and the iteration does not move~\cite[Example~4.6]{Demmel}. An example is $A = \left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$, with eigenvalues $\pm 1$.

For real $A$, the generic case is a pair of complex conjugate eigenvalues (\Cref{sec:qr-alg-real-generic}). All $U_k$, $R_k$, $T_k$ are real, so $T_k$ cannot converge to a triangular matrix with non-real diagonal entries. Splits between eigenvalues of different moduli still have a gap, so those blocks still decay. If the only ties in modulus come from conjugate pairs, $T_k$ converges to the real Schur form (\Cref{thm:real-schur}). The entries of each $2 \times 2$ block keep changing, but its eigenvalues converge to $\lambda, \overline{\lambda}$.
