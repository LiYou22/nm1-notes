\section{From Elimination to Similarity Transformations}

\Cref{ch:cholesky} factored an s.p.d.\ matrix as $A = LL^\ast$ by symmetric elimination $A \mapsto L_1AL_1^\ast$, showed that no pivoting is needed and that there is no element growth, and related the steps of elimination to Schur complements (\Cref{lem:schur-inverse}). In the board notation of this lecture, eigenvalues were written $\delta$; we write $\lambda$ throughout.

All factorizations so far rest on \emph{elimination} or \emph{projection}. LU and Cholesky are Gaussian elimination, which uses lower triangular matrices to zero out the entries below the pivots column by column. In QR, Gram--Schmidt subtracts from each column its projections onto the previous ones, and Householder uses reflections $I - 2\vec{v}\vec{v}^\ast$ to map a column onto the direction of $\vec{e}_1$. These factorizations share two features.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Their purpose is to \emph{solve equations}. The transformations used are one-sided ($A \mapsto LA$, $A \mapsto Q^\ast A$) or congruences ($A \mapsto LAL^\ast$), and they do not preserve eigenvalues. This is why the pivots of Cholesky are not the eigenvalues: a congruence is not a similarity.
  \item They finish in finitely many steps, about $O(m^3)$, and are exact if there is no rounding.
\end{enumerate}

The four factorizations below are all closely tied to the \emph{eigenpairs} $(\lambda_i, \vec{x}_i)$ of a matrix. To expose the structure of $A$ as a linear map, we must use eigenvalue-preserving \emph{similarity transformations} $A \mapsto X^{-1}AX$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth}@{}}
  \toprule
  Factorization & Form & Exists for & Transformation \\
  \midrule
  Eigen & $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ & diagonalizable (nondefective) $A$ & general $X$, possibly ill-conditioned \\
  Polar & $A = QP$, $P = (A^\ast A)^{1/2} \succeq 0$ & every square $A$ & $Q$ unitary \\
  Schur & $A = QTQ^\ast$, $T$ upper triangular & every square $A$ & $Q$ unitary \\
  SVD & $A = U\Sigma V^\ast$ & every $A$ & $U, V$ unitary \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Only the eigendecomposition may fail to exist. The other three hold for every (square) matrix and use only unitary transformations, which have condition number $1$ and do not amplify errors. A \emph{normal} matrix ($A^\ast A = AA^\ast$, which includes Hermitian matrices) can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$.

The polar decomposition (\Cref{thm:polar}) is the matrix version of $z = e^{i\theta}r$: $Q$ is a ``rotation or reflection'' and $P$ is a ``stretch''. It follows directly from the SVD,
\begin{equation}
  A = U\Sigma V^\ast = (UV^\ast)(V\Sigma V^\ast), \qquad Q = UV^\ast, \quad P = V\Sigma V^\ast.
\end{equation}
The factor $Q$ is the unitary matrix closest to $A$ in the Frobenius norm (the orthogonal Procrustes problem). Note that the Schur \emph{decomposition} has nothing directly to do with the Schur \emph{complement} of \Cref{ch:cholesky}.

\section{Eigenpairs of Hermitian Matrices}

\paragraph{Problem.} Given a Hermitian $A \in \K^{m \times m}$, find $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m \in \R$ and $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m \in \K^m$ with
\begin{equation}
  A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation}
Recall that $\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \vec{x}^\ast\vec{y}$ is conjugate-linear in its first argument.

\subsection{Real Eigenvalues and the Rayleigh Quotient}

\Cref{prop:herm-eig} showed that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are real. Written with inner products, the argument is the chain
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i = \frac{\ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_i}}{\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2} = \frac{\ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_i}}{\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2} = \overline{\lambda_i}.
\end{equation}
The first equality is $\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\lambda_i\vec{x}_i} = \lambda_i\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2$; the second uses $A = A^\ast$; the third uses conjugate symmetry, $\ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_i} = \overline{\ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_i}}$. The first expression makes sense for any nonzero vector.

\begin{definition}[Rayleigh Quotient]
  \label{def:rayleigh}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times m}$. The \emph{Rayleigh quotient} of a nonzero $\vec{x} \in \K^m$ is
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{x}) \doteq \frac{\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x}}{\vec{x}^\ast\vec{x}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

If $A$ is Hermitian, the Rayleigh quotient is always real, and at an eigenvector it equals the eigenvalue.

\subsection{Orthogonality of Eigenvectors}

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal Eigenvectors]
  \label{prop:herm-orth}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian, $A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i$ and $A\vec{x}_j = \lambda_j\vec{x}_j$ with $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j$. Then $\vec{x}_i \perp \vec{x}_j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  We have
  \begin{equation}
    \lambda_i\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = \ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = \ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_j} = \lambda_j\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j}.
  \end{equation}
  The first equality is $\ip{\lambda_i\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = \overline{\lambda_i}\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j}$ together with $\overline{\lambda_i} = \lambda_i$ (\Cref{prop:herm-eig}); the second uses $A = A^\ast$. Thus $(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = 0$, and since $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j$, $\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = 0$.
\end{proof}

The proof uses realness of the eigenvalues, so the two facts must be proved in this order. Within the eigenspace of a repeated eigenvalue we can orthonormalize by Gram--Schmidt, so orthogonality is never the problem. What has \emph{not} yet been proved is \emph{existence}: why a Hermitian matrix has $m$ linearly independent eigenvectors, i.e., the spectral theorem $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$. The standard route is to prove the Schur decomposition $A = QTQ^\ast$ for every square matrix, and then note that $T = Q^\ast AQ$ is both Hermitian and upper triangular, hence diagonal.

\section{Eigenvalues Cannot Be Computed in Finitely Many Steps}

LU and its relatives compute their results exactly in finitely many steps, if there is no rounding. Eigenvalues cannot be computed this way. The obstruction comes from algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Abel--Ruffini]
  \label{thm:abel}
  For every $m \ge 5$, there is a polynomial of degree $m$ with rational coefficients whose roots cannot be expressed in terms of its coefficients using finitely many operations $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, and $k$-th roots.
\end{theorem}

To carry this statement about polynomials over to matrices, we need a reduction: for every polynomial, a matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots of the polynomial.

\begin{lemma}[Companion Matrix]
  \label{lem:companion}
  Let $p(z) = z^m + a_{m-1}z^{m-1} + \cdots + a_1z + a_0$ be monic, and let
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix}
      0 & 1 & & & \\
      & 0 & 1 & & \\
      & & \ddots & \ddots & \\
      & & & 0 & 1 \\
      -a_0 & -a_1 & \cdots & -a_{m-2} & -a_{m-1}
    \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ z \\ z^2 \\ \vdots \\ z^{m-1} \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  If $p(z) = 0$, then $A\vec{x} = z\vec{x}$, so $z$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  The first $m - 1$ rows of $A$ pick out the next entry of $\vec{x}$: $(A\vec{x})_i = x_{i+1} = z^i = z \cdot z^{i-1} = zx_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, m - 1$. The last row gives
  \begin{equation}
    (A\vec{x})_m = -\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_iz^i = z^m - \underbrace{p(z)}_{=0} = z \cdot z^{m-1} = zx_m.
  \end{equation}
  Finally $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$ since $x_1 = 1$.
\end{proof}

Expanding $\det(zI - A)$ along the last row shows more: $\det(zI - A) = p(z)$, so the eigenvalues of $A$ are exactly the roots of $p$, counted with multiplicity. If $p$ is not monic, divide by the leading coefficient $a_m$ first; the last row is then $-a_i/a_m$.

\begin{theorem}[Eigenvalue Algorithms Must Be Iterative]
  \label{thm:eig-iterative}
  For $m \ge 5$, there is no algorithm that computes the eigenvalues of every $m \times m$ matrix exactly in finitely many operations $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, and $k$-th roots.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Suppose such an algorithm exists. Given any monic polynomial $p$ of degree $m$, form its companion matrix; this needs no arithmetic at all, since its entries are $0$, $1$, and the coefficients $-a_i$. By \Cref{lem:companion} and the remark after it, the algorithm applied to this matrix returns the roots of $p$ in finitely many operations. This contradicts \Cref{thm:abel}.
\end{proof}

So every eigenvalue algorithm must be \emph{iterative}: it can only produce a sequence converging to the eigenvalues.

\paragraph{The direction of the reduction.} The familiar direction ``matrix $\to$ characteristic polynomial $\to$ roots'' only shows that the eigenvalue problem is no harder than root finding; it proves no impossibility. To show that the eigenvalue problem is \emph{at least as hard} as root finding, we need the opposite direction: every polynomial can be disguised as an eigenvalue problem. The disguise must also be free. The companion matrix is built from $0$, $1$, and the coefficients without any computation; using $\diag(z_1, \dots, z_m)$ instead would require knowing the roots, which is circular. The structure of the companion matrix comes from the linear map ``multiply by $z$'' on the quotient space $\C[z]/(p)$ in the basis $1, z, \dots, z^{m-1}$: the superdiagonal ones move each basis vector to the next, and the last row uses $p(z) = 0$ to rewrite the overflowing $z^m$ in terms of lower powers.

The conclusion also holds for Hermitian matrices: for a polynomial whose roots are all real, a Sturm sequence (built with arithmetic operations and square roots) gives a real symmetric tridiagonal matrix with that characteristic polynomial.

\paragraph{Iterative does not mean slow.} The standard approach has two phases. First, finitely many unitary similarity transformations reduce $A$ to Hessenberg form (tridiagonal if $A$ is Hermitian); this phase is a direct method. Then an iteration runs on the reduced matrix and converges very fast. The total cost is still $O(m^3)$. In the opposite direction, \texttt{roots} in MATLAB and NumPy finds the roots of a polynomial by forming its companion matrix and calling an eigenvalue solver.

\section{Power Iteration}

\subsection{Multiplying by $A$ in the Eigenbasis}

Let $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ be diagonalizable. Write any vector as a combination of eigenvectors, $\vec{z} = X\vec{y} = \sum_i y_i\vec{x}_i$, where $\vec{y} = X^{-1}\vec{z}$ holds the coordinates of $\vec{z}$ in the eigenbasis. Then
\begin{equation}
  A\vec{z} = X\Lambda\vec{y}, \qquad A^2\vec{z} = X\Lambda^2\vec{y}, \qquad A^k\vec{z} = X\Lambda^k\vec{y} = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^k y_i\vec{x}_i.
\end{equation}
In the eigenbasis, multiplication by $A$ multiplies each coordinate by its own eigenvalue, and the directions do not interact.

Assume that the largest eigenvalue strictly dominates, $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \cdots \ge \abs{\lambda_m}$. Factoring out $\lambda_1^k$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:power-expand}
  A^k\vec{z} = \lambda_1^k\left(y_1\vec{x}_1 + \sum_{i=2}^m \left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k y_i\vec{x}_i\right).
\end{equation}
Since $(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k \to 0$, the \emph{direction} of $A^k\vec{z}$ approaches $\vec{x}_1$. The approximations $\Lambda^k \approx \lambda_1^k\diag(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $A^k\vec{z} \approx \lambda_1^ky_1\vec{x}_1$ hold in the relative sense, $\Lambda^k/\lambda_1^k \to \diag(1, 0, \dots, 0)$; the vector $A^k\vec{z}$ itself may blow up or vanish.

\subsection{The Condition $y_1 \ne 0$}

The coordinate $y_1$ is the inner product of the first row of $X^{-1}$ with $\vec{z}$. From $X^{-1}A = \Lambda X^{-1}$, the $i$-th row $\vec{w}_i^\ast$ of $X^{-1}$ satisfies $\vec{w}_i^\ast A = \lambda_i\vec{w}_i^\ast$; it is a \emph{left eigenvector}. Hence
\begin{equation}
  y_1 \ne 0 \iff \ip{\vec{w}_1}{\vec{z}} \ne 0,
\end{equation}
i.e., the starting vector must not be orthogonal to the left eigenvector of $\lambda_1$. If $A$ is Hermitian, $X^{-1} = Q^\ast$, the left and right eigenvectors coincide, and the condition says that $\vec{z}$ is not orthogonal to $\vec{x}_1$.

If $y_1 = 0$, then in exact arithmetic the direction $\vec{x}_1$ never appears, and the iteration converges to $\vec{x}_j$ for the first $j$ with $y_j \ne 0$, without noticing. In practice, a random starting vector lies on the hyperplane $\vec{w}_1^\perp$ with probability $0$; and even if it does, rounding errors introduce an $O(\epsm)$ component along $\vec{x}_1$, which is then amplified step by step.

\subsection{The Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Power iteration.}
  \label{alg:power}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ vector with i.i.d.\ standard normal entries; $\vec{q} \gets \vec{q}/\norm{\vec{q}}$
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots$}
      \State $\vec{z} \gets A\vec{q}$
      \State $\lambda \gets \vec{q}^\ast\vec{z}$ \Comment{Rayleigh quotient, reuses $\vec{z}$}
      \If{$\norm{\vec{z} - \lambda\vec{q}} \le \mathrm{tol} \cdot \abs{\lambda}$}
        \State \Return $\lambda, \vec{q}$
      \EndIf
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A standard normal vector has a rotation-invariant distribution, so after normalization it is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere and satisfies $y_1 \ne 0$ almost surely. Line 4 must come before line 8, because $\vec{z} = A\vec{q}$ belongs to the old $\vec{q}$. In the complex case the order $\vec{q}^\ast\vec{z}$ matters: $\vec{z}^\ast\vec{q} = \overline{\vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}}$, which agrees only when $\vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$ is real. Each step costs one matrix-vector product, $O(m^2)$ for a dense matrix and $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ for a sparse one, so power iteration suits large matrices that we can only multiply by (PageRank, for example).

\paragraph{Why normalize.} $\norm{A^k\vec{z}}$ changes like $\abs{\lambda_1}^k$, while double precision covers roughly $10^{\pm 308}$. With $\abs{\lambda_1} = 10$ the iterates overflow to \texttt{Inf} after about $300$ steps; with $\abs{\lambda_1} = 0.1$ they underflow to $0$. The number of steps needed for convergence is governed by $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$, independently of $\abs{\lambda_1}$, so overflow or underflow would almost always come first, and normalizing only at the end is too late. Normalization does not change the result: $A(c\vec{v}) = c \cdot A\vec{v}$, so scaling at each step does not affect later directions, and in exact arithmetic $\vec{q}^{(k)} = A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}/\norm{A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}}$. As a bonus, the Rayleigh quotient needs no division by $\norm{\vec{q}}^2$, and the stopping test is not affected by scaling.

\paragraph{Why the residual.} The stopping test uses the residual $\norm{A\vec{q} - \lambda\vec{q}}$ and not $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - \vec{q}^{(k-1)}}$: if $\lambda_1 < 0$ (or is complex), $\vec{q}$ changes sign (is multiplied by a phase) at every step, so the direction converges but the vector does not. For Hermitian matrices the residual also bounds the eigenvalue error directly.

\begin{proposition}[Residual Bound]
  \label{prop:residual-bound}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian, $\vec{q}$ a unit vector, and $\lambda \in \R$. Then $A$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ with
  \begin{equation}
    \abs{\lambda_j - \lambda} \le \norm{A\vec{q} - \lambda\vec{q}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  Expand $\vec{q} = \sum_j c_j\vec{x}_j$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis, so that $\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = 1$. Then $A\vec{q} - \lambda\vec{q} = \sum_j(\lambda_j - \lambda)c_j\vec{x}_j$, and
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{A\vec{q} - \lambda\vec{q}}^2 = \sum_j\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda}^2\abs{c_j}^2 \ge \min_j\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda}^2\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = \min_j\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda}^2,
  \end{equation}
  as desired.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Rate of Convergence}

The key to the eigenvalue estimate is that the Rayleigh quotient of a Hermitian matrix is \emph{quadratically} accurate.

\begin{lemma}[Quadratic Accuracy of the Rayleigh Quotient]
  \label{lem:rayleigh-quad}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$, and let $\vec{q}$ be a unit vector. Then for every $J$,
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J = \sum_{j \ne J}(\lambda_j - \lambda_J)\abs{c_j}^2, \qquad c_j = \ip{\vec{x}_j}{\vec{q}},
  \end{equation}
  and hence $\abs{r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J} \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Since $\vec{q} = \sum_j c_j\vec{x}_j$ and $\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = 1$,
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{q}) = \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q} = \sum_j\lambda_j\abs{c_j}^2 = \lambda_J\underbrace{\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2}_{=1} + \sum_{j \ne J}(\lambda_j - \lambda_J)\abs{c_j}^2.
  \end{equation}
  For the bound, $\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda_J} \le \lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}$, and
  \begin{equation}
    \sum_{j \ne J}\abs{c_j}^2 = \norm{\vec{q} - c_J\vec{x}_J}^2 \le \norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}^2,
  \end{equation}
  since $c_J\vec{x}_J$ is the orthogonal projection of $\vec{q}$ onto $\spn(\vec{x}_J)$, the closest point of that line to $\vec{q}$.
\end{proof}

The right side is quadratic in the coefficients $c_j$, which are themselves the vector error. More generally, the Rayleigh quotient of a Hermitian matrix is stationary at eigenvectors: in the real case $\nabla r(\vec{x}) = \frac{2}{\vec{x}^\T\vec{x}}(A\vec{x} - r(\vec{x})\vec{x})$, which vanishes exactly at eigenvectors. An $\eps$ error in the vector therefore moves the Rayleigh quotient only by $O(\eps^2)$.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Power Iteration]
  \label{thm:power}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$ and eigenvalues $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \cdots \ge \abs{\lambda_m}$, and let the unit starting vector satisfy $c_1 = \ip{\vec{x}_1}{\vec{q}^{(0)}} \ne 0$. Then there are unimodular scalars $s_k$ ($s_k = \pm 1$ in the real case) such that the iterates of \Cref{alg:power} satisfy
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1} = O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^k\right), \qquad \abs{r(\vec{q}^{(k)}) - \lambda_1} = O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^{2k}\right).
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  By \eqref{eq:power-expand} with $y_j = c_j$, $A^k\vec{q}^{(0)} = \lambda_1^kc_1(\vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k)$, where
  \begin{equation}
    \vec{e}_k = \sum_{j=2}^m\left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^k\frac{c_j}{c_1}\vec{x}_j, \qquad
    \norm{\vec{e}_k}^2 = \sum_{j=2}^m\abs{\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}}^{2k}\frac{\abs{c_j}^2}{\abs{c_1}^2} \le \abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^{2k}\frac{1}{\abs{c_1}^2},
  \end{equation}
  using $\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = 1$. Let $s_k = \lambda_1^kc_1/\abs{\lambda_1^kc_1}$ and $\vec{u} = \vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k$, so that $\vec{q}^{(k)} = s_k\vec{u}/\norm{\vec{u}}$. Since $\norm{\vec{x}_1} = 1$,
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\frac{\vec{u}}{\norm{\vec{u}}} - \vec{x}_1} \le \norm{\frac{\vec{u}}{\norm{\vec{u}}} - \vec{u}} + \norm{\vec{u} - \vec{x}_1} = \underbrace{\abs{1 - \norm{\vec{u}}}}_{\le\,\norm{\vec{u} - \vec{x}_1}} + \norm{\vec{u} - \vec{x}_1} \le 2\norm{\vec{e}_k},
  \end{equation}
  so $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1} \le 2\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k/\abs{c_1}$. For the eigenvalue, $s_k\vec{x}_1$ is a unit eigenvector for $\lambda_1$, so \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad} gives
  \begin{equation}
    \abs{r(\vec{q}^{(k)}) - \lambda_1} \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1}^2 \le \frac{4(\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})}{\abs{c_1}^2}\abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^{2k},
  \end{equation}
  as desired.
\end{proof}

For a diagonalizable non-Hermitian $A$, the first estimate still holds, with a constant that also depends on the condition number of $X$; the second does not. The eigenvectors are then not orthogonal, the expansion of $r(\vec{q})$ contains cross terms linear in the $c_j$, and the eigenvalue error is only $O(\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k)$.

Linear convergence to an error $\eps$ takes about $k \approx \log\eps/\log\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$ steps. For a vector accuracy of $10^{-8}$, this is about $27$ steps when the ratio is $0.5$, $175$ when it is $0.9$, and $1800$ when it is $0.99$.

\subsection{When $\lambda_2 \approx \lambda_1$: The Eigenvalue Still Converges}

Suppose $\abs{\lambda_1} \sim \abs{\lambda_2} \gg \abs{\lambda_3}$. In the space spanned by $\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \vec{x}_3$, the iterates move in two phases. They first fall quickly into the plane $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2)$, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_1}^k$, and then turn slowly toward $\vec{x}_1$ within the plane, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k$.

After the first phase, however, $\vec{q} \approx c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\vec{x}_2$, and the Rayleigh quotient $\lambda_1\abs{c_1}^2 + \lambda_2\abs{c_2}^2$ is a weighted average of $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. Its error is at most $\abs{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}$, which is already small. In the formula $\sum_{j \ge 2}(\lambda_j - \lambda_1)\abs{c_j}^2$ of \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad}, directions far from $\lambda_1$ have $\abs{c_j}^2$ decaying quickly, while directions close to $\lambda_1$ decay slowly but have a small lever $\lambda_j - \lambda_1$. One of the two factors is always small.

In the extreme case $\lambda_2 = \lambda_1$, $\vec{q}$ converges to some vector in $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2)$ determined by the starting vector. Every nonzero vector in this eigenspace is an eigenvector for $\lambda_1$, so the algorithm in fact succeeds, and the eigenvalue converges at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_1}$.

This matches the conditioning of the problem. Eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices are always well-conditioned (Weyl: $\abs{\delta\lambda} \le \norm{\delta A}_2$), whereas the sensitivity of an eigenvector is about $\norm{\delta A}/\mathrm{gap}$. When the gap is small, $\vec{x}_1$ itself is ill-conditioned.

\subsection{When $\lambda_2 \approx -\lambda_1$: The Real Failure}

Now only the plane matters, $\vec{q} = c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\vec{x}_2$, and one multiplication by $A$ gives $\lambda_1(c_1\vec{x}_1 - c_2\vec{x}_2)$. Geometrically this is a \emph{reflection across the $\vec{x}_1$ axis}. The iterates jump back and forth between two directions symmetric about $\vec{x}_1$; if $\lambda_2 = -\lambda_1$ exactly, $\abs{c_2/c_1}$ never changes and there is no convergence at all. The Rayleigh quotient
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q} \approx \lambda_1\left(\abs{c_1}^2 - \abs{c_2}^2\right)
\end{equation}
is a value determined by the starting vector, which may lie anywhere in $[-\lambda_1, \lambda_1]$.

\begin{center}

\section{Functions of Matrices: Manipulating the Spectrum}

The question becomes: how can we change the spectrum of a matrix so that the eigenvalue we want becomes the one that power iteration finds most easily? For every polynomial $p$, $p(A)\vec{x}_i = p(\lambda_i)\vec{x}_i$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  Transformation & Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors \\
  \midrule
  $A \to A^k$ & $\lambda \to \lambda^k$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to \alpha A$ & $\lambda \to \alpha\lambda$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to A + \beta I$ & $\lambda \to \lambda + \beta$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to p(A)$ & $\lambda \to p(\lambda)$ & unchanged \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The key is the last column: power iteration on $p(A)$ produces eigenvectors of $A$, from which the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ reads off the eigenvalues of $A$. Power iteration itself filters the spectrum with $p(z) = z^k$ (``polynomial filtering''), amplifying eigenvalues of large modulus and damping small ones; a different $p$ filters differently. Scaling by $\alpha$ does not change the ratios and acts like normalization; the useful operation is the shift.

\paragraph{Shifting.} Suppose a cluster of eigenvalues sits at the left end of the negative axis and an isolated eigenvalue at the right end of the positive axis, with similar moduli; then the ratio for plain power iteration is close to $1$. Iterating with $A + I$ shifts the whole spectrum to the right: the cluster moves toward the origin and the isolated eigenvalue moves farther out, so the ratio drops. In general, let $A$ have real eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_m$, ordered by value, and let $\lambda_1$ be the one we want. Power iteration on $A + \beta I$ converges at the rate $\max_{j \ge 2}\abs{\lambda_j + \beta}/\abs{\lambda_1 + \beta}$. The best shift moves the midpoint of $[\lambda_m, \lambda_2]$ to the origin, $\beta = -\frac{\lambda_2 + \lambda_m}{2}$, and gives the ratio
\begin{equation}
  \frac{(\lambda_2 - \lambda_m)/2}{\lambda_1 - \frac{\lambda_2 + \lambda_m}{2}}.
\end{equation}
For larger $\beta$ the ratio is $(\lambda_2 + \beta)/(\lambda_1 + \beta)$, which increases with $\beta$; for smaller $\beta$ it is $-(\lambda_m + \beta)/(\lambda_1 + \beta)$, which decreases with $\beta$. The case $\lambda_2 \approx -\lambda_1$ of the previous section is solved in the same way: with $\lambda_1 > 0$ and any $\beta > 0$, the ratio becomes $\abs{\beta - \lambda_1}/(\beta + \lambda_1) < 1$.

A shift can only translate the spectrum, and choosing it requires rough knowledge of where the spectrum lies. Higher-degree polynomials $p$ can be designed to be large at $\lambda_1$ and uniformly small on the interval containing the rest of the spectrum (Chebyshev acceleration). Replacing polynomials by rational functions leads to inverse iteration.

\section{Inverse Iteration}

Suppose we want an eigenvalue in the \emph{middle} of the spectrum: the one closest to a given $\mu \in \R$. Take
\begin{equation}
  f(z) = \frac{1}{z - \mu}, \qquad (A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{x}_j = \frac{1}{\lambda_j - \mu}\vec{x}_j.
\end{equation}
The eigenvalue $\lambda_J$ closest to $\mu$ is mapped to $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$, which has the largest modulus; the second closest is mapped a bit closer to the origin; the far ones are all squeezed toward the origin (those with $\lambda_j < \mu$ onto the negative axis, those with $\lambda_j > \mu$ onto the positive axis).

\small The map $\lambda \mapsto 1/(\lambda - \mu)$ sends the eigenvalue closest to $\mu$ far away and squeezes all the others toward $0$.
\end{center}

Power iteration on $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$ therefore finds $\vec{x}_J$. Let $\lambda_K$ be the second closest eigenvalue to $\mu$.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Inverse Iteration]
  \label{thm:inverse-iter}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian, let $\lambda_J$ be the eigenvalue closest to $\mu$ and $\lambda_K$ the second closest, with $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu} < \abs{\lambda_K - \mu}$, and let $\ip{\vec{x}_J}{\vec{q}^{(0)}} \ne 0$. Then there are unimodular $s_k$ such that the iterates of \Cref{alg:inverse} satisfy
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_J} = O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_J - \mu}{\lambda_K - \mu}}^k\right), \qquad \abs{\lambda^{(k)} - \lambda_J} = O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_J - \mu}{\lambda_K - \mu}}^{2k}\right).
  \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  The matrix $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$ is Hermitian, with the same orthonormal eigenvectors as $A$ and eigenvalues $1/(\lambda_j - \mu)$, the largest in modulus being $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$ and the second largest $1/(\lambda_K - \mu)$. Up to normalization, the iterates of \Cref{alg:inverse} are those of \Cref{alg:power} applied to $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$, so the first estimate is \Cref{thm:power} with the ratio $\abs{(\lambda_K - \mu)^{-1}/(\lambda_J - \mu)^{-1}}$. The eigenvalue estimate $\lambda^{(k)} = r(\vec{q}^{(k)})$ is the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ itself, and \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad} applied to $A$ squares the vector error.
\end{proof}

The closer $\mu$ is to $\lambda_J$, the smaller the ratio. The rate of convergence is now controlled by our choice of $\mu$, not by the intrinsic spectrum of the matrix.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Inverse iteration with a fixed shift $\mu$.}
  \label{alg:inverse}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State factor $A - \mu I = PLU$ \Comment{once, $O(m^3)$}
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ random unit vector
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots$}
      \State solve $(A - \mu I)\vec{z} = \vec{q}$ with the factorization \Comment{$O(m^2)$}
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
      \State $\vec{w} \gets A\vec{q}$, \quad $\lambda \gets \vec{q}^\ast\vec{w}$ \Comment{Rayleigh quotient of $A$}
      \If{$\norm{\vec{w} - \lambda\vec{q}} \le \mathrm{tol} \cdot \abs{\lambda}$}
        \State \Return $\lambda, \vec{q}$
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Since $\mu$ is fixed, the factorization is computed once, outside the loop; solving with it from scratch in every step would cost $O(m^3)$ per step. For Hermitian $A$ we can use a symmetric indefinite factorization $LDL^\ast$ (Bunch--Kaufman, \Cref{sec:chol-errors}), but not Cholesky: with $\mu$ in the middle of the spectrum, $A - \mu I$ is indefinite. The eigenvalue estimate is the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ itself, which gives $\lambda_J$ directly; there is no need to recover it from $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$. Normalization is especially important here, since $\norm{\vec{z}} \approx 1/\abs{\lambda_J - \mu}$ can be huge.

\paragraph{Ill-conditioning is harmless.} The closer $\mu$ is to $\lambda_J$, the closer $A - \mu I$ is to singular and the larger the error in the solve. But this error lies almost entirely along $\vec{x}_J$, which is exactly the direction we want, and normalization removes it. An exact zero pivot, when $\mu$ is exactly an eigenvalue, almost never happens in floating point; if it does, perturbing $\mu$ by about $\epsm$ suffices.

\section{Rayleigh Quotient Iteration}

Inverse iteration with a fixed $\mu$ still converges only linearly. The idea of Rayleigh quotient iteration (RQI) is to let $\mu$ be the current best guess for the eigenvalue: the Rayleigh quotient of the current vector.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Rayleigh quotient iteration.}
  \label{alg:rqi}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ unit starting vector; $\mu \gets \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$ (or a given guess)
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots$}
      \State solve $(A - \mu I)\vec{z} = \vec{q}$
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
      \State $\mu \gets \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

This sets up a positive feedback loop: a more accurate $\mu$ gives a smaller inverse iteration ratio, which improves $\vec{q}$ more, which makes the Rayleigh quotient $\mu$ more accurate. The Rayleigh quotient turns good vectors into better eigenvalues, and inverse iteration turns good eigenvalues into good vectors.

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Rayleigh Quotient Iteration {\cite[Thm.~27.3]{TB}}]
  \label{thm:rqi}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian. If RQI converges to an eigenpair $(\lambda_J, \vec{x}_J)$, then for $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ sufficiently close to $\pm\vec{x}_J$ (in the complex case, to a unimodular multiple of $\vec{x}_J$),
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k+1)} - (\pm\vec{x}_J)} = O\left(\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - (\pm\vec{x}_J)}^3\right), \qquad \abs{\mu^{(k+1)} - \lambda_J} = O\left(\abs{\mu^{(k)} - \lambda_J}^3\right).
  \end{equation}
  For a general (non-Hermitian) matrix, the convergence is quadratic instead.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Why cubic.} Suppose $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - \vec{x}_J} = \eps$. One step of inverse iteration multiplies the vector error by $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu}/\abs{\lambda_K - \mu}$, whose denominator is roughly the spectral gap and stays bounded away from $0$. For a general matrix the Rayleigh quotient is only first-order accurate, $\abs{\mu - \lambda_J} = O(\eps)$, so the new error is $O(\eps^2)$: quadratic convergence. On the board this is written as: for $\eps$ small enough, $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - \vec{x}_J} \le \eps$ implies $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k+1)} - \vec{x}_J} \le \eps^2$. For Hermitian $A$, \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad} gives $\abs{\mu - \lambda_J} = O(\eps^2)$, so the new error is $O(\eps^3)$: cubic convergence. The number of correct digits triples at every step, $10^{-2} \to 10^{-6} \to 10^{-18}$. The board's bound $\le \eps^2$ holds here as well, since $C\eps^3 \le \eps^2$ once $\eps \le 1/C$; ``$\eps$ small enough'' absorbs the constant.

This is a \emph{local} statement: it applies only once $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ is already close enough to some eigenvector.

\paragraph{Cost and limitations.} Since $\mu$ changes at every step, the factorization cannot be reused, and each step costs $O(m^3)$ for a dense matrix. In practice $A$ is first reduced to tridiagonal (Hermitian) or Hessenberg form, after which each solve costs only $O(m)$ or $O(m^2)$; the shifted QR algorithm is essentially RQI in disguise. As it converges, $A - \mu I$ becomes ever closer to singular, which is again ill-conditioned but harmless; an exact zero pivot means that the iteration has converged, and we can stop. Which eigenvalue RQI converges to depends on the starting vector and cannot be prescribed in advance; for Hermitian matrices it can be shown to converge for almost every starting vector. A common practice is to run a few steps of inverse iteration with a fixed shift to steer the vector toward the target, and then switch to RQI to accelerate.

\paragraph{The Jordan normal form.} Diagonalizability requires $m$ linearly independent eigenvectors. A counterexample is
\begin{equation}
  J = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
whose eigenvalue $\lambda$ has algebraic multiplicity $2$, but $\nullsp(J - \lambda I) = \spn(\vec{e}_1)$, so its geometric multiplicity is $1$. A matrix with an eigenvalue whose geometric multiplicity is smaller than its algebraic multiplicity is called \emph{defective}. Every square matrix is similar to a Jordan normal form $A = XJX^{-1}$, $J = \diag(J_1, \dots, J_p)$, where each Jordan block is $J_i = \lambda_iI + N$ with $N$ the nilpotent matrix with ones on the superdiagonal; $J$ is unique up to the order of the blocks. The algebraic multiplicity of an eigenvalue is the total size of its blocks, and the geometric multiplicity is the number of its blocks; $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if all blocks are $1 \times 1$. The columns of $X$ belonging to a $k \times k$ block satisfy $(A - \lambda I)\vec{x}_j = \vec{x}_{j-1}$: $\vec{x}_1$ is an eigenvector and the rest are \emph{generalized eigenvectors}.

In theory, the Jordan form explains the transient growth of defective matrices: $J_i^n = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{n}{j}\lambda^{n-j}N^j$, so even with $\abs{\lambda} < 1$, $\norm{A^n}$ may first grow like $n^{k-1}\abs{\lambda}^n$; the factor $t$ in the solutions of ODEs with repeated roots has the same origin. Numerically, it is not used, because it is discontinuous under perturbation: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ \eps & \lambda \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ has eigenvalues $\lambda \pm \sqrt{\eps}$ and is immediately diagonalizable, and a $k \times k$ block amplifies perturbations like $\eps^{1/k}$. In floating point, whether a matrix is defective and how large its blocks are cannot be decided, and $\kappa(X) \to \infty$ as a matrix approaches a defective one. This is why we give up on diagonal form and settle for the upper triangular Schur form.

\paragraph{Why not go through the characteristic polynomial.} Even in low degrees where root formulas exist, we do not compute $\det(zI - A)$ and then its roots, because the step from a matrix to the coefficients of its polynomial is extremely ill-conditioned. For the Wilkinson polynomial $\prod_{i=1}^{20}(z - i)$, changing the coefficient of $z^{19}$ by about $10^{-10}$ sends several roots into the complex plane, moving them by $O(1)$; yet the eigenvalues of $\diag(1, \dots, 20)$ are perfectly well-conditioned under perturbations of the matrix. So all iterative methods of this lecture act directly on the matrix, using only matrix-vector products or linear solves. They exploit $p(A)\vec{x}_i = p(\lambda_i)\vec{x}_i$, rather than approximating roots of a polynomial.

\paragraph{A gap-independent eigenvalue bound.} Let $A$ be s.p.d.\ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots > 0$ and orthonormal eigenvectors, and let $t_j = \lambda_j/\lambda_1 \in (0, 1]$. The Rayleigh quotient of power iteration satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\lambda_1 - r(\vec{q}^{(k)})}{\lambda_1} = \frac{\sum_{j \ge 2}(1 - t_j)t_j^{2k}\abs{y_j}^2}{\sum_j t_j^{2k}\abs{y_j}^2} \le \sum_{j \ge 2}(1 - t_j)t_j^{2k}\frac{\abs{y_j}^2}{\abs{y_1}^2} \le \frac{1}{2k + 1} \cdot \frac{\norm{\vec{y}}^2}{\abs{y_1}^2}.
\end{equation}
The last step holds because the maximum of $(1 - t)t^{2k}$ on $[0, 1]$, attained at $t = \frac{2k}{2k+1}$, is at most $\frac{1}{2k+1}$. This bound does not depend on $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$: however small the gap, the relative eigenvalue error decreases at least like $O(1/k)$.

\paragraph{Matrix functions in general.} A matrix function does not act entrywise (in Julia, \texttt{exp(A)} is the matrix exponential, while \texttt{exp.(A)} acts entrywise). Polynomials extend to power series, such as $e^A = \sum_k A^k/k!$, and for diagonalizable $A$
\begin{equation}
  f(A) = Xf(\Lambda)X^{-1}, \qquad f(\Lambda) = \diag(f(\lambda_1), \dots, f(\lambda_m))
\end{equation}
can serve as the definition for a general $f$. For example, an s.p.d.\ matrix has the square root $\sqrt{A} = Q\diag(\sqrt{\lambda_i})Q^\ast$, which is how $P = (A^\ast A)^{1/2}$ in the polar decomposition is defined. For a Jordan block, $f(J)$ is upper triangular, with $f'(\lambda), f''(\lambda)/2!, \dots$ on the successive superdiagonals. The methods of this lecture are all choices of $f$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.3\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.28\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.32\textwidth}@{}}
  \toprule
  $f(z)$ & Amplified eigenvalue & Method \\
  \midrule
  $z$ & largest modulus & power iteration \\
  $z + \sigma$ & breaks symmetry of moduli & shifted power iteration \\
  $(z - \mu)^{-1}$ & closest to $\mu$ & inverse iteration \\
  $(z - \mu_k)^{-1}$, $\mu_k$ updated & adaptive & Rayleigh quotient iteration \\
  well-designed polynomial $p_k(z)$ of degree $k$ & small on the rest of the spectrum & Chebyshev acceleration, Krylov methods \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The last row is the natural generalization of power iteration: $A^k\vec{q}$ is just one of many polynomials of degree $k$ in $A$ applied to $\vec{q}$, and searching for the best one in $\spn(\vec{q}, A\vec{q}, \dots, A^k\vec{q})$ is what Krylov subspace methods (Lanczos, Arnoldi) do. Numerically, when $X$ is ill-conditioned, the error of $Xf(\Lambda)X^{-1}$ is amplified by $\kappa(X)$. For Hermitian $A$, $X = Q$ is unitary and the formula is safe; for general matrices one uses the Schur decomposition $f(A) = Qf(T)Q^\ast$ and a recurrence on the triangular $T$ (Schur--Parlett); $e^A$ is computed by scaling and squaring, $e^A = (e^{A/2^s})^{2^s}$, with a Pad\'e approximant on the small matrix. See Higham~\cite{HighamFM}.

\paragraph{Why iterative approximation is reliable.} Eigenvalues cannot be computed exactly in finitely many steps, yet iterative approximation is numerically on equal footing with direct methods. There are three layers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Why the vector converges.} Every vector is a combination of eigenvectors, and in $f(A)^k\vec{q} = \sum_j f(\lambda_j)^ky_j\vec{x}_j$ each direction is independently multiplied by $f(\lambda_j)^k$. Exponential growth lets the direction with the largest $\abs{f(\lambda)}$ dominate, with geometric convergence. The three algorithms differ only in the choice of $f$.
  \item \emph{Why the eigenvalue can be read off.} The Rayleigh quotient equals the eigenvalue at an eigenvector and (for Hermitian matrices) is stationary there, so a roughly correct vector already gives a quadratically accurate eigenvalue.
  \item \emph{Why an approximation is good enough.} For any unit vector $\vec{q}$ and number $\mu$, let $\vec{r} = A\vec{q} - \mu\vec{q}$ and $\delta A = -\vec{r}\vec{q}^\ast$. Then
  \begin{equation}
    (A + \delta A)\vec{q} = A\vec{q} - \vec{r} = \mu\vec{q}, \qquad \norm{\delta A}_2 = \norm{\vec{r}}\norm{\vec{q}} = \norm{\vec{r}}.
  \end{equation}
  An approximate eigenpair with residual $\norm{\vec{r}}$ is an \emph{exact} eigenpair of a matrix that differs from $A$ by $\norm{\vec{r}}$. The input $A$ already carries errors of order $\epsm$, so once $\norm{\vec{r}} \approx \epsm\norm{A}$, the result cannot be distinguished from an exact computation on a matrix indistinguishable from $A$. This is exactly backward stability (\Cref{def:backward-stable}), the same standard as for LU and QR. Combined with the well-conditioning of Hermitian eigenvalues (Weyl), a small backward error guarantees a small forward error. This is the fundamental reason for using the residual as the stopping criterion.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A preview of subspace iteration.} In the example $\abs{\lambda_1} \sim \abs{\lambda_2} \gg \abs{\lambda_3}$, what converges quickly is not a vector but the subspace $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2)$, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_2}$, regardless of how close $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are. Iterating several vectors at once and keeping them orthonormal by a QR factorization at each step quickly produces an invariant subspace; solving a small eigenvalue problem inside it then separates the eigenpairs. This is simultaneous (subspace) iteration, the precursor of the QR algorithm. When $\lambda_2 \approx -\lambda_1$, two consecutive iterates $\vec{q}^{(k)}, \vec{q}^{(k+1)}$ also span this invariant subspace, and the same approach separates $\pm\lambda_1$.
