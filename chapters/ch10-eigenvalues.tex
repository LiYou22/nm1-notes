\section{Eigen-Type Factorizations}

LU, Cholesky, and QR are based on \emph{elimination} or \emph{projection}. Their purpose is to \emph{solve linear systems}, using one-sided transformations ($A \mapsto LA$, $A \mapsto Q^\ast A$) or congruences ($A \mapsto LAL^\ast$), none of which preserve eigenvalues. They finish in about $O(m^3)$ steps and, without rounding, are exact.

Starting with this lecture we study a second family, which shows the eigenstructure of a matrix: the eigen, polar, Schur, and singular value decompositions. The eigen and Schur decompositions use \emph{similarity transformations}
\begin{equation*}
  A \mapsto X^{-1}AX,
\end{equation*}
which preserve eigenvalues. The SVD and the polar decomposition use unitary transformations $A \mapsto U^\ast AV$, which preserve singular values. The table compares them.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth}@{}}
  \toprule
  Factorization & Form & Exists for & Transformation \\
  \midrule
  Eigen & $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ & diagonalizable (nondefective) $A$ & general $X$, possibly ill-conditioned \\
  Polar & $A = QP$, $P = (A^\ast A)^{1/2} \succeq 0$ & every square $A$ & $Q$ unitary \\
  Schur & $A = QTQ^\ast$, $T$ upper triangular & every square $A$ & $Q$ unitary \\
  SVD & $A = U\Sigma V^\ast$ & every $A$ & $U, V$ unitary \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Only the eigendecomposition may fail to exist. The other three exist for every (square) matrix, and they use only unitary transformations, which do not amplify errors. A \emph{normal} matrix ($A^\ast A = AA^\ast$, for example a Hermitian one) can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix, $A = Q\Lambda Q^\ast$. We start with the eigendecomposition of Hermitian matrices.

\section{Eigenpairs of Hermitian Matrices}

\paragraph{Problem.} Given a Hermitian $A \in \K^{m \times m}$, find $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m \in \R$ and $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m \in \K^m$ with
\begin{equation}
  A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, m.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Real eigenvalues and the Rayleigh quotient.} \Cref{prop:herm-eig} showed that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are real. Here is the same proof written with inner products:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i = \frac{\ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_i}}{\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2} = \frac{\ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_i}}{\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2} = \overline{\lambda_i}.
\end{equation}
We check the three equalities. Recall that $\ip{\vec{x}}{\vec{y}} = \vec{x}^\ast\vec{y}$ and $A = A^\ast$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{First.} We put $A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i$ into the second slot. The scalar $\lambda_i$ comes out as it is:
  \begin{equation}
    \ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_i} = \vec{x}_i^\ast(\lambda_i\vec{x}_i) = \lambda_i\,\vec{x}_i^\ast\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2.
  \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Second.} Since $A = A^\ast$, we can move $A$ to the other slot:
  \begin{equation}
    \ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_i} = \vec{x}_i^\ast A\vec{x}_i = \vec{x}_i^\ast A^\ast\vec{x}_i = (A\vec{x}_i)^\ast\vec{x}_i = \ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_i}.
  \end{equation}
  \item \emph{Third.} We put $A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i$ into the first slot. Now $\lambda_i$ comes out conjugated:
  \begin{equation}
    \ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_i} = (\lambda_i\vec{x}_i)^\ast\vec{x}_i = \overline{\lambda_i}\,\vec{x}_i^\ast\vec{x}_i = \overline{\lambda_i}\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2.
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
So $\lambda_i = \overline{\lambda_i}$, that is, $\lambda_i$ is real.

The first fraction, $\vec{x}_i^\ast A\vec{x}_i/\norm{\vec{x}_i}^2$, can be computed for \emph{any} nonzero vector, not only for eigenvectors. At an eigenvector it gives the eigenvalue. This leads to the following definition, our main tool for estimating eigenvalues.

\begin{definition}[Rayleigh Quotient]
  \label{def:rayleigh}
  Let $A \in \K^{m \times m}$. The \emph{Rayleigh quotient} of a nonzero $\vec{x} \in \K^m$ is
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{x}) \defeq \frac{\vec{x}^\ast A\vec{x}}{\vec{x}^\ast\vec{x}}.
  \end{equation}
\end{definition}

If $A$ is Hermitian, the Rayleigh quotient is always real. At an eigenvector it equals the eigenvalue. So if we have a good approximate eigenvector, its Rayleigh quotient is a natural guess for the eigenvalue. We will see that this guess is surprisingly accurate.

\begin{proposition}[Orthogonal Eigenvectors]
  \label{prop:herm-orth}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian, $A\vec{x}_i = \lambda_i\vec{x}_i$ and $A\vec{x}_j = \lambda_j\vec{x}_j$ with $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j$. Then $\vec{x}_i \perp \vec{x}_j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  We have
  \begin{equation}
    \lambda_i\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = \ip{A\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = \ip{\vec{x}_i}{A\vec{x}_j} = \lambda_j\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j}.
  \end{equation}
  The first step uses that $\lambda_i$ is real (\Cref{prop:herm-eig}), so it comes out of the first slot unchanged. The second uses $A = A^\ast$. So $(\lambda_i - \lambda_j)\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = 0$, and since $\lambda_i \ne \lambda_j$, $\ip{\vec{x}_i}{\vec{x}_j} = 0$.
\end{proof}

\section{A Negative Result}
\label{sec:negative-result}

LU and its relatives can be computed exactly (up to rounding) in $O(m^3)$ arithmetic operations. Can we do the same for eigenvalues? No. The reason comes from algebra.

\begin{theorem}[Abel--Ruffini]
  \label{thm:abel}
  For every $m \ge 5$, there is a polynomial of degree $m$ with rational coefficients whose roots cannot be expressed in its coefficients by finitely many $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, and $k$-th roots.
\end{theorem}

Why is this a problem for eigenvalues? We can turn any polynomial into a matrix whose eigenvalues are its roots. So an exact eigenvalue algorithm would find the roots of any polynomial exactly, which contradicts Abel.

\begin{lemma}[Companion Matrix]
  \label{lem:companion}
  Let $p(z) = z^m + a_{m-1}z^{m-1} + \cdots + a_1z + a_0$ be monic, and let
  \begin{equation}
    A = \begin{bmatrix}
      0 & 1 & & & \\
      & 0 & 1 & & \\
      & & \ddots & \ddots & \\
      & & & 0 & 1 \\
      -a_0 & -a_1 & \cdots & -a_{m-2} & -a_{m-1}
    \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
    \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ z \\ z^2 \\ \vdots \\ z^{m-1} \end{bmatrix}.
  \end{equation}
  If $p(z) = 0$, then $A\vec{x} = z\vec{x}$, so $z$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ with eigenvector $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Clearly $\vec{x} \ne \zerovec$ since $x_1 = 1$. We check $A\vec{x} = z\vec{x}$ row by row.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{The first $m - 1$ rows.} Row $i$ of $A$ picks out the next entry of $\vec{x}$: $(A\vec{x})_i = x_{i+1} = z^i = z \cdot z^{i-1} = zx_i$.
    \item \emph{The last row.} Using $p(z) = 0$,
    \begin{equation*}
      (A\vec{x})_m = -\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_iz^i = z^m - \underbrace{p(z)}_{=0} = z \cdot z^{m-1} = zx_m. \qedhere
    \end{equation*}
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

Expanding $\det(zI - A)$ along the last row gives $\det(zI - A) = p(z)$. So the eigenvalues of $A$ are \emph{exactly} the roots of $p$. (If $p$ is not monic, we first divide by its leading coefficient.)

\begin{theorem}[Eigenvalue Algorithms Must Be Iterative]
  \label{thm:eig-iterative}
  For $m \ge 5$, no algorithm computes the eigenvalues of every $m \times m$ matrix exactly by finitely many $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$, and $k$-th roots.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Given any monic $p$ of degree $m$, form its companion matrix. This needs no arithmetic, since its entries are $0$, $1$, and $-a_i$. The algorithm would then return the roots of $p$ in finitely many operations, which contradicts \Cref{thm:abel}. So eigenvalue algorithms must be \emph{iterative}.
\end{proof}

\section{Power Iteration}

We cannot hope for exact computation. So how can we \emph{approximate} the eigenpairs of $A$? The simplest idea is to multiply a vector by $A$ again and again. To see why this works, we first look at what multiplication by $A$ does in the eigenbasis.

\paragraph{Multiplying by \texorpdfstring{$A$}{A} in the eigenbasis.} Let $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ be diagonalizable, where the columns $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$ of $X$ are eigenvectors and $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$.

\emph{Every vector can be expanded in eigenvectors.} Diagonalizable means $X$ is invertible, i.e.\ the $m$ eigenvectors are linearly independent. Since $\K^m$ has dimension $m$, they form a basis of the \emph{whole} space $\K^m$. (A single eigenspace may be small, but all eigenvectors together span everything.) Hence every $\vec{z} \in \K^m$ can be written uniquely as
\begin{equation*}
  \vec{z} = X\vec{y} = \sum_{i=1}^m y_i\vec{x}_i, \qquad \vec{y} = X^{-1}\vec{z}.
\end{equation*}
The vector $\vec{y}$ holds the coordinates of $\vec{z}$ in the eigenbasis; in practice it is obtained by solving $X\vec{y} = \vec{z}$. Note that $y_i$ is in general \emph{not} the orthogonal projection coefficient $\vec{x}_i^\ast\vec{z}$, because the eigenvectors need not be orthogonal; the two agree only when $X$ is unitary (i.e.\ $A$ is normal). Also, $\vec{z}$ lies in $\range(X) = \K^m$, but it need not lie in $\range(A)$, which is smaller when $A$ is singular.

\emph{The result.} Then
\begin{equation}
  A\vec{z} = X\Lambda\vec{y}, \qquad A^2\vec{z} = X\Lambda^2\vec{y}, \qquad A^k\vec{z} = X\Lambda^k\vec{y} = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i^k y_i\vec{x}_i.
\end{equation}
We derive this in three steps.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{One multiplication.} Substitute $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ and $\vec{z} = X\vec{y}$. The inner $X^{-1}X$ cancels:
  \begin{equation*}
    A\vec{z} = X\Lambda(X^{-1}X)\vec{y} = X\Lambda\vec{y}.
  \end{equation*}
  Read $A = X\Lambda X^{-1}$ from right to left: $X^{-1}$ converts $\vec{z}$ to eigen-coordinates $\vec{y}$, $\Lambda$ scales the $i$-th coordinate by $\lambda_i$, and $X$ converts back to standard coordinates.
  \item \emph{$k$ multiplications.} In $A^k = (X\Lambda X^{-1})(X\Lambda X^{-1})\cdots(X\Lambda X^{-1})$, every $X^{-1}X$ between neighboring factors cancels, so $A^k = X\Lambda^kX^{-1}$ and
  \begin{equation*}
    A^k\vec{z} = X\Lambda^kX^{-1}X\vec{y} = X\Lambda^k\vec{y}.
  \end{equation*}
  \item \emph{The sum.} $\Lambda$ is diagonal, so $\Lambda^k = \diag(\lambda_1^k, \dots, \lambda_m^k)$, and entry $i$ of $\Lambda^k\vec{y}$ is $\lambda_i^ky_i$. A matrix times a vector is a combination of its columns, $X\vec{v} = \sum_i v_i\vec{x}_i$. With $\vec{v} = \Lambda^k\vec{y}$ this gives $X\Lambda^k\vec{y} = \sum_i \lambda_i^ky_i\vec{x}_i$.
\end{enumerate}
In words: \emph{in the eigenbasis, multiplication by $A$ multiplies each coordinate by its own eigenvalue. The directions do not mix.}

Now assume that the largest eigenvalue strictly dominates, $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \cdots \ge \abs{\lambda_m}$. We factor out $\lambda_1^k$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:power-expand}
  A^k\vec{z} = \lambda_1^k\left(y_1\vec{x}_1 + \sum_{i=2}^m \left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^k y_i\vec{x}_i\right).
\end{equation}
Every ratio $\abs{\lambda_i/\lambda_1}$ with $i \ge 2$ is less than $1$. So $(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k \to 0$, and the \emph{direction} of $A^k\vec{z}$ approaches $\vec{x}_1$.

Informally,
\begin{equation*}
  \Lambda^k \approx \lambda_1^k\diag(1, 0, \dots, 0), \qquad A^k\vec{z} \approx \lambda_1^ky_1\vec{x}_1.
\end{equation*}
These approximations hold in the relative sense, $\Lambda^k/\lambda_1^k \to \diag(1, 0, \dots, 0)$. The vector $A^k\vec{z}$ itself may blow up or go to zero.

\paragraph{The condition \texorpdfstring{$y_1 \ne 0$}{y1 ≠ 0}.} All of this needs $y_1 \ne 0$. If the starting vector has no component along $\vec{x}_1$, multiplying by $A$ never creates one. A random starting vector has $y_1 \ne 0$ with probability $1$.

\paragraph{The algorithm.} The algorithm has four ingredients.
\begin{itemize}
  \item We compute $\vec{z}_k = A^k\vec{z}/\norm{A^k\vec{z}}$ as an approximation to $\vec{x}_1$.
  \item We use the Rayleigh quotient $\ip{\vec{z}_k}{A\vec{z}_k}$ as an approximation to $\lambda_1$.
  \item We choose $\vec{z} \sim N(0, I)$ to make sure that $y_1 \ne 0$.
  \item We normalize at every step to avoid overflow.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Power iteration.}
  \label{alg:power}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ vector with i.i.d.\ standard normal entries; $\vec{q} \gets \vec{q}/\norm{\vec{q}}$
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots, k_{\max}$}
      \State $\vec{z} \gets A\vec{q}$
      \State $\lambda \gets \vec{q}^\ast\vec{z}$ \Comment{Rayleigh quotient}
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Why normalize.} We only care about the direction of $A^k\vec{z}$, and rescaling does not change it, since $A(c\vec{v}) = c \cdot A\vec{v}$. The length, however, behaves like $\abs{\lambda_1}^k$. If $\abs{\lambda_1} > 1$, the iterates overflow; if $\abs{\lambda_1} < 1$, they underflow. Either happens long before the direction converges. So we rescale to unit length at every step.

\paragraph{Rate of convergence.} We now study how fast power iteration converges, for Hermitian $A$. We track two errors: the error in the vector and the error in the eigenvalue. The key point is that the second error is much smaller than the first. The reason is that the Rayleigh quotient of a Hermitian matrix is \emph{quadratically} accurate.

\emph{Notation.} $A$ is Hermitian, with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$ and $A\vec{x}_j = \lambda_j\vec{x}_j$. The $\lambda_j$ are real. For a unit vector $\vec{q}$, $c_j = \ip{\vec{x}_j}{\vec{q}}$ are its coordinates in this basis. The index $j$ is summed over; $J$ is fixed, the index of the eigenvalue being estimated. In \Cref{thm:power} the eigenvalues are ordered by modulus, $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \cdots$. By contrast, $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ are the largest and smallest by value.

\begin{lemma}[Quadratic Accuracy of the Rayleigh Quotient]
  \label{lem:rayleigh-quad}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$, and let $\vec{q}$ be a unit vector. Then for every $J$,
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J = \sum_{j \ne J}(\lambda_j - \lambda_J)\abs{c_j}^2, \qquad c_j = \ip{\vec{x}_j}{\vec{q}},
  \end{equation}
  and hence $\abs{r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J} \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}^2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  Expand $\vec{q}$ in the orthonormal eigenvectors:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q} = \sum_j c_j\vec{x}_j, \qquad \sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = \norm{\vec{q}}^2 = 1.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{The identity.} Since $A\vec{q} = \sum_j \lambda_j c_j\vec{x}_j$ and the $\vec{x}_j$ are orthonormal,
  \begin{equation}
    r(\vec{q}) = \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q} = \sum_j\lambda_j\abs{c_j}^2.
  \end{equation}
  So $r(\vec{q})$ is a weighted average of the eigenvalues, with weights $\abs{c_j}^2$ that sum to $1$. This gives quadratic accuracy: if $\vec{q}$ is $\varepsilon$ away from $\vec{x}_J$, the wrong components $c_j$ ($j \ne J$) are $O(\varepsilon)$ by \eqref{eq:rayleigh-tail}, but they enter squared, $\abs{c_j}^2 = O(\varepsilon^2)$.

  We subtract $\lambda_J = \lambda_J\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2$. The $j = J$ term cancels, and we get
  \begin{equation*}
    r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J = \sum_j(\lambda_j - \lambda_J)\abs{c_j}^2 = \sum_{j \ne J}(\lambda_j - \lambda_J)\abs{c_j}^2.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{The bound.} The eigenvalues are real, so they all lie in $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ and $\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda_J} \le \lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}$. Hence
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{r(\vec{q}) - \lambda_J} \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\sum_{j \ne J}\abs{c_j}^2.
  \end{equation*}
  It remains to bound the sum by $\norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}^2$. We write
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q} - \vec{x}_J = (c_J - 1)\vec{x}_J + \sum_{j \ne J}c_j\vec{x}_j.
  \end{equation*}
  By Pythagoras, and then dropping the nonnegative term $\abs{c_J - 1}^2$,
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:rayleigh-tail}
    \norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}^2 = \abs{c_J - 1}^2 + \sum_{j \ne J}\abs{c_j}^2 \ge \sum_{j \ne J}\abs{c_j}^2. \qedhere
  \end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Power Iteration]
  \label{thm:power}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian, with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$ and eigenvalues
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2} \ge \cdots \ge \abs{\lambda_m}.
  \end{equation*}
  Let the unit starting vector satisfy $c_1 = \ip{\vec{x}_1}{\vec{q}^{(0)}} \ne 0$. Then there are unimodular scalars $s_k$ ($s_k = \pm 1$ in the real case) such that the iterates of \Cref{alg:power} satisfy
  \begin{align}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1} &= O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^k\right), \\
    \abs{r(\vec{q}^{(k)}) - \lambda_1} &= O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}^{2k}\right).
  \end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Write $\rho = \abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1} < 1$. Normalizing at every step only rescales, so $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ is $A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}$ scaled to unit length.

  \emph{Expand.} We split $A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}$ into its main direction $\vec{x}_1$ and a deviation $\vec{e}_k$. By \eqref{eq:power-expand} with $y_j = c_j$,
  \begin{equation*}
    A^k\vec{q}^{(0)} = \lambda_1^kc_1(\vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k), \qquad \vec{e}_k = \sum_{j=2}^m\left(\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}\right)^k\frac{c_j}{c_1}\vec{x}_j.
  \end{equation*}
  The deviation involves only $\vec{x}_2, \dots, \vec{x}_m$, so $\vec{e}_k \perp \vec{x}_1$. Each $\abs{\lambda_j/\lambda_1} \le \rho$ and $\sum_j\abs{c_j}^2 = 1$, so by Pythagoras
  \begin{equation*}
    \norm{\vec{e}_k}^2 = \sum_{j=2}^m\abs{\frac{\lambda_j}{\lambda_1}}^{2k}\frac{\abs{c_j}^2}{\abs{c_1}^2} \le \frac{\rho^{2k}}{\abs{c_1}^2}\sum_{j=2}^m\abs{c_j}^2 \le \frac{\rho^{2k}}{\abs{c_1}^2}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \norm{\vec{e}_k} \le \frac{\rho^k}{\abs{c_1}}.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{Normalize.} Let $s_k = \lambda_1^kc_1/\abs{\lambda_1^kc_1}$ and $n_k = \norm{\vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k}$. Then $\norm{A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}} = \abs{\lambda_1^kc_1}\,n_k$, and dividing leaves the phase $s_k$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q}^{(k)} = \frac{A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}}{\norm{A^k\vec{q}^{(0)}}} = \frac{\lambda_1^kc_1}{\abs{\lambda_1^kc_1}}\cdot\frac{\vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k}{n_k} = \frac{s_k(\vec{x}_1 + \vec{e}_k)}{n_k}.
  \end{equation*}
  We need $s_k$ because $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ converges in direction only: if $\lambda_1 < 0$ the iterates flip sign every step, and a complex $c_1$ adds a phase. So we compare $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ with $s_k\vec{x}_1$, not with $\vec{x}_1$.

  The error is
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1 = s_k\left[\left(\frac{1}{n_k} - 1\right)\vec{x}_1 + \frac{\vec{e}_k}{n_k}\right].
  \end{equation*}
  Since $\vec{e}_k \perp \vec{x}_1$, Pythagoras gives $n_k = \sqrt{1 + \norm{\vec{e}_k}^2}$. As $\sqrt{1 + t} \le 1 + t/2$, we get $1 \le n_k \le 1 + \norm{\vec{e}_k}^2/2$. This controls both terms:
  \begin{equation*}
    1 - \frac{1}{n_k} = \frac{n_k - 1}{n_k} \le n_k - 1 \le \frac{\norm{\vec{e}_k}^2}{2}, \qquad \frac{\norm{\vec{e}_k}}{n_k} \le \norm{\vec{e}_k}.
  \end{equation*}
  By the triangle inequality,
  \begin{equation*}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1} \le \left(1 - \frac{1}{n_k}\right) + \norm{\vec{e}_k} \le \frac{\norm{\vec{e}_k}^2}{2} + \norm{\vec{e}_k} = O(\rho^k).
  \end{equation*}
  The dominant term is $\norm{\vec{e}_k}$.

  \emph{Eigenvalue.} $s_k\vec{x}_1$ is also a unit eigenvector for $\lambda_1$. So \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad}, with $\vec{x}_1$ replaced by $s_k\vec{x}_1$, squares the vector error:
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{r(\vec{q}^{(k)}) - \lambda_1} \le (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min})\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_1}^2 = O(\rho^{2k}). \qedhere
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

In words, if $\abs{\lambda_1} > \abs{\lambda_2}$ and $c_1 \ne 0$, power iteration converges:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the direction of $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ converges to $\vec{x}_1$ (up to sign), and the error shrinks by about $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$ per step;
  \item the eigenvalue estimate $r(\vec{q}^{(k)})$ converges to $\lambda_1$ twice as fast, since its error is the square of the vector error.
\end{itemize}
For example, if $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1} = 0.5$, then after $10$ steps the vector error is about $0.5^{10} \approx 10^{-3}$ and the eigenvalue error is about $0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}$. A random start gives $c_1 \ne 0$ with probability $1$.

The square needs $A$ Hermitian (\Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad}). For a general diagonalizable matrix, the eigenvalue error is only $O(\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k)$.

What happens when $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$ is close to $1$? There are two very different cases: $\lambda_2 \approx \lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2 \approx -\lambda_1$. The vector's convergence is governed by the \emph{ratio of the moduli}, $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$, while the Rayleigh quotient's error is governed by the \emph{differences of the values}, $\lambda_j - \lambda_1$ (\Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad}).

\paragraph{When \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_2 \approx \lambda_1$}{λ2 ≈ λ1}: the eigenvalue still converges.} In short, in this case \emph{the eigenvalue converges well enough, but the eigenvector converges slowly.}

Suppose $\abs{\lambda_1} \sim \abs{\lambda_2} \gg \abs{\lambda_3}$. In the space spanned by $\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2, \vec{x}_3$, the iterates move in two phases.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item They first fall quickly into the plane $\spn(\vec{x}_1, \vec{x}_2)$, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_3/\lambda_1}^k$. The components along $\vec{x}_3, \dots$ die out within a few steps.
  \item Then they turn slowly toward $\vec{x}_1$ within the plane, at the rate $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}^k$. The $\vec{x}_2$ component shrinks only a little per step.
\end{enumerate}
After the first phase, $\vec{q} \approx c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\vec{x}_2$. The Rayleigh quotient
\begin{equation*}
  \lambda_1\abs{c_1}^2 + \lambda_2\abs{c_2}^2
\end{equation*}
is then a weighted average of $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$. The two numbers are close, so the average is close to $\lambda_1$: its error is at most $\abs{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2}$, which is already small. The eigenvalue is accurate even though the vector has not yet converged.

\paragraph{When \texorpdfstring{$\lambda_2 \approx -\lambda_1$}{λ2 ≈ −λ1}: the real failure.} Now only the plane matters. Let $\vec{q} = c_1\vec{x}_1 + c_2\vec{x}_2$. One multiplication by $A$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  A\vec{q} = \lambda_1c_1\vec{x}_1 + \lambda_2c_2\vec{x}_2 \approx \lambda_1(c_1\vec{x}_1 - c_2\vec{x}_2).
\end{equation*}
Relative to the $\vec{x}_1$ coefficient, the $\vec{x}_2$ coefficient flips sign at every step. Geometrically this is a \emph{reflection across the $\vec{x}_1$ axis}. The iterates jump back and forth between two directions that are symmetric about $\vec{x}_1$.

If $\lambda_2 = -\lambda_1$ exactly, $\abs{c_2/c_1}$ never changes, and there is no convergence at all. The Rayleigh quotient
\begin{equation}
  \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q} \approx \lambda_1\left(\abs{c_1}^2 - \abs{c_2}^2\right)
\end{equation}
is fixed by the starting vector. It is a weighted average of $\lambda_1$ and $-\lambda_1$, which are far apart, so it may lie anywhere in $[-\abs{\lambda_1}, \abs{\lambda_1}]$.

So the truly bad case is eigenvalues with \emph{similar modulus but far-apart values}; close values do no harm. The cure is to change the spectrum so that the moduli separate. For example, if $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$, the shift $\mu = 0.5$ gives $A - \mu I$ with eigenvalues $0.5$ and $-1.5$, whose moduli now differ. This is the topic of the next section.

\section{Matrix Functions: Changing the Spectrum}

Power iteration has two weaknesses.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It always finds the eigenvalue of largest modulus, which may not be the one we want.
  \item It is slow when another eigenvalue has nearly the same modulus.
\end{itemize}
We keep the algorithm and change the matrix instead. So we ask: \emph{how can we change the eigenvalues of a matrix, while keeping its eigenvectors, so that the one we want stands out?}

\paragraph{Polynomials keep the eigenvectors.} Let $A\vec{x} = \lambda\vec{x}$. Then
\begin{equation*}
  A^2\vec{x} = A(\lambda\vec{x}) = \lambda^2\vec{x}, \qquad \text{and in general} \qquad A^k\vec{x} = \lambda^k\vec{x}.
\end{equation*}
A polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + a_1z + \dots + a_dz^d$ is a linear combination of powers, so
\begin{equation*}
  p(A)\vec{x} = a_0\vec{x} + a_1\lambda\vec{x} + \dots + a_d\lambda^d\vec{x} = p(\lambda)\vec{x}.
\end{equation*}
So $\vec{x}$ is still an eigenvector of $p(A)$, and its eigenvalue is now $p(\lambda)$. Some special cases:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
  \toprule
  Transformation & Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors \\
  \midrule
  $A \to A^k$ & $\lambda \to \lambda^k$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to \alpha A$ & $\lambda \to \alpha\lambda$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to A + \beta I$ & $\lambda \to \lambda + \beta$ & unchanged \\
  $A \to p(A)$ & $\lambda \to p(\lambda)$ & unchanged \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $A^k$ makes large eigenvalues larger relative to small ones. This is exactly what power iteration does.
  \item $\alpha A$ scales all eigenvalues by the same factor, so the ratios $\abs{\lambda_j/\lambda_1}$ do not change. It acts like normalization and does not help.
  \item $A + \beta I$ shifts all eigenvalues by the same amount. This \emph{does} change the ratios, and it is the main tool below.
  \item $p(A)$ is the general case; see~\cite{HighamFM} for more on matrix functions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Power iteration on \texorpdfstring{$p(A)$}{p(A)}.} We run power iteration with $p(A)$ in place of $A$. By \Cref{thm:power}, it converges to the eigenvector $\vec{x}_i$ for which $\abs{p(\lambda_i)}$ is largest, at the rate
\begin{equation*}
  \max_{j \ne i}\frac{\abs{p(\lambda_j)}}{\abs{p(\lambda_i)}}.
\end{equation*}
Since $\vec{x}_i$ is also an eigenvector of $A$, the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ (not of $p(A)$) gives the eigenvalue we want:
\begin{equation*}
  r(\vec{q}^{(k)}) = \vec{q}^{(k)\ast}A\vec{q}^{(k)} \to \lambda_i.
\end{equation*}
So $p$ acts as a \emph{filter}: we want $\abs{p}$ large at the wanted eigenvalue and small at all the others. This is called \emph{polynomial filtering}. Plain power iteration is the case $p(z) = z$; after $k$ steps it has applied the filter $z^k$.

\paragraph{Shifting.} Suppose an isolated eigenvalue sits on the positive axis, and a cluster sits on the negative axis with similar modulus, say
\begin{equation*}
  \lambda_1 = 1, \qquad \lambda_2 = -0.9, \qquad \lambda_3 = -0.8, \quad \dots
\end{equation*}
Then $\abs{\lambda_2/\lambda_1} = 0.9$, and power iteration is slow. The problem is not that $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are close; they are $1.9$ apart. The problem is that their moduli are close.

Now iterate with $A + I$ instead. The eigenvalues become
\begin{equation*}
  2, \qquad 0.1, \qquad 0.2, \quad \dots,
\end{equation*}
and the ratio drops to $0.2/2 = 0.1$. The shift moves the cluster toward the origin and the isolated eigenvalue farther out.

\paragraph{The limit of shifting.} For real eigenvalues, $\abs{\lambda + \beta}$ is largest at one of the two ends of the spectrum, whatever $\beta$ is. So a shift can only make the smallest or the largest eigenvalue stand out. To reach an eigenvalue in the middle, close to some $\mu$, we need a function that is large near $\mu$ and small elsewhere, such as $f(z) = 1/(z - \mu)$. This leads to inverse iteration.

\section{Inverse Iteration}

A shift can only make an eigenvalue at an end of the spectrum stand out. Now suppose we want an eigenvalue in the \emph{middle}: the one closest to a given number $\mu \in \R$. Following the previous section, we look for a function $f$ that is large near $\mu$ and small far from $\mu$. The simplest choice is
\begin{equation}
  f(z) = \frac{1}{z - \mu}.
\end{equation}
The matching matrix is $f(A) = (A - \mu I)^{-1}$. It has the same eigenvectors as $A$:
\begin{equation*}
  (A - \mu I)\vec{x}_j = (\lambda_j - \mu)\vec{x}_j
  \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
  (A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{x}_j = \frac{1}{\lambda_j - \mu}\vec{x}_j.
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{What $f$ does to the spectrum.} Let $\lambda_J$ be the eigenvalue closest to $\mu$, and $\lambda_K$ the second closest.
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\lambda_J$ is mapped to $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$. Its denominator is the smallest, so it has the largest modulus.
  \item $\lambda_K$ is mapped to the second largest modulus, $1/\abs{\lambda_K - \mu}$.
  \item The far eigenvalues are squeezed toward $0$. Those with $\lambda_j < \mu$ go to the negative axis, and those with $\lambda_j > \mu$ go to the positive axis.
\end{itemize}

\small The map $\lambda \mapsto 1/(\lambda - \mu)$ sends the eigenvalue closest to $\mu$ far away and squeezes all the others toward $0$.
\end{center}

\paragraph{The algorithm.} So power iteration on $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$ finds $\vec{x}_J$. This is \emph{inverse iteration}. We never form the inverse; we compute $\vec{z} = (A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q}$ by solving $(A - \mu I)\vec{z} = \vec{q}$. Since $\mu$ is fixed, we factor $A - \mu I$ once, and each step costs $O(m^2)$ (\Cref{sec:impl-inverse}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Inverse iteration with a fixed shift $\mu$.}
  \label{alg:inverse}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ random unit vector
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots, k_{\max}$}
      \State solve $(A - \mu I)\vec{z} = \vec{q}$
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
      \State $\lambda \gets \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$ \Comment{Rayleigh quotient of $A$}
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Convergence of Inverse Iteration]
  \label{thm:inverse-iter}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian. Let $\lambda_J$ be the eigenvalue closest to $\mu$ and $\lambda_K$ the second closest, with $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu} < \abs{\lambda_K - \mu}$, and let $\ip{\vec{x}_J}{\vec{q}^{(0)}} \ne 0$. Then there are unimodular $s_k$ such that the iterates of \Cref{alg:inverse} satisfy
  \begin{align}
    \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_J} &= O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_J - \mu}{\lambda_K - \mu}}^k\right), \\
    \abs{\lambda^{(k)} - \lambda_J} &= O\left(\abs{\frac{\lambda_J - \mu}{\lambda_K - \mu}}^{2k}\right).
  \end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
  Up to normalization, \Cref{alg:inverse} is \Cref{alg:power} applied to $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$. This matrix is Hermitian, with the same orthonormal eigenvectors as $A$. Its largest eigenvalue in modulus is $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$, and its second largest is $1/(\lambda_K - \mu)$. So \Cref{thm:power} applies with the ratio
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{\frac{1/(\lambda_K - \mu)}{1/(\lambda_J - \mu)}} = \abs{\frac{\lambda_J - \mu}{\lambda_K - \mu}}.
  \end{equation*}
  For the eigenvalue, $\lambda^{(k)}$ is the Rayleigh quotient of $A$ itself, so \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad} squares the vector error.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The role of $\mu$.} The closer $\mu$ is to $\lambda_J$, the smaller the ratio: \emph{the rate is controlled by our choice of $\mu$}. For example, $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu} = 0.01$ and $\abs{\lambda_K - \mu} = 1$ give a factor $100$ per step. Where does a good $\mu$ come from? The next section takes $\mu$ to be the current eigenvalue estimate.

\section{Rayleigh Quotient Iteration}

In some problems we know a good $\mu$ in advance. But what if we don't? The idea of Rayleigh quotient iteration (RQI) is to \emph{choose $\mu$ to be our present guess of the eigenvalue}, that is, the Rayleigh quotient of the current vector.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Rayleigh quotient iteration.}
  \label{alg:rqi}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \State $\vec{q} \gets$ random unit vector; $\mu \gets \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$
    \For{$k = 1, 2, \dots, k_{\max}$}
      \State solve $(A - \mu I)\vec{z} = \vec{q}$
      \State $\vec{q} \gets \vec{z}/\norm{\vec{z}}$
      \State $\mu \gets \vec{q}^\ast A\vec{q}$
    \EndFor
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The two updates help each other.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A better $\vec{q}$ gives a better Rayleigh quotient $\mu$.
  \item A better $\mu$ gives a smaller inverse iteration ratio $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu}/\abs{\lambda_K - \mu}$, so the next $\vec{q}$ improves more.
\end{itemize}
To see how fast this goes, we first ask how good the Rayleigh quotient is.

\paragraph{The Rayleigh Quotient Near an Eigenvector.}

Let $A \in \R^{m \times m}$ be symmetric. We define the Rayleigh quotient as
\begin{equation*}
  r(\vec{x}) = \frac{\vec{x}^\T A\vec{x}}{\vec{x}^\T\vec{x}}, \qquad \vec{x} \ne \zerovec.
\end{equation*}
In inverse iteration and RQI, the eigenvalue estimate is $\mu_k = r(\vec{q}^{(k)})$.

For an eigenpair $(\lambda_J, \vec{x}_J)$, we have $r(\vec{x}_J) = \lambda_J$. So $\vec{y} \approx \vec{x}_J$ implies $r(\vec{y}) \approx \lambda_J$. How close? We compute the Taylor expansion around $\vec{x}_J$:
\begin{equation}
  r(\vec{y}) = r(\vec{x}_J) + \nabla r(\vec{x}_J)^\T(\vec{y} - \vec{x}_J) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\vec{y} - \vec{x}_J)^\T H(\vec{x}_J)(\vec{y} - \vec{x}_J) + O(\norm{\vec{y} - \vec{x}_J}^3),
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the Hessian of $r$. By the quotient rule, the gradient is
\begin{equation}
  \nabla r(\vec{x}) = \frac{2A\vec{x}\,(\vec{x}^\T\vec{x}) - 2\vec{x}\,(\vec{x}^\T A\vec{x})}{(\vec{x}^\T\vec{x})^2} = \frac{2}{\vec{x}^\T\vec{x}}\bigl(A\vec{x} - r(\vec{x})\vec{x}\bigr).
\end{equation}
At an eigenvector, $A\vec{x}_J = \lambda_J\vec{x}_J = r(\vec{x}_J)\vec{x}_J$, so $\nabla r(\vec{x}_J) = \zerovec$. The linear term drops out, and
\begin{equation}
  r(\vec{y}) = \lambda_J + O(\norm{\vec{y} - \vec{x}_J}^2).
\end{equation}
In fact, $\nabla r(\vec{x}) = \zerovec$ exactly when $\vec{x}$ is an eigenvector: \emph{the eigenvectors are the critical points of $r$.} \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad} gives the same result for complex Hermitian $A$, with an explicit constant.

The derivative is zero at the eigenvectors. So an angle error $\eps$ moves $r$ by only $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\sin^2\eps \approx (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\eps^2$.

This is the good news: \emph{getting $\vec{q}$ close to $\vec{x}_J$ gets $\mu$ even closer to $\lambda_J$.} A vector error $\eps$ gives an eigenvalue error $O(\eps^2)$. RQI is similar to Newton's method (see the supplement).

\paragraph{Cubic Convergence.}

We now bound one step of RQI,
\begin{equation*}
  \vec{q}^{(k+1)} = \frac{(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q}^{(k)}}{\norm{(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q}^{(k)}}}, \qquad \mu = r(\vec{q}^{(k)}).
\end{equation*}
Assume $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - \vec{x}_J} \le \eps$ with $\eps$ small. The argument has two parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item One step of inverse iteration gives $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k+1)} - \vec{x}_J} = O(\eps\abs{\lambda_J - \mu})$. The normalization does not change this order, by the estimate
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{a + \eps} - \frac{1}{a} \approx -\frac{\eps}{a^2}:
  \end{equation*}
  dividing by a length that changes by a small amount changes the result only by the same order.
  \item The Rayleigh quotient gives $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu} = O(\eps^2)$.
\end{itemize}
Together, $\norm{\vec{q}^{(k+1)} - \vec{x}_J} = O(\eps^3)$: \emph{cubic convergence}. The number of correct digits roughly triples at every step, for example $10^{-2} \to 10^{-6} \to 10^{-18}$. This is a \emph{local} result: it applies only once $\vec{q}^{(k)}$ is close enough to some eigenvector.

The proposition below makes one step precise. Since $s\vec{x}_J$ is again a unit eigenvector for $\lambda_J$, it applies at every step. With $\eps_k = \norm{\vec{q}^{(k)} - s_k\vec{x}_J}$ and $C = 4s_A/g$, it gives
\begin{equation*}
  \eps_{k+1} \le C\eps_k^3, \qquad \abs{\mu^{(k)} - \lambda_J} \le s_A\eps_k^2.
\end{equation*}

\begin{proposition}[One Step of RQI]
  \label{prop:rqi-step}
  Let $A$ be Hermitian with orthonormal eigenvectors $\vec{x}_1, \dots, \vec{x}_m$, and let $\lambda_J$ be a simple eigenvalue. Define the gap and the spread
  \begin{equation*}
    g \defeq \min_{j \ne J}\abs{\lambda_j - \lambda_J} > 0, \qquad s_A \defeq \lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}.
  \end{equation*}
  Let $\vec{q}$ be a unit vector with
  \begin{equation*}
    \norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J} \le \eps, \qquad \eps \le \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad s_A\eps^2 \le \tfrac{g}{2}.
  \end{equation*}
  Take one RQI step from $\vec{q}$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \mu = r(\vec{q}) \ne \lambda_J, \qquad \vec{q}^+ = \frac{(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q}}{\norm{(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q}}}.
  \end{equation*}
  Then there is a unimodular $s$ with
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{q}^+ - s\vec{x}_J} \le \frac{4s_A}{g}\eps^3.
  \end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  \emph{Expand.} Write
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q} = \alpha\vec{x}_J + \beta\vec{v}, \qquad \alpha = \ip{\vec{x}_J}{\vec{q}}, \quad \norm{\vec{v}} = 1, \quad \vec{v} \perp \vec{x}_J.
  \end{equation*}
  By \eqref{eq:rayleigh-tail} and since $\eps \le \frac{1}{2}$,
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{\beta} \le \norm{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J} \le \eps, \qquad
    \abs{\alpha - 1} = \abs{\ip{\vec{x}_J}{\vec{q} - \vec{x}_J}} \le \eps
    \;\Longrightarrow\; \abs{\alpha} \ge \tfrac{1}{2}.
  \end{equation*}

  \emph{Apply $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$.} Since $\vec{x}_J$ is an eigenvector of $(A - \mu I)^{-1}$ with eigenvalue $1/(\lambda_J - \mu)$,
  \begin{equation*}
    (A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{q} = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda_J - \mu}\vec{x}_J + \beta(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{v} = \frac{\alpha}{\lambda_J - \mu}(\vec{x}_J + \vec{d}),
  \end{equation*}
  where
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{d} = \frac{\beta(\lambda_J - \mu)}{\alpha}(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{v} \perp \vec{x}_J.
  \end{equation*}
  To bound $\vec{d}$, we bound each factor. By \Cref{lem:rayleigh-quad}, $\abs{\lambda_J - \mu} \le s_A\eps^2 \le g/2$. So for every $j \ne J$,
  \begin{equation*}
    \abs{\lambda_j - \mu} \ge \abs{\lambda_j - \lambda_J} - \abs{\lambda_J - \mu} \ge g - \tfrac{g}{2} = \tfrac{g}{2}.
  \end{equation*}
  Since $\vec{v} \perp \vec{x}_J$, only these eigenvalues act on $\vec{v}$, and $\norm{(A - \mu I)^{-1}\vec{v}} \le 2/g$. Hence
  \begin{equation}
    \norm{\vec{d}} \le \frac{\eps}{1/2} \cdot s_A\eps^2 \cdot \frac{2}{g} = \frac{4s_A}{g}\eps^3.
  \end{equation}

  \emph{Normalize.} Let $s$ be the phase of $\alpha/(\lambda_J - \mu)$. Then
  \begin{equation*}
    \vec{q}^+ = s\vec{u}, \qquad \vec{u} = \frac{\vec{x}_J + \vec{d}}{n}, \qquad n = \norm{\vec{x}_J + \vec{d}} = \sqrt{1 + \norm{\vec{d}}^2},
  \end{equation*}
  where the last equality uses $\vec{d} \perp \vec{x}_J$. Hence
  \begin{equation*}
    \norm{\vec{u} - \vec{x}_J}^2 = \Bigl(1 - \frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^2 + \frac{\norm{\vec{d}}^2}{n^2} = 2 - \frac{2}{n} \le \norm{\vec{d}}^2,
  \end{equation*}
  where the last step uses $1/\sqrt{1 + t} \ge 1 - t/2$. So $\norm{\vec{q}^+ - s\vec{x}_J} = \norm{\vec{u} - \vec{x}_J} \le \norm{\vec{d}}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Implementing Inverse Iteration}
\label{sec:impl-inverse}

\emph{Factor once.} Since $\mu$ is fixed, we compute the factorization once, outside the loop. If we solved from scratch in every step, each step would cost $O(m^3)$ instead of $O(m^2)$. (Rayleigh quotient iteration cannot do this, since its shift changes at every step.)

\emph{Ill-conditioning is harmless.} The closer $\mu$ is to $\lambda_J$, the closer $A - \mu I$ is to singular, and the larger the error in the solve. Is this a problem?

No. This error lies almost entirely along $\vec{x}_J$, which is exactly the direction we want, and normalization removes it.

An exact zero pivot (when $\mu$ is exactly an eigenvalue) almost never happens in floating point. If it does, it is enough to perturb $\mu$ by about $\epsm$.

\subsection{RQI and Newton's Method}

RQI is similar to Newton's method, and we can make this precise. An eigenpair is a root of
\begin{equation}
  F(\vec{x}, \lambda) = \begin{bmatrix} (A - \lambda I)\vec{x} \\ \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - \vec{x}^\T\vec{x}) \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
  F'(\vec{x}, \lambda) = \begin{bmatrix} A - \lambda I & -\vec{x} \\ -\vec{x}^\T & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The first block row of the Newton step $F'(\vec{x}, \lambda)(\Delta\vec{x}, \Delta\lambda) = -F(\vec{x}, \lambda)$ reads
\begin{equation*}
  (A - \lambda I)\Delta\vec{x} - \Delta\lambda\,\vec{x} = -(A - \lambda I)\vec{x},
\end{equation*}
i.e.,
\begin{equation}
  \vec{x} + \Delta\vec{x} = \Delta\lambda\,(A - \lambda I)^{-1}\vec{x}.
\end{equation}
So the Newton update of the vector is parallel to one step of inverse iteration with shift $\lambda$. RQI takes this direction and normalizes it. Then it replaces Newton's $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ by the Rayleigh quotient of the new vector.

Newton's method converges quadratically, because the error of the linearization is quadratic. For symmetric $A$, the stationarity of $r$ adds one more order.


\subsection{Why Not Go Through the Characteristic Polynomial}

Even in low degrees, where root formulas exist, we do not compute $\det(zI - A)$ and then its roots. The reason: going from a matrix to the coefficients of its polynomial is extremely ill-conditioned. Take the Wilkinson polynomial $\prod_{i=1}^{20}(z - i)$. If we change the coefficient of $z^{19}$ by $2^{-23} \approx 10^{-7}$, several roots move into the complex plane, by $O(1)$. Yet the eigenvalues of $\diag(1, \dots, 20)$ are perfectly well-conditioned under perturbations of the matrix. So all iterative methods of this lecture work directly on the matrix, using only matrix-vector products or linear solves. They use $p(A)\vec{x}_i = p(\lambda_i)\vec{x}_i$, and do not approximate roots of a polynomial.
